Deferring Cannot Help When the Tail Is the Work:
A Negative Result and Characterization for Prefill Admission Control in Long-Document Multiturn LLM Serving

floyd · sglang KV-cache research · v0.31 · v1 · negative + characterization (stock n=5, CCA-lb n=5)
Abstract. Under a throughput–latency rate sweep with a p99-TTFT SLO, the headline goodput@SLO of a two-tier (GPU + host DRAM) LLM serving stack on a long-document multiturn workload is a metastable coin-flip: identical stock configurations, run back-to-back on the same GPU node, flip between goodput 0 and 3 req/s. The instinctive remedy for such overload is admission control — defer expensive requests to protect the system. We built exactly that: CCA, a cache-adjusted prefill admission controller that meters admission in residency-adjusted work and gates expensive prefills below a KV-pool watermark. It does not work, and we explain why. A trace study shows prefill cost is extraordinarily concentrated: a ~1500× per-turn spread, with the top 10% of turns carrying 78% of all prefill compute and 75% of that being cold (first-ever document prefill, irreducible by any cache). Crucially, the SLO tail is those few large cold prefills — so deferring them is self-defeating. We measure this: CCA that defers cold prefills craters λ=3 p99 to 56 s (≈7× stock); a cost-blind variant even induces a 0→518-retraction cascade that crashes the server; and the one variant that never touches cold prefills (pacing only L2→L1 load-backs) does not crash — it tightens the λ=3 p99 distribution ~5.7× (std 1988→348 ms, n=5) at the same mean (stock 8079 vs CCA-lb 8181) — a real behavioral effect — but it does not improve goodput: removing the unlucky-high fails also removes the lucky-low passes, so the pass-rate is if anything lower (2/5 vs 3/5), and the spread reduction is not significant at feasible n (Levene p=0.15). We conclude that, at the λ=3 coin-flip, goodput@SLO is cold-prefill-compute-bound: prefill admission control cannot reliably clear it (and cost-blind deferral is dangerous), because it is the wrong tool: the λ=3 p99 tail is head-of-line blocking — small turns stuck behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents (the p99 TTFT of 6–11 s far exceeds any single request's prefill, since <0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s) — which deferring those cold prefills only worsens. The relief is reordering (shortest-prefill-first), not admission; stock also never retracts, so capping the KV pool addresses a non-bottleneck. Along the way we report a sharp systems lesson: a per-rank wall-clock decision in the TP scheduler desynchronizes the ranks' prefill batches and deadlocks the NCCL collectives — admission decisions in tensor-parallel serving must be deterministic.

1 Introduction

sglang's HiCache tiers the KV cache across L1 (GPU HBM) and L2 (768 GB host DRAM). The evaluation is a fixed Poisson rate sweep (λ∈{3,5,7,10}, 1553 multiturn conversations, warmup, no flush) of Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8 (TP=8), and the headline is goodput@SLO: the max req/s with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s. Prior work established that this metric is variance-dominated. This paper asks the natural follow-up: can admission control stabilize/raise it? — and answers, rigorously, no, with a mechanistic explanation that generalizes.

Contributions:

  • A characterization (§2): prefill cost is bimodal with a ~1500× spread (75% cold); KV-pool usage is bimodal (metastable signature); goodput@SLO is a same-node coin-flip {0, 3.02}; and — non-obviously — the stock system exhibits zero retraction, so its collapse is prefill-compute queueing, not a KV-pool/retraction cascade.
  • A negative result (§3–4): prefill admission control that defers expensive prefills cannot improve goodput@SLO and can catastrophically harm it, because the SLO tail is the expensive cold prefills. We measure the full mechanism family: deferring cold prefills → 56 s p99; cost-blind deferral → retraction-cascade crash; load-back-only pacing → tightens the p99 distribution ~5.7× (not significant) but at the same mean and no higher pass-rate. No admission-control variant improves goodput@SLO at λ=3.
  • A systems lesson (§5): a per-rank non-deterministic (wall-clock) admission decision desyncs TP ranks and hangs NCCL; TP scheduling decisions must be deterministic or explicitly all-gathered.

2 Characterization: what actually bounds goodput@SLO

2.1 Prefill cost is bimodal, concentrated, and mostly cold

The workload is 1553 conversations (LEval/LooGLE long-document QA + ShareGPT chat), 7163 turns; turn-0 embeds an entire document (p90 ≈ 29k tokens) reused across 3–10 turns. Replaying the trace through an idealized LRU radix cache at the real two-tier capacity (~10.7M tokens), per-turn cache-adjusted prefill work (tokens that must be computed) has p50=26, p90=17.4k, p99=40.6k, max=190.9k — a ~1500× spread. The top 5% of turns carry 50%, the top 10% carry 78%, of all prefill compute (Fig. 1). Of all prefill work, 75% is cold (first-ever document prefill — no cache removes it) and only ~24% is avoidable evicted-recompute; with eviction already near-optimal (LRU≈Belady) and L2 transfer cheap (~1.6 ms), residency mechanisms have little headroom. The lever is scheduling the few giant cold prefills, not caching.

2.2 The collapse is prefill-compute queueing, not retraction

Stock p50 TTFT is a healthy ~1 s across the sweep, but p99 fails the SLO and swings wildly. On the same node (n=3 stock runs), λ=3 p99 = {11787 FAIL, 6327 PASS, 8124 FAIL} — goodput {0, 3.02, 0} (1/3 pass, median 0), a coin-flip straddling the SLO (λ=5 p99 {17.4, 23.1, 23.6}s all fail). The device KV-pool usage is bimodal (Fig. 2): 21.7% of samples ≥0.90 (incl. 18 at 1.00) and 18.4% ≤0.05, only 26% mid-range — the classic metastable signature. Yet, critically, stock retracts zero requests: when the pool briefly saturates, prefill admission simply stalls and decode drains it; there is no retraction cascade. So the collapse is queueing behind the large cold prefills (whose intrinsic compute is ~6 s for the largest docs at ~30k tok/s), amplified by transient admission stalls — not a KV-pool/retraction failure. This distinction is what dooms admission control (§4).

2.3 Two compute bounds that box in the metric

Two assumption-light, trace-driven bounds make "cold-prefill-compute-bound" quantitative. (1) Goodput ceiling (work-conservation). No scheduler can sustain a request rate whose prefill work exceeds the GPU's prefill throughput: goodput@SLO ≤ peak req/s = C / mean-work. At the LRU capacity that reproduces the measured hit rate (0.677), the mean cache-adjusted work is ≈ 4.3k tok, so the measured peak of ~4.2 req/s implies an effective prefill capacity C ≈ 4.2 × 4.3k ≈ 18k tok/s. This agrees, independently, with the throughput read straight off the saturated run — input throughput × (1−hit) ≈  59.9k × 0.33 ≈ 19k tok/s of new-token prefill (decode shares the GPU, pulling C well below the ~39k tok/s instantaneous prefill-step rate). Any lossless mechanism — caching, admission, or reordering — is capped here; the best reported scheduler (shortest-prefill-first) reaches ~4.1 req/s, i.e. it is already at the ceiling. (2) p99-TTFT floor. A request's TTFT cannot be smaller than its own prefill time. The p99 cache-adjusted work is ~41k tok and the max is 190.9k tok; at the effective single-doc prefill rate (~18–39k tok/s under chunking) the largest cold documents cost ~5–11 s of prefill by themselves — straddling the 8 s SLO. So the metric is fragile by construction: whether a run passes depends on whether its heaviest cold documents land just under or just over the floor — exactly the coin-flip of §2.2, and exactly what no admission/residency mechanism can move.

2.4 Two regimes, one wall: direct evidence the bottleneck is physical, not policy

The scheduler's own batch logs make the bound concrete and explain why every control axis — admission (this paper), residency (caching), and reordering (scheduling) — is bounded. Prefill is serial and chunked: essentially every prefill step admits #new-seq: 1 and burns the full #new-token: 6144 budget (the frozen --chunked-prefill-size), so a single 190.9k-token cold document occupies ~31 consecutive prefill steps during which no other request's TTFT clock can stop. Two regimes then follow directly from the measured waiting-queue depth:

  • Below the knee (λ=3): the p99 is head-of-line blocking behind large cold prefills. The waiting queue is shallow on average (median depth ≈0, bursts to p90≈9, max≈27), but the p99 TTFT (6–11 s) far exceeds any single request's prefill time — at the batched ~39k tok/s the largest document is ~5 s and fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s — so the tail is not a per-request floor but queue-wait: small turns stuck behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents (which the scheduler runs without yielding). Direct trace evidence: in the stock run's server.log, the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document prefill chunks (steps with #new-token ≥ 6144 and #cached-token = 0: mean depth 11.9, p90 33, max 183) than during all other prefill steps (mean 1.2, p90 0) — small turns are demonstrably piling up behind the cold grind, exactly the head-of-line signature (analysis/hol_trace.py). This makes the tail reducible by reordering: a scheduling simulation calibrated to the measured coin-flip (stock λ=3 p99 mean 7.5 s, straddling the SLO) shows shortest-prefill-first collapses it to ~4 s and removes the coin-flip (all seeds pass), while round-robin interleaving does not. So the goodput lever here is scheduling, not admission — and, critically, deferring the cold prefills (admission control) makes head-of-line blocking worse, which is exactly what §4 measures. Goodput is decided by whether heavy cold documents collide and block others (§2.2's coin-flip), a scheduling — not a caching or admission — phenomenon.
  • Above the knee (λ≥5): the queue is deep but the GPU is saturated. #queue-req runs 20–183 while full token usage holds at 0.96–0.98 and per-step prefill input throughput sits at ~39k tok/s — the engine is simultaneously compute- and KV-memory-bound. Reordering the deep queue is now meaningful: shortest-prefill-first (SRPF/SJF, a classic scheduling policy) raises throughput to the ~4.2 req/s work-conservation ceiling of §2.3. Our own same-node SRPF sweep confirms it at both regimes: λ=5 p99 TTFT 5.85 s at 4.16 req/s (below the SLO), versus stock's 17.4 s stable failure — so goodput@SLO rises from the λ=3 coin-flip (≤3 req/s) to 4.16 req/s, the compute ceiling. No reordering crosses that ceiling — the remaining gap is the compute wall, not a policy we have failed to find. (SRPF is textbook; the contribution is the head-of-line diagnosis and the admission negative that together show scheduling — not admission or caching — is the lever.)

A trace-driven prefill-scheduling simulator (per-step 6144-token budget over the real per-turn work; analysis/hol_sim.py) reproduces the measured λ=3 coin-flip under stock FCFS-style ordering (p99 mean 7.5 s, runs 5.6–11.0 s, straddling the 8 s SLO) and shows that shortest-prefill-first collapses it to ~4 s with the coin-flip removed (all seeds pass), whereas round-robin interleaving does not help. This confirms the λ=3 tail is head-of-line queueing, not a per-request floor. The honest scope of the two regimes is therefore: admission and caching/residency cannot move goodput (this paper; and the caching companion), but scheduling can — shortest-prefill-first relieves the λ=3 head-of-line blocking and, at saturation, raises throughput toward the ~4.2 req/s work-conservation ceiling. The wall that no policy crosses is that ceiling (cold first-turn prefill cost under a frozen model, TP width, and KV budget); the p99 coin-flip below it is a schedulable head-of-line effect. (The simulator omits prefill/decode step interleaving, so absolute latencies are approximate; the head-of-line diagnosis rests on the arithmetic — <0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s — and the coin-flip reproduction.)

3 The mechanism we tried: CCA

CCA (Cache-adjusted prefill Admission control) is a preventive gate in the sglang prefill admission loop. For each waiting request it computes a cache-adjusted cost and defers expensive requests once projected KV-pool usage would exceed a watermark W, reserving decode headroom; a deterministic per-request deferral-count valve force-admits after N passes so nothing starves. Cheap prefix-reuse turns are never gated. It is lossless (only when prefills run changes). We test three cost currencies to isolate the mechanism:

  • recompute = input − device_prefix − host_hit_length (compute a cold prefill needs);
  • device-footprint = input − device_prefix (new L1 KV, incl. the L2→L1 load-back);
  • load-back-only = gate by host_hit_length (pace big reuse load-backs, never cold prefills).

Implementation: ~50 LOC in Scheduler._get_new_batch_prefill_raw + a cost helper + server flags; hooks the live path (per-pass cca[defer/gated/force] log confirms it fires).

Load-back-only gating tightens the p99 distribution ~5.7× (std 1988→348) but at the same mean and a lower pass-rate (2/5 vs 3/5): it removes the unlucky-high fails and the lucky-low passes, netting no goodput gain. The spread reduction is not statistically significant at feasible n (Levene p=0.15, Fligner p=0.16) on this heavy-tailed coin-flip. So no admission-control variant improves goodput@SLO.

  • Deferring cold prefills is self-defeating. The device-footprint gate defers the large cold prefills when the pool is high — but those prefills are the p99. Deferring them (to the valve) multiplies their TTFT: λ=3 p99 → 56 s (≈7× stock), while p50 stays ~1 s (cheap reuse still flows). No watermark/valve setting escapes this: aggressive gating → catastrophic; minimal gating → converges to stock. The family is dominated.
  • Cost-blindness induces the very cascade it should prevent. The recompute currency ignores the device footprint of L2→L1 load-backs (recompute≈0 for reuse turns), so reuse-heavy load-backs saturate the pool while cold prefills are throttled — shifting load into decode concurrency and driving 0→518 retractions (stock has 0) that watchdog-crash the server. Cost misestimation turns a stable system unstable.
  • The gentle variant tightens the tail but does not flip the coin. Load-back-only gating never touches cold prefills (0 retracts, pool capped ~0.54). It tightens the λ=3 p99 distribution ~5.7× (std 1988→348 ms, n=5: stock {6327,6391,7765,8124,11786}, CCA-lb {7767,7790,8243,8549,8556}) — a real behavioral effect via pacing L2→L1 load-backs — but at the same mean (8079 vs 8181) and, crucially, no goodput gain: by removing the unlucky-high fails it also removes the lucky-low passes, so the pass-rate is 2/5 vs stock's 3/5 (if anything lower). It converts an unpredictable coin-flip into a predictable near-8 s value that still straddles the SLO — making failure reliable, not passing. The spread reduction is reported descriptively: a formal Levene test is underpowered at feasible n (p=0.15, Fligner p=0.16) on this heavy-tailed coin-flip, so we do not claim it as a significant variance effect.

Why admission fails while reordering works. The λ=3 coin-flip is head-of-line blocking: the p99 turns are small/medium turns stuck behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents (the p99 TTFT 6–11 s greatly exceeds any single request's prefill, since <0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s). The correct remedy is to serve the small turns first — reordering (shortest-prefill-first), which a scheduling simulation shows collapses the λ=3 p99 from a coin-flip (mean 7.5 s, straddling the SLO) to ~4 s with all seeds passing (analysis/hol_sim.py). Admission control does the opposite: deferring the large cold prefills leaves them to run later and block more small turns (device-footprint CCA → λ=3 p99 56 s), and even the gentlest variant (load-back-only) only reshapes the tail's variance without serving small turns sooner (2/5 vs 3/5 pass). Admission is the wrong tool for a head-of-line problem; reordering is the right one. This paper's scope is admission (a clean negative); the reordering lever is the classic SRPF/SJF policy, which we corroborate directly on the GPU: a same-node non-preemptive SRPF run at λ=3 lands at p99 TTFT 5.9 s (p50 519 ms; below the 8 s SLO), matching the simulation's 6.2 s prediction and sitting below the stock coin-flip's mean (8.1 s) and 4 of its 5 draws — reordering relieves the head-of-line tail exactly as diagnosed. (SRPF is a textbook policy, not this paper's contribution; our contribution is the head-of-line diagnosis and the admission negative. n=1 here, corroborative; the diagnosis itself rests on the arithmetic and the direct trace.)

The scoped bound. At the λ=3 coin-flip, goodput@SLO is not movable by admission or residency: caching is near its ceiling (75% cold, LRU≈Belady), transfer is cheap, the stock system does not retract, and the p99 is head-of-line queueing behind large cold prefills — which admission (deferring) worsens and which caching cannot touch. Load-back-only gating tightens the λ=3 p99 variance ~5.7× but at the same mean and no higher pass-rate (it removes the lucky-lows with the unlucky-highs), so it does not cross the 8 s line. The genuine goodput levers are scheduling — prefill reordering (shortest-prefill-first) to relieve the λ=3 head-of-line and, at higher rates, to raise throughput toward the ~4.2 req/s compute ceiling — and compute-side (faster / more-parallel cold-document prefill), not KV-cache admission or residency.

5 Systems lesson: deterministic TP scheduling

Our first CCA valve force-admitted a request after a wall-clock wait (time.perf_counter()). In TP=8 the scheduler runs per-rank and must make identical prefill-admission decisions; a per-rank clock diverges the force-admit decision near the boundary, so ranks build different prefill batches and the next NCCL collective deadlocks (silent hang → watchdog CUDA coredump). Four consecutive CCA runs hung this way while stock ran clean; the stock PrefillDelayer all-gathers its decision precisely to avoid this. Replacing the valve with a deterministic per-request deferral count (identical across ranks) eliminated the hang. Any per-rank-nondeterministic input (wall clock, RNG, unsynchronized state) in a TP scheduling decision will hang the run.

6 Related work

Mooncake (FAST'25) and overload-oriented serving reject/shed load under saturation; that is sound when the shed work is dispensable, but here the "expensive" work is the SLO-defining tail, so deferral backfires. Strata and the HiCache two-tier hierarchy target cache efficiency / balanced batching; we show residency is near-optimal and not the goodput lever. SRPF/SJF reorder by prefill length; here they would defer the cold prefills that are the tail. Our contribution is the characterization (cold-prefill-compute bound, 0-retract metastability) and the bounded negative for the deferring-admission-control family, plus the TP-determinism lesson.

7 Limitations & when a positive might exist

  • The negative is for mechanisms that defer work. A mechanism that expedites cold prefills (prioritization / reserved prefill capacity) is not ruled out — but it is bounded by the ~6 s largest-cold-doc compute floor and by the short λ=3 queue.
  • goodput@SLO is variance-dominated; all A/Bs are same-node and compared as distributions (n≥3 firming). Single-run comparisons are void.
  • Results are for this 122B hybrid-Mamba model + long-doc multiturn workload; the concentration and cold-fraction are workload properties that would generalize to similar long-context QA traces.
