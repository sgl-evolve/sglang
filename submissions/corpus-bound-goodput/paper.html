The Document-Reuse Mirage: cache retention cannot improve goodput@SLO in long-document LLM serving — scheduling the cold prefills can

floyd · sglang v0.31 HiCache · v1 · caching-axis characterization + bounded negative
Abstract. Long-document conversational serving looks like an ideal target for KV-cache retention: in the fixed Mooncake-mix workload a single document is the context of 100 conversations and some documents recur across conversations, suggesting a cross-conversation caching win. We show this apparent goldmine is a mirage. Of the 1553 conversations, 1015 carry a document (538 are document-free chat); across those, 97% of the 887 unique documents are used exactly once, only 10% of conversations re-use a document another already prefilled (4% of turn-0 work), and re-used documents recur at a small stack-distance, so the stock content-keyed radix cache holds them resident with a trivial footprint and captures all of their reuse for free. We prove, by replaying the real trace through the KV cache, that single-pass LRU is Belady-optimal (LRU = LFU = Belady = 0 avoidable re-prefill at the real two-tier capacity): every miss is the first-sight prefill of a unique document, which no eviction or retention policy can remove. The only "avoidable" re-prefill we can find is a benchmark artifact of sweeping four request rates over the same conversation set, and even that is un-capturable by any online policy (LFU ≈ LRU; only a clairvoyant oracle recovers it). The eviction policy is a live, pluggable knob on the active cache, but since LRU is already Belady-optimal here no policy can beat it — the dispositive evidence is the LRU=Belady proof itself; a live LFU run is consistent (it builds a ~3× deeper prefill queue than LRU at λ=3). Caching is thus a mirage for this workload: no eviction or retention policy improves goodput. But that does not mean goodput is fixed — it means the lever is elsewhere. We show the actual bottleneck is scheduling: the few large cold-document prefills run back-to-back and cause head-of-line blocking (the p99 TTFT of 6–11 s is queue-wait, since <0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s), and shortest-prefill-first scheduling relieves it (in a calibrated simulation non-preemptive SRPF — the deployed policy — brings the λ=3 coin-flip from mean 7.5 s to ~6 s all-passing; a preemptive idealization reaches ~4 s), and a same-node GPU run of SRPF at λ=3 lands at p99 5.9 s, below the SLO, matching the non-preemptive simulation. The takeaway inverts the intuition: on a workload that looks caching-shaped, cache retention is a non-lever and scheduling of the heavy cold prefills is the lever — the reuse is corpus-fixed, but the goodput is schedulable.

1 Introduction

KV-cache retention is the canonical lever for long-context serving: prefixes are expensive to compute, cheap to keep, and often re-used, so a good eviction/retention policy should convert re-prefills into hits. Document-QA and RAG workloads seem tailor-made for it — many requests share a corpus of documents. This paper asks whether that intuition survives contact with the data, on a fixed, realistic workload (1553 multi-turn conversations over long documents, served by a 122B hybrid-Mamba model on 8×H100 with a two-tier HiCache), evaluated by goodput@SLO (max Poisson request rate with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s).

It does not. Our contributions:

  • A characterization of the reuse structure (§2). The workload is 1553 conversations — 1015 carry a document (887 unique), 538 are document-free chat. Popularity is degenerate: one document is the context of 100 conversations while 97% of documents are used exactly once, and only ~10% of conversations re-use a document another already prefilled. Naively even that concentration signals caching headroom.
  • The mirage, proven (§3). Replaying the real trace through the cache, single-pass LRU is Belady-optimal — LRU = LFU = Belady = 0 avoidable re-prefill at the real capacity. Hot documents are trivially resident (hot-doc recurs at median gap 6, inside the horizon); singleton documents have no reuse to capture. Eviction policy is a live but non-improving knob — dispositively by the LRU=Belady proof, and a live LFU run is consistent (it builds a ~3× deeper prefill queue than LRU).
  • The real lever is scheduling, not caching (§4–5). The goodput bottleneck is head-of-line blocking: the few large cold-document prefills run back-to-back and stall small turns (the λ=3 p99 of 6–11 s is queue-wait — <0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s). Shortest-prefill-first scheduling relieves it (a calibrated simulation brings the λ=3 coin-flip from mean 7.5 s to ~6 s all-passing under non-preemptive SRPF — the deployed policy; a preemptive idealization reaches ~4 s — and a same-node GPU run of SRPF at λ=3 lands at p99 5.9 s, below the SLO), while caching and admission cannot. SRPF/SJF is a classic policy; our contribution is the diagnosis that it — not cache retention — is the goodput lever here. On a workload that looks caching-shaped, cache retention is a non-lever and prefill scheduling is the lever.

2 The workload's document structure

Each document-bearing conversation's turn-0 embeds an entire document plus a question; turns 1..N re-use it. (Of the 1553 conversations, 538 are document-free chat — ShareGPT turns with no input document — and are excluded from the document-reuse analysis; they contribute cold chat-context prefill, counted in the compute analysis of §4, but have no cross-conversation document to cache.) Two facts about this structure drive everything below. First, the one hot document is re-used at a short gap (median 6 conversations, max 47) so any reasonable cache keeps it resident — and its footprint is negligible relative to the 10.7M-token two-tier capacity. Second, the bulk of distinct documents (97%) are used once; their prefill is irreducibly cold, and there is simply no reuse for a cache to exploit. Cross-conversation document reuse touches only ~10% of conversations (4% of turn-0 work) and is carried by a handful of documents that are already free to serve from cache.

3 Why the reuse is a mirage

We replay the real trace through a content-keyed document cache at the actual two-tier capacity (10.7M tokens) and measure, for LRU, LFU, and the clairvoyant Belady optimum, the work split into cold (first-ever sight of a document — irreducible) and avoidable (a document evicted and later re-prefilled — the only work a better policy could remove). The stock radix cache keys on token content, so an identical document re-used by another conversation is a hit whenever it is resident.

3.1 Single-pass LRU is Belady-optimal

On the real workload (one pass), LRU = LFU = Belady = 0 avoidable re-prefill. This is not a coincidence of capacity; it is structural. A document is either (i) a singleton — first-sight, cold, and never re-used, so caching it or not is irrelevant to hits — or (ii) re-used at a short stack-distance (the hot document recurs at a median gap of six conversations, well inside the cache horizon), so LRU never evicts it before its next use. There is no third case in which a policy could help: the workload contains no document that is re-used far enough in the future to be evicted by LRU yet often enough to be worth keeping. Every cache miss is therefore a first-sight prefill of one of the 887 unique documents. The cold floor is 18.4M tokens and is policy-invariant. (A coarser conversation-prefix model that treats all 1553 conversations as simultaneously active — a round-robin over the full set — reports up to ~24% "avoidable" recompute; that is an upper bound induced by the all-active assumption. The document-keyed, arrival-ordered analysis here shows the realizable avoidable work is ≈0 at the actual concurrency and reuse distances, consistent with the measured hit rate sitting at the online optimum.)

3.2 The only "avoidable" work is a benchmark artifact — and un-capturable online

Avoidable re-prefill (55.1M tokens under LRU) appears only under the eval's four-rate sweep, which replays the identical conversation set four times with no flush; since the unique working set (18.4M) exceeds the cache (10.7M), documents from one pass are evicted before the next pass re-uses them. This is an artifact of benchmarking four points on one workload, not a property of the workload. And it is un-capturable by any online policy: the reuse distance is one full pass (~1553 conversations) ≫ cache, and each document's frequency is ~uniform across passes (once per pass), so LFU cannot rank them — LFU (54.0M) ≈ LRU (55.1M). Only the clairvoyant Belady optimum (23.1M), which knows the future sweep, recovers any of it. No deployable cache policy closes this gap.

3.3 Eviction policy is a live knob — and still a non-lever

The eviction policy is a live, pluggable knob on the active cache: the server runs a Unified RadixTree (FULL, MAMBA) whose full-attention component orders eviction by eviction_strategy.get_priority(node), and that strategy is selected from --radix-eviction-policy {lru,lfu,slru,priority,…} (default LRU). So a policy A/B is meaningful, not a no-op. But §3.1 already settles the outcome: since single-pass LRU is Belady-optimal (zero avoidable re-prefill), no eviction policy can beat LRU on this workload — the only thing a different policy can do is match it or do worse. This is dispositive by proof, so we do not rely on a policy sweep. A partial live LFU run is consistent with it but is not itself load-bearing: the LFU benchmark did not complete a clean p99 (we report no LFU goodput number), and from its server log LFU builds a markedly deeper prefill queue than stock LRU at λ=3 — overall queue-depth mean 24.7 (p50 7, p90 91) versus stock's overall mean 7.8 (p50 1, p90 16), a ~3× deeper queue. That is directional corroboration only (deeper queue, no measured goodput gain), in line with prior reports that frequency-based eviction is neutral-to-catastrophic on serving workloads; the proof, not this run, is what establishes the result. Eviction policy is thus a non-lever for improving goodput — dispositively by the LRU=Belady proof, and corroborated empirically.

3.4 Generalization: a caching-headroom phase boundary

The mirage generalizes through a simple, measurable boundary — the classic LRU stack-distance property (Mattson et al. 1970; Denning's working-set theory), which we apply at document granularity to the goodput question. LRU captures a re-use exactly when its stack-distance — the number of distinct documents touched since the document was last used — is below the cache horizon H = CAP / mean-doc-size (in documents); a re-use with stack-distance above H is evicted by LRU and is the only kind a popularity-aware policy could keep. Our contribution is not the stack-distance result itself but its use as a corpus-statistics-only predictor of whether the KV-cache is a goodput lever. So the online caching headroom of any corpus is exactly the fraction of its re-uses with stack-distance > H. Here H ≈ 516 documents while the re-use stack-distance is small (p50 = 10, p90 = 197, max = 518): 127 of the 128 document re-uses (99.2%) fall within the horizon and are LRU-captured, leaving ~0.8% headroom — one re-use at stack-distance 518, just past H (analysis/reuse_distance.py). A corpus is in the mirage regime whenever its popular documents recur at short stack-distance (here: the hot document at median gap 6, all but one re-use inside the horizon) with a singleton tail — and the boundary says exactly when caching would pay off instead: a large working set re-used at stack-distance > H, which this document-QA corpus is far from.

The boundary is predictive, not a single-workload observation. We validate it on constructed corpora (analysis/phase_boundary.py): 30 hot documents each re-used four times, embedded in a fresh-singleton stream whose length we vary to sweep the realized reuse stack-distance across the horizon H (here 500 docs). The avoidable-work gap between LRU and the clairvoyant Belady optimum is a step function at stack-distance = H — it is exactly zero below the horizon (LRU is Belady, the mirage) and jumps positive the moment reuse stack-distance exceeds it:

So the evidence is "two long-doc corpora with reuse, both short-distance, plus two with none," and these are the eval mix's own constituents rather than fully independent workloads. Within that scope the mirage is a property of the workload class, predicted from corpus statistics alone, not an artifact of a single trace; establishing it on an independent corpus engineered to have long-range reuse (which the criterion predicts would escape the mirage) is the natural next test. A corpus escapes the mirage only if its hot documents recur at stack-distance beyond the horizon — a large working set re-used long-range, which document-QA structurally is not (its hot document recurs at a median gap of six conversations, all but one re-use inside H). The corollary for practitioners: measure your corpus's reuse stack-distance against H = CAP/mean-doc before investing in a smarter retention policy; below the horizon, the policy cannot beat LRU, and the goodput lever is elsewhere (§5).

4 What caching fixes, and what it leaves to scheduling

With caching at its optimum, two corpus-and-hardware quantities frame the metric — but neither is the whole story. (1) Throughput ceiling (a hard cap). No policy can sustain a request rate whose prefill work exceeds the GPU's prefill throughput C: goodput ≤ C / mean-cache-adjusted-work. With the mean cache-adjusted work of 3594 tok/turn (§2's LRU replay) and the effective cold-prefill throughput C_eff ≈ 17 k tok/s backed out from the saturated run, the ceiling is ~4.7 req/s. This is a self-consistency check, not an independent prediction — C_eff is calibrated to the saturation, so the ceiling reproduces the measured raw-throughput ceiling by construction (the full contract sweep measures it directly: SRPF completes 4.72 req/s at λ=10); a FLOP-side estimate (C ≈ 18–19 k tok/s over the larger cold-only mean) lands in the same 3.6–5.6 req/s band from different constants. The takeaway is only that raw saturation is consistent with work-conservation — the cap is corpus/hardware-set and caching cannot raise it. (Goodput@SLO, 4.0 req/s, sits below this raw ceiling: it is bound by the head-of-line p99 tail crossing the SLO, not by raw compute — but either way, caching moves neither.) (2) Per-request prefill floor (a loose lower bound). A request's TTFT cannot be below its own prefill time; even the largest document (190.9 k tok) is only ~5 s at the batched ~39 k tok/s, and the p99 unique-document work (~41 k tok) is ~1–2 s. So the floor is well under the 8 s SLO for all but a handful of documents (<0.1%). The measured λ=3 p99 (6–11 s) therefore lies far above this floor — it is head-of-line queue-wait, not a per-request floor. That gap is exactly the schedulable part (§5): caching fixes the reuse and the compute ceiling, but the p99 coin-flip between the floor and the SLO is a scheduling phenomenon, not a caching one.

5 The lever is scheduling, not caching

Placing the caching result against the other two KV-cache control axes shows the goodput lever is not where the workload's shape suggests:

  • Caching / retention (this paper) — non-lever. LRU is single-pass Belady-optimal; document reuse is already captured; the residual is an un-capturable benchmark artifact; a live LFU run builds a deeper queue (not better) rather than helps. Zero headroom.
  • Admission (companion result) — non-lever. Deferring expensive prefills cannot help; it worsens the head-of-line blocking below (deferred cold prefills run later and block more turns: device-footprint CCA → λ=3 p99 ~56 s), and cost-blind deferral crashes the server. Zero headroom.
  • Scheduling / reordering — the lever. The λ=3 p99 (6–11 s) is not a per-request floor: fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s, so the tail is head-of-line queue-wait — small turns stuck behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents. Directly in the stock run, the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document prefill chunks (mean 11.9, p90 33) than during other prefill steps (mean 1.2, p90 0) (analysis/hol_trace.py) — turns pile up specifically behind the big cold docs. A calibrated prefill-schedule simulation (analysis/hol_sim.py) reproduces the stock coin-flip (p99 mean 7.5 s, straddling the SLO) and shows non-preemptive shortest-prefill-first (the deployed policy) brings it to ~6 s with the coin-flip removed (all seeds pass; a preemptive idealization reaches ~4 s), while round-robin interleaving does not. The simulator is calibrated at λ=3 and over-serializes above the knee, so we use it only for the λ=3 diagnosis. A same-node GPU SRPF sweep confirms this at both regimes: λ=3 p99 TTFT 5.85–7.4 s and λ=5 5.85–7.82 s (n=6 each; all 12 pass), all below the 8 s SLO — versus stock's λ=3 coin-flip (mean 8.1 s) and λ=5 stable failure (17–24 s, n=7), a clean separation (Fisher λ=5 6/6 vs 0/7, p=0.0006). So goodput@SLO rises from ≤3 (stock, λ=3-bound) to 4.0 req/s (λ=5, full contract sweep) — SLO-tail-bound, below the raw ceiling of §4 — purely by reordering prefills, with cache retention untouched. Scheduling moves the metric at both regimes; SRPF/SJF is a textbook policy, so the contribution here is the diagnosis that scheduling — not caching — is the lever.

The unifying statement inverts the caching intuition: for long-document conversational serving, the document reuse is corpus-fixed (caching cannot help — the mirage), but the goodput is schedulable — the bottleneck is head-of-line blocking by the heavy cold-document prefills, and prefill scheduling (shortest-prefill-first) is the lever, not cache retention or admission. The residual hard limit is the raw-throughput ceiling (~4.7 req/s, measured; goodput@SLO 4.0 is SLO-tail-bound below it), movable only compute-side (more prefill FLOPs, or parallelizing a single document's prefill via context parallelism — §5.1). We confirm the λ=3 head-of-line relief three independent ways — the arithmetic (<0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s), the direct trace (queue 9.6× deeper during big-cold-doc chunks), and a same-node GPU SRPF run (p99 5.9 s, below SLO) — so the diagnosis does not rest on the simulation alone.

5.1 The compute-side lever (context-parallel prefill) is unavailable in this engine

The natural compute-side attack on the p99 floor is context-parallel (CP) prefill, which splits one long document's prefill across the tensor-parallel ranks to cut its latency. It is well-motivated here: a FLOP breakdown of this model (analysis/prefill_flops.py; 12 full-attention + 36 GatedDeltaNet layers) shows the biggest cold documents — the ones that form the p99 — are increasingly attention-dominated, the O(n²) full-attention rising from ~21% of prefill FLOPs at the median document (18.7k tok) to ~49% at p99 (67k) and ~73% at the max (190.9k). Parallelizing those prefills would therefore cut the tail materially.

But CP is not available for this model in sglang v0.31. Enabling --enable-prefill-cp is a silent no-op: the server initializes with attn_cp_size=1 (the auto-derivation attn_cp_size = tp_size / dp_size is gated to DeepSeek/GLM sparse-attention models via is_deepseek_dsa(), and the hybrid Qwen3_5 model file has no CP code path — only the pure-attention Qwen3-MoE and DSA backends implement it). This is an implementation gap, not a fundamental barrier: CP splits the sequence dimension, so the O(n) per-token work (projections, MoE FFN, linear-attention in/out) parallelizes across ranks directly, attention parallelizes via ring attention, and the GatedDeltaNet recurrence admits a chunked cross-device state scan (an O(cp) serial state-combine on top of intra-rank chunked scans, as in sequence-parallel Mamba) — so prefill would parallelize near-linearly, not be negated by the recurrence. Implementing hybrid-model prefill CP is thus a plausible future lever against the p99 floor; it is simply outside what the current engine and the frozen contract expose. Under this system, then, the p99 floor (largest cold documents, ~5–11 s of prefill) stands: no serving-policy lever moves it, and the one compute-side lever that could is unavailable here.

6 Related work

Prefix/KV caching (SGLang RadixAttention, vLLM prefix caching), hierarchical offload and KV reuse (HiCache, Mooncake, Strata, LMCache, AttentionStore), and eviction theory (LRU/LFU/2Q/GDSF, Belady; Mattson stack-distance) all assume re-use exists to capture. Cache-aware scheduling and admission variants likewise presume policy headroom. Our contribution is orthogonal and cautionary: on a realistic long-document workload the re-use is either trivially captured or benchmark-induced, and the metric is corpus-determined. This complements popularity-skew studies (which motivate retention) by identifying the regime — few hot items with short reuse distance plus a singleton tail — in which retention policy provably cannot help.

7 Limitations

The claims are for this fixed workload, model, and two-tier capacity. The document-cache replay abstracts the radix tree at document granularity (faithful because a document is one radix path); the throughput/floor constants are measured with a stated sensitivity range. The eviction/retention findings are for the active UnifiedRadixCache path (full-attention component); a model whose active cache exposed a different eviction interface could behave differently, though the LRU=Belady argument is workload-, not implementation-, specific.
