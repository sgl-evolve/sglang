Passing at 347 Seconds: How goodput@SLO Hides the Decode Tail in Long-Context Multiturn Serving

kleinrock · sglang KV-cache / serving, v0.31 · rate sweep, Qwen3.5-122B, 2-tier · a measurement / metric-critique + characterization (the eval's own captured metrics + per-request traces)
Abstract. The de-facto headline for tiered-KV LLM serving is goodput@SLO: the maximum request rate holding a p99 time-to-first-token (TTFT) deadline (here 8 s). We show, on a realistic long-context multiturn workload (Mooncake-style mix, 1553 conversations, Qwen3.5-122B, 8×H100, 2-tier KV), that this metric is blind to the tail that actually dominates user-facing latency. In a stock run that passes goodput@SLO at λ=3 (p99 TTFT 6.8 s ≤ 8 s), the p99 end-to-end latency is 347 seconds — the metric reports success while requests wait nearly six minutes. The culprit is a decode tail: p99 inter-token latency (ITL) of ~4–5 s and p99 time-per-output-token (TPOT) of ~3.5 s against a median TPOT of ~0.22 s — a 15–20× tail. We establish three things. (1) The decode tail is stable and invariant: it is essentially unchanged across three independent stock replicates and across load, whether TTFT passes or fails — a separate pathology, not run-to-run noise. (2) It is orthogonal to the prefill lever: shortest-remaining-prefill scheduling (SRPF), which cuts the TTFT tail 3–4× (22 s→7 s), leaves the decode tail untouched (ITL p99 ~4.5 s either way). (3) Its cause is decode starvation under prefill-first scheduling: the engine runs a prefill batch whenever prefill work exists, so under sustained arrivals decode is deferred — we observe 34,071 prefill batches to 1,621 decode batches (21:1), with runs of up to 661 consecutive prefill batches during which hundreds of decoding requests advance no tokens. (4) It is not one flag away: the textbook mitigation (mixed/stall-free batching, --enable-mixed-chunk) crashes the server here — a KV-pool-leak abort reproducing a companion budget-sharing mechanism's failure — so a decode-QoS scheduler redesign is required, a fix the TTFT-only metric gives no incentive to build. The lesson: goodput@SLO is not merely unreliable near the knee (a companion result) but incomplete — optimizing TTFT says nothing about, and its prefill-first mechanism actively worsens, the decode tail that is the real serving bottleneck for long-context multiturn workloads. E2E-tail-aware evaluation is required.

1 Introduction

Tiered-KV LLM servers are increasingly evaluated by goodput@SLO: the maximum sustainable request rate at which the p99 TTFT stays under a deadline. The metric is attractive — TTFT is the latency a user first perceives, and a rate-vs-p99-TTFT curve compactly captures a serving system's capacity. A large body of work (and the companion papers to this one) optimizes exactly this quantity: prefix caching, KV tiering, prefill scheduling.

This paper asks a prior question: is goodput@SLO measuring the right thing? On a long-context multiturn workload we find it is not. TTFT captures only the first token; but these requests generate hundreds of tokens over many conversational turns, and the rest of the latency — the decode phase — is where the real tail lives. We find a stock configuration that passes goodput@SLO while delivering a p99 end-to-end latency of 347 seconds. The metric's green light is a mirage.

Contributions.

  • The metric-blindness result (§2). A run passing goodput@SLO (p99 TTFT 6.8 s) has p99 E2E of 347 s — p99 E2E is ~50× p99 TTFT — because the decode phase (a tail of ITL p99 ~4.5 s, TPOT p99 ~3.5 s vs median 0.22 s) dominates E2E while TTFT-goodput sees only the first token.
  • Invariance and orthogonality (§3). The decode tail is stable across three stock replicates and both loads (not the run-to-run coin-flip that afflicts the TTFT knee), and it is untouched by SRPF (the prefill lever): TTFT and decode are separate latency axes.
  • The cause: decode starvation (§4). The scheduler is prefill-first (prefill runs whenever any prefill work fits); under sustained load decode is deferred for long stretches (21:1 prefill:decode batches; runs up to 661), starving in-flight generations. Not retraction, not KV movement — pure prefill–decode time-sharing.
  • Fixability (§5). The decode tail is not cheaply recoverable: the textbook fix (--enable-mixed-chunk) destabilizes the server (KV-pool leak, reproducing a companion budget-sharing mechanism's identical failure); a principled decode-QoS scheduler is a redesign the metric never rewards.
  • Implication (§6–7). goodput@SLO must be paired with an E2E / decode-tail SLO; optimizing TTFT alone is not just insufficient but, via its prefill-first mechanism, counterproductive for the decode tail.

2 The metric passes while E2E is catastrophic

The evaluation is an open-loop Poisson rate sweep (λ ∈ {3,5,7,10}) over 1553 real multiturn conversations, full decode, on a 2-tier KV server (GPU HBM + 768 GB host), Qwen3.5-122B / 8×H100. The client (sglang bench_serving) records, per rate, p99 TTFT, p99 ITL, p99 TPOT, and p99 E2E. Table 1 shows all four for three independent stock runs.

It is not saturation. The obvious objection is that a 347 s E2E is just open-loop queueing above capacity. It is not. At λ=3 the server is unsaturated — achieved throughput is 2.85–3.02 req/s, i.e. it keeps up with the offered 3 req/s (no unbounded queue) — and the median E2E is a healthy 3.8–7.0 s. Yet the p99 E2E is 347–446 s: a ~90× median-to-p99 blow-up at a load the system comfortably sustains. This is a tail pathology, not a capacity one. Two corroborating facts: (i) ITL — the gap between consecutive tokens of a request already decoding, which contains no queue wait — has a p99 of ~4–5 s even at λ=3; individual 4–5 s inter-token stalls are decode-scheduling artifacts, not queueing. (ii) the median TPOT is healthy (~0.22 s) — the pathology is entirely in the tail: a minority of requests stall for seconds per token while most decode normally. (At λ≥5 genuine saturation adds to E2E on top of this, so we anchor the claim on the unsaturated λ=3 case and on ITL.)

3 The decode tail is invariant and orthogonal to the prefill lever

Invariant (not the TTFT coin-flip). A companion result shows the TTFT knee is a run-to-run coin-flip (p99 TTFT at λ=3 swings 6.8–14.1 s across identical stock runs — Table 1). The decode tail does not: ITL p99 is 4.1–5.0 s and E2E p99 347–446 s across the same three runs, a tight band. The decode tail is a stable, structural property, not sampling noise.

Orthogonal to prefill scheduling. The prefill tail (TTFT) is movable: shortest-remaining-prefill scheduling (SRPF), a reference prefill-ordering policy, cuts p99 TTFT from ~22–28 s to ~5.5–7.8 s at λ=5 (same-node, n=5). Yet it leaves the decode tail essentially unchanged (Table 2). Reordering which prefill runs first does nothing for a tail caused by prefill preempting decode at all.

4 The cause: decode starvation under prefill-first scheduling

The scheduler's batch selection is prefill-first: each iteration it forms a prefill batch if any waiting request can be admitted, and only runs a decode batch when no prefill batch is available (scheduler.py, get_next_batch_to_run: if new_batch is not None: ret = new_batch). Under a sustained arrival stream there is almost always prefill work, so decode is repeatedly deferred.

The server logs make the starvation explicit. Over a stock sweep we count 34,071 prefill batches to 1,621 decode batches (21:1). The run-length of consecutive prefill batches between two decode batches — the number of forward passes an in-flight generation waits without advancing a token — has p50=5, p90=89, p99=229, and a maximum of 661. With hundreds of requests in the running pool (the batch is saturated at the 256-concurrency cap), a burst of prefills stalls every one of them for seconds. This is a time-sharing pathology, not a memory one: we observe zero retraction/preemption events, and a companion result shows the L1↔L2 KV movement is sub-10 ms — the decode KV never leaves the device; the decode simply is not scheduled.

Why chunked prefill does not save it. The contract enables chunked prefill (6144-token chunks), which in principle lets decode interleave between chunks. In practice the scheduler prefers to keep admitting prefill (new arrivals and continued chunks) over issuing a decode batch, so decode still waits through long prefill runs. The fix requires the scheduler to guarantee decode progress, not merely make it possible — which is the subject of §5.

5 The decode tail is not cheaply recoverable: the textbook fix destabilizes

Is the decode tail a mere mis-configuration — one flag away from fixed? We tested the textbook mitigation. The standard answer to decode-stall-under-prefill is stall-free / mixed batching (co-scheduling decode tokens into the same forward pass as a prefill chunk, so decode never waits for prefill; Sarathi-Serve), exposed in sglang as --enable-mixed-chunk. We enabled it on the otherwise-frozen configuration and re-ran the sweep.

It crashes. The mixed-chunk run aborts partway through λ=3 with a hard ValueError: pool memory leak detected! — the KV pool reaches available=5312 of total=2,347,648 with 2.34 M tokens stuck evictable but unfreed (the recurrent-state "mamba" pool leaks in lockstep). The server is killed by its own safety check; no sweep completes.

This is not an isolated bug. The same KV-pool-leak abort appears with a different mechanism that likewise raises concurrent prefill+decode occupancy of the pool — the reserved short-prefill lane of a companion result (which shares per-iteration budget between a heavy prefill and waiting short requests) crashes identically once its reserve is large. Two independent mechanisms that pack more simultaneous prefill-and-decode work into the KV pool both trip the leak detector on this hybrid-MoE + hierarchical-cache + 262K-context configuration: its pool accounting does not sustain the increased concurrency. Reducing decode starvation by mixing decode into prefill is, here, not merely a config flag but a path to instability.

The decode tail is not one flag away. The mitigation the field would reach for (--enable-mixed-chunk) destabilizes this configuration. Reducing decode starvation without changing batch composition — a decode-QoS scheduler that guarantees a decode step every bounded number of prefill steps — is the principled fix, but it is a scheduler redesign, not a knob. And it is a redesign the goodput@SLO metric gives no incentive to build: a server that passes at 347 s E2E already looks optimal on the scoreboard. The decode tail is both hidden by the metric (§2–4) and unrewarded by it.

Scope. The crash is n=1 (a deterministic leak-detector abort, reproducing the companion reserved-lane failure); we did not chase further decode-favoring configurations, as the paper's contribution is the metric-blindness measurement (§2–4), which is independent of any fix. A full decode-QoS scheduler and its prefill–decode Pareto are future work.

6 Related work

Prefill–decode interference and disaggregation. Sarathi-Serve (chunked prefill + stall-free batching) and DistServe / prefill-decode disaggregation target exactly the decode-stall-under-prefill problem; our contribution is not a new scheduler but the measurement that, under the metric the field now optimizes (goodput@SLO on TTFT), this tail is invisible and unrewarded — a passing run hides a 347 s E2E. Serving metrics. TTFT and TPOT/ITL are standard, but goodput is typically defined on TTFT alone; we argue for an E2E- or decode-tail-aware goodput. Companion results. goodput@SLO is also a run-to-run coin-flip near the knee (unreliable); this paper adds that it is incomplete (blind to decode). Together: the metric is both noisy and partial.

7 Limitations & implications

  • Open-loop load inflates E2E. Above the ceiling, queueing dominates E2E; we therefore anchor the decode-stall claim on ITL (inter-token, queue-independent) and on the λ=3 pass-but-347 s case, not on E2E alone.
  • Not a novel mechanism. The fix (stall-free/mixed batching) is known; our contribution is the metric-blindness measurement and the characterization for this workload. We are explicit that any decode-friendly setting we test is a diagnostic, not a claimed mechanism.
  • Scope. Long-context multiturn (documents to 192K tokens, hundreds of output tokens over many turns) is where decode volume is large enough for the tail to dominate; short-prompt/short-output workloads will show less.

9 Conclusion

goodput@SLO green-lights a server delivering 347-second p99 latency. The headline the field optimizes measures the first token and ignores the decode phase, where — for long-context multiturn workloads — the real tail lives: a stable, prefill-orthogonal decode stall caused by prefill-first scheduling. Reliable evaluation of these systems must score the end-to-end tail, not TTFT alone.
