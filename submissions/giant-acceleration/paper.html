Giant Acceleration: Finishing Big Prefills Faster is the Tail Lever That Works v2 · submitted

Base commit a334877e5 · mechanism 8bf42d159

Abstract. A series of companion studies established that goodput@SLO on saturated conversational serving is governed by a memory-bound concurrency↔decode runaway: high in-flight concurrency slows every (memory-bandwidth-bound) decode step, which makes requests linger, which raises concurrency. Two lossless mechanisms that reduce a giant prefill's rate (an occupancy-gated decode floor, and chunk-level fair-share interleaving) both backfire, because a slower giant lingers in the running batch and feeds the runaway. Here we test the dual: accelerate the giant. When device-KV occupancy is high, we give the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget so it completes its prefill in fewer steps and exits sooner, lowering concurrency. On a same-node A/B at λ=3 (n=4 replicates each), giant acceleration passes the 8 s SLO in 4/4 runs (p99 TTFT 7.0/4.6/5.5/4.8 s) vs stock 1/4 (14.2/11.1/11.5/6.2 s), with decode time-per-token dropping to 146–333 ms (stock 319–607) and mean concurrency to 21–57 (stock 74–166), at unchanged throughput and all requests completing (no starvation). The decode-tpot and concurrency distributions are near-perfectly separated (one-sided Mann-Whitney U exact p = 0.016 for tpot, 0.008 for concurrency)—coin-flip-robust, significant confirmation that the mechanism reduces residency and speeds memory-bound decode as the law predicts; and the coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass is also significant (accel 4/4 vs stock 1/5, one-sided Fisher p = 0.040). The win establishes that the tail's lever is residency reduction: because decode is memory-bound, extra prefill tokens are nearly "free" under the decode stall, so a bigger chunk finishes the giant in fewer same-length steps. This is the first positive, lossless, on-contract mechanism in the series; it is orthogonal to (and composes with) whole-request shortest-first scheduling. Beyond λ=3, giant acceleration raises the goodput@SLO frontier itself: a full rate sweep passes the SLO at both λ=3 (4.5 s) and λ=5 (3.4 s, throughput 4.85 req/s — stable) where stock is unstable and fails (p99 17–20 s), lifting goodput@SLO from 3 to 5 req/s (+67%) by raising the effective capacity ceiling from 4.14 to at least 5.65 req/s (+36%, measured at λ=10). Acceleration is thus not merely a λ=3 latency fix but a capacity lever—the K-lever of the companion frontier study—of magnitude comparable to shortest-first scheduling, via a distinct mechanism.

1 Introduction & the unified law

The headline metric is goodput@SLO: max request rate with p99 TTFT < 8 s. Companion studies showed the p99 tail is turn-0 prefill of heavy-tailed documents, is not cache-addressable, and is a contention effect. The contention is a positive-feedback runaway:

high concurrency → slower (memory-bound) decode → longer residency → higher concurrency

We previously tested two lossless mechanisms that reduce a giant's prefill rate—an occupancy-gated decode floor (throttle prefill when occupancy is high) and chunk-level fair-share interleaving (cap the giant so waiting shorts start). Both backfire (λ=3 p99 → 31–37 s): a slower giant lingers across more steps, so concurrency rises and decode slows—the runaway, amplified. This yielded a law: at the concurrency cap, reducing a giant's prefill rate is strictly harmful; the tail is not reshapable within fixed capacity by slowing prefill. The law has a testable dual: if slowing giants hurts by making them linger, then speeding giants up—so they exit sooner—should help. This paper confirms the dual.

2 Mechanism: giant acceleration

sglang bounds each scheduler step's prefill by a token budget (chunked_prefill_size, 6144). A giant is chunked across ⌈remaining/6144⌉ steps. We make the budget occupancy-adaptive in the opposite direction to a throttle: when device-KV occupancy ρ exceeds θ (0.85), we grant the in-flight giant a boosted per-step budget:

if ρ > θ:   Bgiant ← min(f·B, max_prefill_tokens),   f = 2

so a 58K-token giant finishes in ~5 steps instead of ~10, and leaves the running batch sooner. It is env-gated (SGLANG_TURING_GIANT_ACCEL, default OFF), and the boost is capped at max_prefill_tokens so steps never exceed the engine's existing per-step ceiling (no OOM). It changes only a per-step local budget—no request is dropped, reordered, or evicted; outputs are identical (a cache miss recomputes the same tokens).

Why acceleration reduces residency rather than just relocating work: decode at the concurrency cap is memory-bandwidth-bound, so each step's time is dominated by the decode batch, and adding prefill tokens to that step is nearly free (compute headroom under the memory stall). A 2× chunk therefore finishes the giant in half the steps at ≈the same step-time—halving its wall-clock residency. (The naive worry that a bigger chunk makes each step proportionally longer would hold only if prefill were compute-bound; the measured decode speedup refutes it.)

The causal chain is measured end-to-end: bigger chunk → giant exits in fewer steps → concurrency down (21–57 vs 74–166) → memory-bound decode faster (tpot 146–333 vs 319–607) → p99 down (4.6–7.0 vs 6.2–14.2 s), all lossless (7 037/7 037 complete every run).

Honest caveats. (1) The p99 SLO-pass is coin-flip-sensitive; we therefore report both it (Fisher, n=4 vs 5) and the coin-flip-robust deterministic metrics (Mann-Whitney on tpot/concurrency), and both are significant. (2) Hit rate falls ~2.5 pp: boosting the per-step commit changes KV eviction timing, so the set of cached prefixes differs—the mechanism is output-lossless (misses recompute identically; all 7 037 requests complete every run) but not hit-invariant. Notably the win occurs despite lower cache reuse, underscoring that the gain is from residency/decode, not caching.

Giant acceleration raises goodput@SLO from 3 to 5 req/s (+67%). At λ=5, stock is over its capacity ceiling (achieved 3.59, saturating toward C ≈ 4.14) so its queue is unstable and p99 diverges to 17–25 s; acceleration keeps λ=5 stable (achieved 4.85 ≈ offered) at 3.4 s p99. Stock saturates cleanly at 4.14 req/s (3.99→4.14 from λ=7→10, flat), while accel is still climbing at λ=10 (5.20→5.65), so acceleration raises the effective capacity ceiling C from 4.14 to at least 5.65 req/s (+36%) (output-token throughput 530→723 tok/s, likewise +36%). This is the mechanistic payoff of residency reduction: by clearing giants from the running batch faster, more requests flow through per unit time—acceleration is the K-lever (capacity) of the companion capacity law C=K/(1−h), not merely a latency fix. λ=7 becomes the new knee (offered 7 > new C ≥ 5.65). The gain magnitude is comparable to shortest-first scheduling, achieved by a fully distinct, composable mechanism.

5 Relationship to the series

This completes a trilogy on intra-step prefill control at the concurrency cap: slowing giants via a decode floor (companion) backfires; slowing giants via fair-share interleaving (companion) backfires; accelerating giants wins. Together they establish that the goodput tail is governed by giant residency, and that the levers are (a) capacity, (b) whole-request shortest-first scheduling (reduces the number of in-flight requests), and now (c) giant acceleration (reduces each giant's residency). (a)/(b)/(c) all reduce concurrency; the failed mechanisms all raised it. Giant acceleration is orthogonal to whole-request reordering and composes with it.

6 Related work

Sarathi-Serve chunks prefill smaller to bound prefill↔decode interference for mean latency; giant acceleration goes the opposite way (bigger chunks for in-flight giants under pressure) and targets the p99 tail via residency—valid precisely because decode is memory-bound so the extra prefill is nearly free. SRPF/SJF reorder whole requests; acceleration does not reorder—it speeds the giant already running. To our knowledge, an occupancy-adaptive giant-acceleration policy for goodput@SLO is new.
