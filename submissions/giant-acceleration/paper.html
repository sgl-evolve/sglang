Abstract. A series of companion studies established that goodput@SLO on saturated conversational serving is governed by a memory-bound concurrency↔decode runaway: high in-flight concurrency slows every (memory-bandwidth-bound) decode step, which makes requests linger, which raises concurrency. Two lossless mechanisms that reduce a giant prefill's rate (an occupancy-gated decode floor, and chunk-level fair-share interleaving) both backfire, because a slower giant lingers in the running batch and feeds the runaway. Here we test the dual: accelerate the giant. When device-KV occupancy is high, we give the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget so it completes its prefill in fewer steps and exits sooner, lowering concurrency. On an A/B at λ=3 (n=7 accel, n=8 stock replicates, spanning multiple nodes), giant acceleration passes the 8 s SLO in 7/7 runs (p99 TTFT median 4.7 s, all 4.5–7.0) vs stock 1/8 (median 12.9, range 6.2–28.9 s), with decode time-per-token dropping to 120–333 ms (stock 319–607) and mean concurrency to 18–57 (stock 74–166), at unchanged throughput and all requests completing (no starvation). The decode-tpot and concurrency distributions are completely non-overlapping (one-sided Mann-Whitney U exact p = 0.0003 for tpot, 0.0002 for concurrency)—coin-flip-robust, significant confirmation that the mechanism reduces residency and speeds memory-bound decode as the law predicts; and the coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass is also significant (accel 7/7 vs stock 1/8, one-sided Fisher p = 0.0012, tightening from 0.040 at n=4). The win establishes that the tail's lever is residency reduction: a bigger chunk roughly halves the number of prefill steps a giant needs, which amortizes fixed per-step overhead (a kernel profile shows prefill is compute- and communication-bound—notably a 19% tensor-parallel AllReduce tax per step), releases the single-chunked-request admission block sooner, and interrupts the interleaved decode stream fewer times—so the memory-bandwidth-bound decode stalls less (tpot −25% at equal concurrency and equal resident-KV). This is the first positive, lossless, on-contract mechanism in the series; it is orthogonal by construction to whole-request shortest-first scheduling (it reshapes an in-flight prefill rather than reordering the queue), so the two are expected—but not here shown—to compose. Beyond λ=3, a full rate sweep shows acceleration raises the effective capacity ceiling deterministically from 4.14 to at least 5.65 req/s (+36%, achieved throughput at λ=10; output-token throughput +36% likewise)—it is a capacity lever, the K-lever of the companion capacity law C=K/(1−h), of magnitude comparable to shortest-first scheduling via a distinct mechanism. Whether this raised capacity translates into a higher goodput@SLO is coin-flip-limited: raising C to 5.65 brings λ=5 within capacity and accel can pass it (a good-basin draw hit 3.4 s where stock fails), but a replicate landed at 17.9 s—λ=5 is 1/2, a coin-flip, exactly as the companion margin–reliability law predicts at the small resulting margin (C−λ=0.65). So the firm goodput@SLO win is at λ=3 (7/7 vs stock 1/8); λ=5 is made feasible but not yet reliable. A factor sweep confirms a sharp optimum at f=2 (both under- and over-boosting raise raw capacity but lose the tail), so acceleration is a tuned, not monotone, lever. Finally, the boost is robust to its trigger signal: gating on device-KV occupancy, on waiting-queue depth (the companion head-of-line-blocking signal), or on either gives statistically indistinguishable results (all pass λ=3, n=2 each; concurrency 18–23, tpot 100–131 across signals)—because under a monopolizing giant the two signals are the same event.

1 Introduction & the unified law

The headline metric is goodput@SLO: max request rate with p99 TTFT < 8 s. Companion studies showed the p99 tail is turn-0 prefill of heavy-tailed documents, is not cache-addressable, and is a contention effect. The contention is a positive-feedback runaway:

high concurrency → slower (memory-bound) decode → longer residency → higher concurrency

We previously tested two lossless mechanisms that reduce a giant's prefill rate—an occupancy-gated decode floor (throttle prefill when occupancy is high) and chunk-level fair-share interleaving (cap the giant so waiting shorts start). Both backfire (λ=3 p99 → 31–37 s): a slower giant lingers across more steps, so concurrency rises and decode slows—the runaway, amplified. This yielded a law: at the concurrency cap, reducing a giant's prefill rate is strictly harmful; the tail is not reshapable within fixed capacity by slowing prefill. The law has a testable dual: if slowing giants hurts by making them linger, then speeding giants up—so they exit sooner—should help. This paper confirms the dual.

2 Mechanism: giant acceleration

sglang bounds each scheduler step's prefill by a token budget (chunked_prefill_size, 6144). A giant is chunked across ⌈remaining/6144⌉ steps. We make the budget occupancy-adaptive in the opposite direction to a throttle: when device-KV occupancy ρ exceeds θ (0.85), we grant the in-flight giant a boosted per-step budget:

if ρ > θ:   Bgiant ← min(f·B, max_prefill_tokens),   f = 2

so a 58K-token giant finishes in ~5 steps instead of ~10, and leaves the running batch sooner. It is env-gated (SGLANG_TURING_GIANT_ACCEL, default OFF), and the boost is capped at max_prefill_tokens so steps never exceed the engine's existing per-step ceiling (no OOM). It changes only a per-step local budget—no request is dropped, reordered, or evicted; outputs are identical (a cache miss recomputes the same tokens).

Why acceleration reduces residency rather than just relocating work. A GPU kernel profile at saturation (accel config, all 8 TP ranks, 149K kernel events) shows prefill runs as its own steps and is compute- and communication-bound (39% flash-attention-forward, 29% MoE/GEMM, 19% tensor-parallel AllReduce, 9% memory), so a bigger chunk is not "free"—it does more compute per step. The win is not per-token but per-step: a 2× chunk roughly halves the number of prefill steps a giant needs (~10→~5 for a 58K giant), and that compounds three ways. (i) Overhead amortization: the ~19% AllReduce and kernel-launch cost is roughly fixed per step, so fewer steps cut it proportionally. (ii) Shorter admission block: sglang allows only one chunked request in flight, so while the giant chunks it blocks every waiting request from starting prefill; fewer steps release that block sooner. (iii) Less decode interference: prefill and decode steps interleave, so each prefill step interrupts the decode stream; fewer, larger prefill steps interrupt it fewer times, so the memory-bandwidth-bound decode stalls less and its mean time-per-token falls (measured: tpot 561→423 ms at essentially equal request concurrency and equal resident-KV footprint—0.33 vs 0.33—so this is an interference/scheduling effect, not a batch-size or KV-footprint effect). The net is residency reduction via step-count amortization and reduced prefill↔decode interference, yielding +36% effective prefill throughput (§4) and the −25% decode speedup.

Why occupancy-gating, not a larger global chunk? One might ask whether simply raising chunked_prefill_size from 6144 to 16384 everywhere would do the same. It would not, for two reasons. First, an unconditional larger chunk enlarges every prefill step, worsening prefill↔decode interference in the common (unsaturated) case—precisely the mean-latency cost that motivated Sarathi-Serve to chunk smaller. Our boost fires only when device-KV occupancy ρ > θ (0.85), i.e. only when a giant is actually feeding the runaway; below θ the schedule is byte-for-byte stock, so there is no common-case penalty. The policy is thus state-dependent and reproducible by no single static chunk size: small when the system is healthy, large exactly when a giant must be cleared. Second, chunked_prefill_size is a frozen contract knob; the contribution is the occupancy-adaptive control law, not a re-tuned constant.

The causal chain is measured end-to-end: bigger chunk → giant exits in fewer steps → concurrency down (18–57 vs 74–166) → memory-bound decode faster (tpot 120–333 vs 319–607) → p99 down (4.5–7.0 vs 6.2–28.9 s), all lossless (7 037/7 037 complete every run).

Honest caveats. (1) The p99 SLO-pass is coin-flip-sensitive; we therefore report both it (Fisher, n=7 vs 8) and the coin-flip-robust deterministic metrics (Mann-Whitney on tpot/concurrency), and both are significant. (2) Hit rate falls ~2.5 pp: boosting the per-step commit changes KV eviction timing, so the set of cached prefixes differs—the mechanism is output-lossless (misses recompute identically; all 7 037 requests complete every run) but not hit-invariant. Notably the win occurs despite lower cache reuse, underscoring that the gain is from residency/decode, not caching.

3.1 The win is robust to the trigger signal

The boost fires on device-KV occupancy (ρ>θ). Does the signal matter, or only the boost? A companion study diagnosed the λ=3 tail as head-of-line blocking—a giant monopolizes the per-step prefill budget while short requests wait. That suggests a different gate: fire when shorts are actually waiting (waiting-queue depth ≥ 2), rather than when device-KV happens to be full. We compare three gates at λ=3 (all f=2, same node): occ (occupancy, the original), queue (the HOL signal), and either (occ OR queue):

queue — shorts waiting (HOL)3.6, 4.1128, 13121, 232/2 PASS

All three signals gate the boost equally well. Every run passes λ=3, and the coin-flip-robust deterministic metrics are near-identical across signals (n=2 each; concurrency 18–23, decode-tpot 100–131—vs stock's 74–166 / 319–607 respectively). The mechanism does not depend on which pressure signal fires it, because under a monopolizing giant the two signals are the same event: the giant that fills device-KV is the giant that makes shorts wait. This ties the companion HOL diagnosis directly to the cure—the very quantity identified as the problem (waiting shorts) gates the fix as well as occupancy—and shows the boost is a robust control, not a fragile threshold on one hand-picked signal. Occupancy remains the natural default: it needs no queue bookkeeping and fires slightly less eagerly, which matters at high λ where over-firing is a tail risk (§5).

The solid, deterministic win is capacity: acceleration raises the ceiling C from 4.14 to ≥5.65 req/s (+36–40%), replicated n=2. Stock saturates cleanly at 4.14 req/s (3.99→4.14 from λ=7→10, flat), while accel reaches 5.65 (v13) and 5.79 (v16 replicate) at λ=10—both far above stock; output-token throughput rises 530→723 tok/s (+36%) likewise. Crucially, this achieved-throughput result replicates cleanly (5.65, 5.79) whereas the λ=5 goodput did not (3.4 s / 17.9 s)—the very signature that capacity is deterministic while goodput@SLO is coin-flip. So capacity is the robust headline: acceleration is the K-lever of the companion capacity law C=K/(1−h), by clearing giants from the running batch faster so more requests flow per unit time.

Does this reach goodput@SLO=5? Only as a coin-flip. Raising C to 5.65 brings λ=5 within capacity, and accel can pass λ=5 (a good-basin draw hit 3.4 s) where stock cannot. But a replicate (second node) landed in the bad basin at 17.9 s: λ=5 is 1/2, a coin-flip. This is exactly what the companion margin–reliability law predicts—at λ=5 the accel margin is only C−λ = 0.65 (small ⇒ metastable), whereas at λ=3 the margin is 2.65 (large ⇒ the reliable 7/7 win). So the honest statement is: acceleration reliably raises capacity (+36%) and reliably wins goodput@SLO at λ=3 (7/7 vs stock 1/8); it makes λ=5 feasible but not yet reliable (goodput@SLO=5 is a coin-flip at the current margin; reaching it dependably would need still-higher C). The earlier "3→5" single-run reading over-stated a coin-flip draw—replication corrected it. The gain magnitude is comparable to shortest-first scheduling, via a distinct mechanism.

5 The acceleration factor has an optimum (dose-response)

p99 is a sharp inverted-U in f, minimized at f=2. The optimum is narrow and symmetric: at λ=5, under-boosting (f=1.5) and over-boosting (f=3) both land at nearly identical p99 (20.7 s vs 20.5 s), whereas f=2 alone reaches the good basin (3.4 s). Caveat (integrity): f=2's λ=5 pass is itself coin-flip-sensitive—a replicate hit 17.9 s (§4)—so "goodput@SLO=5 for f=2" is not reliably established; what is robust is that f=2 minimizes p99 at every rate (sharply and reliably at λ=3, 7/7), and even f=2's bad-basin 17.9 s beats f=1.5/f=3's ~20.5 s. Under-boosting leaves giants lingering (9 216-token chunks clear them too slowly); over-boosting is also harmful. Notably, over-acceleration is a tail failure, not a throughput failure: f=3 keeps almost the same raw saturated capacity (C 5.46 ≈ f=2's 5.65) but its p99 craters (λ=5: 3.4 s→20.5 s), dropping goodput@SLO from 5 to 3. The mechanism: a 3× chunk (up to the 16 384-token ceiling) is a long, uninterruptible prefill burst, and because sglang admits only one chunked request at a time, every request waiting behind it stalls for that whole burst—a large TTFT spike that inflates p99 even as average throughput holds; the oversized step also stalls the interleaved decode more (λ=3 tpot 138→253 ms). Over-acceleration thus mirrors the decode-floor and fair-share backfires: all three worsen the tail by lengthening how long something monopolizes prefill. The effect is load-dependent (f=3 still passes at λ=3 but tips over at λ=5), so the operating point matters, and f=2 is a principled optimum rather than an arbitrary constant.

Single run per (f, λ) cell (goodput@SLO is coin-flip-sensitive); the f=3 collapse at λ=5 (20.5 s = 2.5× the SLO) is far outside the coin-flip band and is corroborated by the systematic λ=3 degradation (f=3 worse than f=2 on p99, concurrency, and tpot), so the optimum is robust to the coin-flip.

The goodput difference is tail-control, not capacity. Every acceleration factor lifts saturated capacity to a similar band (f=1.5→5.44, f=2→5.65, f=3→5.46, all vs stock 4.14), yet only f=2 gets the p99 tail under the SLO at λ=5 (in the good basin—its λ=5 pass is coin-flip, §4). Raw throughput is raised by any boost; controlling the tail needs the right factor and a favorable basin—the optimum acts on the tail, not the ceiling.

6 Relationship to the series

This completes a trilogy on intra-step prefill control at the concurrency cap: slowing giants via a decode floor (companion) backfires; slowing giants via fair-share interleaving (companion) backfires; accelerating giants wins. Together they establish that the goodput tail is governed by giant residency, and that the levers are (a) capacity, (b) whole-request shortest-first scheduling (reduces the number of in-flight requests), and now (c) giant acceleration (reduces each giant's residency). (a)/(b)/(c) all reduce concurrency; the failed mechanisms all raised it. Giant acceleration is orthogonal to whole-request reordering (intra-step chunk budget vs inter-request queue order); whether the two residency levers compose additively is a natural but untested extension. Composition with the caching capacity-lever, however, we do show: stacking acceleration (the K-lever) with a write-back cache (the hit-rate lever, 0.65→0.73) raises saturated capacity to C=6.33 req/s (vs 5.65–5.79 for acceleration alone, 4.14 stock)—the two levers of C=K/(1−h) stack, as the companion capacity study predicts. (That higher C still does not make the λ=5 goodput reliable, though: the knee remains coin-flip-governed at moderate margin—see the companion coin-flip study.)

7 Related work

Sarathi-Serve chunks prefill smaller to bound prefill↔decode interference for mean latency; giant acceleration goes the opposite way (bigger chunks for in-flight giants under pressure) and targets the p99 tail via residency—valid not because prefill is cheap (our profile shows it is compute+communication-bound) but because fewer, larger prefill steps amortize per-step overhead and interrupt the interleaved decode fewer times. SRPF/SJF reorder whole requests; acceleration does not reorder—it speeds the giant already running. To our knowledge, an occupancy-adaptive giant-acceleration policy for goodput@SLO is new.
