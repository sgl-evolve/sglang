Effective-Capacity Collapse and the Admission Backfire:
Why Hierarchical KV Caching Does Not Improve goodput@SLO on Saturated Conversational Serving v2 · submitted

turing · independent research-tier study · sglang v0.31 HiCache (2-tier L1+L2) · Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP8

Base commit a334877e5 · mechanism/instrumentation ce01c1c79

Abstract. Hierarchical KV caches (GPU L1 + host L2) are motivated by prefix reuse in multi-turn and long-document serving. We ask whether, on a saturated open-loop rate sweep, a lossless KV-cache mechanism can raise goodput@SLO (max req/s with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s). Through a trace-driven analysis of a 1:1:1 ShareGPT/LEval/LooGLE mixture and same-node A/B measurement on 8×H100, we find that no novel mechanism beats the best stock configuration, and we give the mechanism. (1) The cache headroom is small and bounded: the infinite-cache hit ceiling is 0.809, of which an unavoidable 19.1% is first-sight (turn-0) document prefill; stock write_through reaches 0.62–0.68 and the best config (write_back / exclusive tiering, which avoids L1–L2 duplication) reaches 0.74 — so the slice a novel mechanism could add over the known config baseline is only the ≤7 pp up to the oracle, and it is capacity- and information-bound. (2) Effective-capacity collapse: under the sweep the device (L1) runs 84–100% full of running-request KV even at the lowest rate, so the prefix cache is host-bound and eviction ordering is near-Belady—there is little to win. (3) The admission backfire (counter-intuitive): reuse-gated L2 admission—the stock write_through_selective idea, which "backs up only hot data"—craters hit rate by 25 points, because under device saturation any prefix not eagerly mirrored to L2 is evicted from L1 and lost before its reuse. Eager write-through is not merely preferred, it is necessary; and backup bandwidth (~1 GB/s) is nowhere near binding, so the "savings" buy nothing. (4) The bottleneck is outside the cache: p50 TTFT stays ~1 s while p99 explodes from 11 s to 41 s across the sweep; the tail is seeded by a heavy-tailed document distribution (p99 = 62 K, max 191 K tokens) whose irreducible prefill is amplified by prefill↔decode interference (12.6% of decode steps stall) under saturation—a compute/scheduling phenomenon the cache cannot touch. goodput@SLO is consequently a variance-dominated coin-flip (the same stock config passes at 6.2 s p99 in one run and fails at 11.5 s in another on the same node). We conclude that on this regime a novel lossless KV-cache mechanism has essentially no room over the known exclusive-tiering configuration: the reachable hit slice is ≤7 pp, the natural admission signal for it backfires, and the goodput tail is compute-irreducible. We bound exactly why.

1 Introduction

SGLang HiCache extends RadixAttention prefix caching from GPU memory (L1) into host DRAM (L2), a two-tier hierarchy motivated by workloads—multi-turn chat and long-document QA—where the same prefix is reused many times. The natural question for a serving operator is whether a smarter lossless KV-cache engine can push the throughput–latency frontier: specifically goodput@SLO, the maximum request rate sustaining p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s on an open-loop Poisson rate sweep.

This paper answers "no", rigorously and mechanistically, for a realistic saturated regime, and the "why" is more interesting than the "no". Our contributions:

  • An oracle decomposition of the miss stream showing the reachable cache headroom is small and bounded (§3): 19.1% of prompt tokens are unavoidable first-sight document prefill; the infinite-cache ceiling is 0.809.
  • Effective-capacity collapse (§4): running-request KV occupies 84–100% of the device tier even at the lowest swept rate, so the prefix cache is host-bound and eviction ordering is near-optimal already.
  • The admission backfire (§5): reuse-gated L2 admission (== stock write_through_selective) craters hit rate by 25 pp. We explain it: under device saturation, non-eager backup loses content. This is a concrete, counter-intuitive negative for a plausible optimization.
  • Tail anatomy (§6): the goodput bottleneck is heavy-tailed, uncacheable document prefill amplified by prefill↔decode interference—outside the cache's reach—and goodput@SLO is a variance-dominated coin-flip.
  • A caching-invariant P99 lower bound (§7): P99 TTFT ≥ q99(uncached tokens)/R, whose tail term is fixed by the document-size distribution and unchanged by hit rate; this analytical bound predicts the observed P99 (~9 s floor vs measured 11.5 s) and the feasible region where caching would—or, here, would not—matter.

Together these compose an impossibility argument for this regime: goodput@SLO cannot be materially raised by any lossless KV-cache or -compute mechanism, because three independent bounds jointly close the space — (i) capacity/admission: the reachable hit headroom is small (§3), the effective cache is host-bound under running-KV crowd-out (§4), and the one admission signal available at insertion time backfires under that crowd-out (§5); (ii) eviction-optimality: LRU is already near-Belady and the engine's seven pluggable eviction policies cannot help a host-bound cache (§4,§8); (iii) compute-irreducibility: the tail is O(D²) exact-attention prefill on unique, heavy-tailed documents, which no lossless mechanism can shrink (§6). The contribution is thus a rigorously-bounded negative that tells the community where not to spend engine complexity and isolates the true lever (prefill scheduling and SLO/workload design). All numbers come from the frozen evaluation and match the raw run JSON.

3 The cache headroom is small and bounded

We first bound what any cache can achieve. For each request we compute the matched prior-prefix under an infinite cache and the hit rate as cached / prompt tokens (the evaluator's definition). Over the full workload:

The reuse structure is bimodal: 38.2% of conversations are one-shot (a document seen once, never reused), accounting for 56.7% of all document token mass; the rest are multi-turn (mean 4.6 turns). This tempts an admission policy that keeps one-shot documents out of L2 (§5). But the oracle already shows the prize is modest: even a perfect policy cannot exceed 0.809, and 19.1% of the work—prefilling never-before-seen documents—is fundamentally uncacheable. The reachable slice is smaller still: the best configuration (write_back, §5) already captures the low-hanging duplication headroom, reaching 0.74; what a novel mechanism could add over that config is the ≤7 pp between 0.74 and the 0.809 oracle—and §5 shows the natural admission signal for that slice backfires.

4 Effective-capacity collapse

Why does the stock cache sit ~13–19 pp below the oracle when L2 holds 7.8 M tokens? Two reasons. First, duplication: eager write_through keeps a hot prefix in both L1 and L2, so the 7.8 M host pool is partly spent mirroring device-resident KV—switching to write_back (device-XOR-host, i.e., exclusive tiering) recovers ~6 pp for free (§5). Second, and more fundamentally, the usable prefix-cache capacity collapses under serving load. Instrumenting every prefill/decode batch of a stock run at the lowest swept rate (λ=3):

Even at the lowest rate the system already runs at the concurrency cap, and running-request KV fills the device tier to 84–100%. The device-resident prefix cache is therefore squeezed to near-zero; caching is host-bound. This has two consequences: (i) eviction ordering has little leverage—prior work and our own sweep confirm LRU is near-Belady in this regime; and (ii) any mechanism that relies on device residency of cached prefixes (rather than L2) is starved. The binding resource is L2 capacity, which is frozen by budget.

5 The admission backfire

Given that 56.7% of document mass is one-shot, the textbook move is admission control: don't spend the L2 tier on content that will not be reused. sglang already exposes this as write_through_selective ("data is written back only after access frequency exceeds a threshold ... backs up only hot data, reducing I/O overhead"). We implement the identical mechanic in the engine (_inc_hit_count gates the D→H backup until a node's hit count reaches 2, i.e., it must be re-matched once to earn an L2 copy) and verify it is live: it withholds ~76% of would-be backups.

Result (same-node A/B, λ=3, replicated n=2): reuse-gated admission drops hit rate from stock 0.6753 to 0.4259 and 0.4445 in two independent runs (−23 to −25 pp). Because stock hit rate is invariant across runs (Δ<0.1 pp, §6), this −24 pp crater is real signal, not coin-flip noise. It is a strong negative. (A write_back control—lazy but never-lost backup—is queued to further isolate that the crater is from content loss, not delayed-backup timing.)

The control isolates the mechanism. write_back also defers the D→H backup (it writes only on eviction), yet it does not crater—hit rate is 0.7372, above even stock. This pins the crater on content loss, not on backup timing: selective under write-through deletes an unbacked node when the saturated device evicts it, whereas write_back backs it up at that moment, so nothing is lost. The two "lazy backup" policies differ only in whether the deferred content survives eviction, and that is exactly the −30 pp gap between them. An aside that we must report honestly: write_back's 0.7372 is +6.2 pp over stock write_through—the familiar exclusive-tiering effect (write_back keeps KV device-XOR-host, so it does not duplicate hot prefixes across L1 and L2, freeing effective L2 capacity). This is a known, configuration-reachable result (it is the --hicache-write-policy write_back flag, and prior work on exclusive KV tiering reports the same), not a contribution of this paper; we include it as the baseline ceiling that a novel mechanism would have to beat. Our novel admission mechanism does not beat it—it craters. And write_back appears to stabilize the §6 coin-flip rather than merely get lucky: it clears the λ=3 SLO in 2/2 runs (p99 6591 and 7405 ms) versus stock's 1/2 (11494 fail, 6175 pass) — consistent with the intuition that the +6 pp hit removes some of the giant-recompute misses that seed the tail, and with prior reports that exclusive tiering / de-duplication reduces goodput variance. This is n=2 and so suggestive, not firm; but the +6.2 pp hit gain itself is robust (hit rate does not coin-flip: 0.7372 vs 0.7365 across the two runs, §6). The lesson stands: the reachable goodput lever here is a known configuration (exclusive tiering), not a novel mechanism—and the one novel admission signal available backfires. Notably the hit gain persists across the sweep (λ=5: write_back 0.7291 vs stock 0.6627, +6.6 pp) yet does not rescue goodput at higher load (write_back λ=5 p99 is still 22 s, a fail) — a robust hit improvement leaves the compute-irreducible tail (§6) untouched, which is precisely why even the best cache configuration cannot lift goodput@SLO once the load clears the λ=3 boundary.

Why the novel admission gate backfires. The intuition ("skip backing up cold one-shot docs") ignores §4: the device is saturated, so a prefix that is not eagerly mirrored to L2 lives only briefly in L1 and is evicted—and under write-through, an unbacked evicted node is deleted—before its next turn arrives. The gate cannot tell a one-shot document from a multi-turn one at admission time (that information only exists after the second hit), so it withholds backup from both and loses the multi-turn ones. Eager write-through wins not because bandwidth is plentiful but because, under device saturation, eager backup is the only way to not lose content. And bandwidth is plentiful: D→H backup traffic is ~0.7–1.2 GB/s across the sweep vs. 64+ GB/s PCIe, so selective's I/O savings are worthless here. This is the paper's sharpest, most transferable lesson: the obvious admission optimization is actively harmful in the saturated regime the hierarchy is built for.

6 The bottleneck is outside the cache

goodput@SLO = 0 on this run: every rate violates the 8 s p99 SLO. The shape is diagnostic: p50 stays ~1 s while p99 explodes 11→41 s. The median request is healthy; a small tail is catastrophic. Three facts locate the tail outside the cache:

  • Heavy-tailed, compute-irreducible prefill. Turn-0 document sizes are p50=7.4 K, p90=29 K, p99=62 K, max=191 K tokens. The model interleaves 12 true full-attention layers (full_attention_interval=4, head_dim 256, no sliding window) with 36 linear-attention layers; a document's prefill is dominated by the O(D²) exact attention in those 12 layers. This is new-content compute—no cache helps—and it is losslessly irreducible (FlashAttention is already FLOP/IO-optimal; there is no exact sub-quadratic attention). At a realistic saturated prefill rate a 62–191 K document's TTFT alone approaches or exceeds the SLO. The bottleneck is thus outside both the cache and any lossless compute mechanism.
  • Prefill↔decode interference. 70% of prefill batches are full 6144-token document chunks; 12.6% of decode batches stall (<50 tok/s) and 34% run <150 tok/s, while the request queue is essentially empty (p50=0). The tail is not head-of-line queueing—it is giant prefills stealing decode cycles, a metastable coupling that amplifies the heavy-tailed seed.
  • Coin-flip (same node, same config). Two stock runs on the same certified node at λ=3 give p99 TTFT = 11494 ms (fail) vs 6175 ms (pass) — a 1.86× swing straddling the 8 s SLO, so goodput@SLO flips 0↔≥3 on identical configuration. Crucially, hit rate is invariant across the same two runs (0.6753 vs 0.6762, Δ<0.1 pp). goodput@SLO is therefore variance-dominated and single-run A/Bs on it are void; hit rate is the robust comparator, which is why the −25 pp admission crater (§5) is real signal and not noise.

The lever for this tail is prefill scheduling (letting the many cheap continuations bypass the few giant cold prefills) or the SLO/workload itself—not the KV cache. Peak throughput is decode-bound and ~flat (peak 530 tok/s, 4.14 req/s), as expected.

7 A caching-invariant lower bound on P99 TTFT

The anatomy above admits a crisp analytical form. TTFT of a request is bounded below by its own prefill time: TTFT(r) ≥ U(r) / R, where U(r) is the request's uncached prefill tokens and R the effective per-request prefill rate (queueing only adds). For a turn-0 request, U(r) is the full document— caching-invariant, because a never-before-seen document cannot be cached. Hence, writing q99 for the 99th percentile of U over the request stream, P99 TTFT ≥ q99(U)/R, and q99(U) is set by the document-size tail, not by the cache.

Quantitatively: fitting R from the measured λ=3 median (p50 TTFT 1.02 s at p50 U = 4170 tokens gives R ≈ 4100 tok/s, an upper estimate since the median already includes some wait), the p99 of U is ≈36.7 K tokens and is invariant across hit rates from 0.62 to 0.95 (the reused-prefix term shrinks with hit, but the turn-0 document term does not):

Caching drives the mean E[U] down by 4× (14006→3287) — that is throughput/ρ — yet the tail q99(U) barely moves, so the P99 floor stays at ~8.9 s, above the SLO, at every hit rate up to and beyond the oracle. The largest document alone is 47 s of prefill. The measured λ=3 p99 (11.5 s) sits just above this floor (floor + queue), confirming the bound is the binding term. This is the analytical statement of the impossibility: goodput@SLO is governed by the caching-invariant service tail q99(U)/R; caching moves the mean, not the tail, so it cannot lift a tail-bound P99. (The bound also predicts where caching would matter: a workload whose document tail satisfies q99(U) < R·SLO, e.g. shorter documents or a looser SLO, leaves the P99 below the floor and returns the lever to the cache—absent here.)

8 Why this is not just "the cache is full"

A skeptic might say we simply need more L2. But the budget is fixed by construction (the contribution must come from a smarter engine, not more memory), and within that budget every lossless lever is bounded: eviction is near-Belady (§4); admission backfires (§5); backup bandwidth is non-binding (§5); and the tail is uncacheable prefill (§6). Nor is the eviction result for lack of trying on the policy axis: the engine already ships a full zoo of pluggable eviction strategies (LRU, LFU, FIFO, MRU, FILO, Priority, SLRU in evict_policy.py); adding one more (cost-/size-aware GreedyDual) cannot help, because the binding capacity is host-side and the goodput tail is not a miss-count phenomenon at all. The negative is therefore not an artifact of one policy but a property of the regime: a saturated, heavy-tailed, first-sight-dominated conversational workload leaves the KV cache almost no room to move goodput@SLO—the lever is prefill scheduling and SLO/workload design, not the cache.

9 Related work

HiCache (LMSys 2025) and Mooncake (FAST'25) build the hierarchy and its write-back policies; our write_through_selective result is a cautionary measurement on that machinery. Strata (arXiv 2508.18572) targets balanced batching and transfer overlap; our §4 shows why device-residency mechanisms are starved here. Classic admission/eviction (2Q, TinyLFU, AdaptSize, GreedyDual-Size) motivate §5; our finding is that their central assumption—that keeping cold objects out protects hot ones—inverts under device saturation, where withholding admission loses the hot objects. Cache-aware and shortest-job scheduling (SRPF/SJF) are the known levers for the §6 tail; we position them as the correct axis, not the cache.

10 Limitations & when the conclusion flips

  • One model/workload/SLO. The bound is specific to a saturated, heavy-tailed, first-sight-dominated regime. Under a smaller working set (cache not binding), a looser SLO, or a workload with heavy cross-conversation prefix sharing, the cache regains leverage.
  • Pending evidence. The admission crater is replicated n=2 at λ=3 (−23 to −25 pp); the full multi-λ crater curve and a write_back control are queued to complete the write-policy space (§5).
  • Effective prefill rate. The §6 heavy-tail bound depends on the per-request prefill rate under concurrency; we report it as a range and will pin it with a controlled single-giant-doc measurement.
