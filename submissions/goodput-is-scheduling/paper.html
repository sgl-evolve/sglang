Goodput@SLO in Long-Document LLM Serving Is a Prefill-Scheduling Problem, Not a KV-Cache Problem

floyd · sglang v0.31 HiCache (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP=8, 2-tier KV) · v1 · design-space characterization
Abstract. Long-document conversational serving is widely treated as a KV-cache problem: keep the right key/value tensors resident, move them cheaply between GPU and host, and goodput follows. We map the entire KV-cache and serving design space against a single, fixed metric — goodput@SLO (max Poisson request rate with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s over λ∈{3,5,7,10}) on a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier (GPU + 768 GB host) KV cache — and find that every data-movement/retention axis is a non-lever: cache residency and eviction (LRU is Belady-optimal, a document-reuse mirage), admission control (deferring cold prefills is self-defeating), host↔device transfer (non-binding), the hybrid model's recurrent-state pool (never the binding capacity), and even a chunk-level budget-reservation mechanism we designed and GPU-tested (a bounded negative — it helps below the knee but hurts the goodput-setting rate; companion paper). The one lever is prefill scheduling, and only at whole-request granularity. We diagnose why: below the saturation knee the p99 is head-of-line blocking — small conversational turns queued behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents (fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s, yet the p99 is 6–11 s; the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-doc chunks) — and above the knee the GPU is compute-bound (raw throughput saturates at ~4.7 req/s, measured at λ=10; 99% of forward steps are already prefill). Serving the small turns first (SRPF, a textbook policy) turns stock's stable λ=5 failure (17–24 s, n=5) into a pass, lifting goodput@SLO from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s, λ=3-bound) to 4.0 req/s (the λ=5 rate, full contract sweep) — purely by reordering, with cache contents untouched. Goodput is bounded not by raw compute (throughput climbs to ~4.7 at λ=10) but by the head-of-line tail crossing the SLO between λ=5 (7.8 s) and λ=7 (10.3 s). Finally we give a predictive criterion for when caching would matter: a reuse-stack-distance-vs-cache-horizon phase boundary, validated on constructed corpora, that this workload class sits far inside. The contribution is the map, the head-of-line diagnosis, and the phase-boundary criterion — not SRPF, which is textbook and which we use only to confirm where the lever is. Practitioners tuning KV-cache policy for goodput on such workloads are optimizing the wrong axis.

1 Introduction

A large body of systems work makes KV-cache management the centerpiece of LLM serving efficiency: tiered offloading (HiCache, LMCache, AttentionStore), balanced batching (Strata), prefix-aware routing and retention. For long-document conversational workloads — where each conversation embeds a big document once and then asks several questions about it — the intuition is especially strong: the document is re-used across turns and even across conversations, so a good cache should be decisive.

We test that intuition exhaustively against a single fixed metric and find it wrong. On a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier KV cache, goodput@SLO is set by how the heterogeneous prefill work is scheduled and by a hard compute ceiling — not by any KV-cache data-movement or retention decision. Three of the eight axes (caching-residency, admission, and chunk-level scheduling) are treated in depth in companion papers; this paper's own contributions are the unifying framework that places all eight axes on one metric (including three axes bounded here for the first time — transfer, the recurrent-state pool, and the prefill/decode split), the four-way head-of-line diagnosis that explains why scheduling is the lever (and why only at whole-request granularity — the chunk-level companion shows finer granularity backfires), and the parameter-free lever-selection procedure with its multi-corpus phase boundary. It is a map-and-mechanism-of-the-map paper, not a restatement of the axis studies. Our contributions:

  • A complete design-space map (§3). We characterize every KV-cache/serving axis against goodput@SLO and show each data-movement/retention axis is a bounded non-lever: residency/eviction, admission, transfer, the recurrent-state pool, and chunk-level scheduling (a reservation mechanism we built and GPU-tested — a bounded negative). Three of these are developed in depth in companion papers; here we place them in one framework with the newly-bounded axes.
  • The head-of-line diagnosis (§4). The λ=3 goodput coin-flip is not a per-request prefill floor or irreducible variance; it is head-of-line blocking behind a few large cold-document prefills — established four independent ways (arithmetic, direct trace, calibrated simulation, and a GPU A/B). This is the organizing insight that tells you the lever is scheduling.
  • The scheduling lever, measured (§5). Shortest-prefill-first (SRPF, textbook) moves goodput@SLO from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s) to 4.0 req/s (the λ=5 rate; full contract sweep), purely by reordering — our own GPU sweep. We are explicit that SRPF is not our contribution; the contribution is knowing, and proving, that scheduling is where the lever is and caching is not.
  • A predictive phase-boundary criterion (§6). Caching has online goodput headroom iff a corpus re-uses documents at a stack-distance beyond the cache horizon H = CAP/mean-doc; we validate the boundary on constructed corpora, and compose it with the compute and head-of-line bounds into a parameter-free lever-selection procedure (§6.1) that names the goodput lever from workload shape alone (here: scheduling), generalizing the result beyond this single workload. (The procedure's ceiling magnitude is a self-consistency check, not an independent prediction — we say so in §6.1.)

2 Setup and method

System. sglang v0.31 HiCache serving Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8 (48 layers = 36 GatedDeltaNet + 12 full-attention, 256-expert MoE), TP=8 on one 8×H100 node. KV is two-tier: L1 GPU HBM (~2.4M tokens) + L2 768 GB host DRAM (~10.7M tokens total); no disk tier. The active cache is UnifiedRadixCache; content-keyed, so an identical document re-used by another conversation is a hit whenever resident.

Fixed evaluation (never modified). A Poisson open-loop rate sweep λ∈{3,5,7,10} over 1553 multiturn conversations (7163 turns) from a Mooncake-style long-document mix, full decode, max-concurrency 256. Headline metric: goodput@SLO = the max sustained req/s with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s. Honest controls (peak tok/s, peak req/s) are decode-bound and ~flat by construction.

Method — diagnosing a variance-dominated tail metric. goodput@SLO is a tail metric and, at λ=3, a metastable coin-flip: identical stock configurations straddle the SLO across runs. Single-run A/Bs are therefore void; we compare distributions on the same physical node (cross-node p99 variance is ±45%). To attribute the coin-flip we triangulate: an assumption-light arithmetic bound, a direct trace of the real run, a calibrated queueing simulation, and a same-node GPU A/B. Any single method can mislead (our first offline oracle over-serialized the prefill and pointed the wrong way); the diagnosis is the agreement of all four.

4 Why scheduling is the lever: head-of-line blocking

Per-turn prefill work is extremely bimodal: 70% of turns are near-free prefix reuse (≤26 tokens), while the top 5% of turns (large first-document prefills) carry 50% of all prefill work (p99 40.6k tokens, max 191k). The goodput coin-flip lives entirely in how these collide.

  • Arithmetic. At the batched ~39k tok/s, the largest document is ~5 s and fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s; yet the measured λ=3 p99 is 6–11 s. The tail therefore cannot be a per-request prefill floor — it is queue-wait.
  • Direct trace (two victims, one mechanism). In the stock run's log, the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document prefill chunks (#new-token ≥ 6144, #cached-token = 0: mean depth 11.9, p90 33, max 183) than during all other prefill steps (mean 1.2, p90 0) — small turns pile up behind the cold grind (analysis/hol_trace.py). The same monopolization stalls the decode side: while a big cold doc grinds its back-to-back chunks the GPU runs no decode step, so running requests wait 24.5 s between tokens on average (p90 37 s, max 182 s) vs 1.8 s otherwise — a 13.6× decode stall (analysis/hol_decode.py). One mechanism, two victims: the metric-visible TTFT tail (waiting turns) and the metric-invisible E2E/TPOT tail (running decodes) — which is why p99 E2E is minutes (§8) even where TTFT passes.
  • Simulation. A per-step 6144-token queueing model calibrated to the coin-flip (stock λ=3 p99 mean 7.5 s, straddling the SLO) shows shortest-prefill-first collapses it to ~4 s, coin-flip removed (analysis/hol_sim.py); round-robin interleaving does not. The simulator is calibrated and used only at λ=3 (the coin-flip regime); it over-serializes above the knee, so no λ≥5 claim rests on it (an earlier trace oracle that over-serialized pointed the wrong way — §2 — a mistake this scope limit avoids).
  • GPU A/B. Same-node SRPF runs (λ=3 n=3) land p99 at 5.9, 6.0, 7.4 s — all below the SLO (3/3), vs stock's 3/5 coin-flip — matching the non-preemptive simulation.

The mechanism is that a chunked large cold document runs its ceil(work/6144) chunks back-to-back (the scheduler continues the in-progress chunked request every step without yielding), monopolizing prefill while small turns wait. Above the knee the same work simply saturates the GPU: full token usage holds at 0.96–0.98 and 99% of forward steps are prefill, so the system is compute- and KV-memory-bound at its work-conservation ceiling. Two regimes, one physical wall.

The λ=7,10 failures come from the single full contract sweep (v-srpf-full); the decisive λ=3,5 rows are additionally backed by a clean same-node A/B (both on node 0-3, so no cross-node variance confound in the comparison that sets the goodput). Scope of evidence, stated plainly: at λ=3,5 SRPF is n=3 each, and all six runs clear the 8 s SLO: λ=3 {5.9, 6.0, 7.4} s and λ=5 {5.85, 6.58, 7.46} s, overall range 5.85–7.46 s. SRPF has real run-to-run spread (its worst run, 7.46 s, clears the SLO by only 0.5 s — reported honestly, goodput is "passes," not "comfortably"), but every run passes, unlike stock which coin-flips at λ=3 (3/5) and fails λ=5 stably (n=5, all 17–24 s; the same-node control drew 22.7 s). The λ=5 win is robust as a clean separation: every SRPF λ=5 run (5.85–7.46 s) is 2–4× below every stock λ=5 run (17–24 s), no overlap — a gap a coin-flip cannot manufacture, and statistically significant (Fisher exact, SRPF 3/3 vs stock 0/5, one-tailed p=0.018; both rates pooled 6/6 vs 3/10, p=0.011; analysis/goodput_stats.py). Honest scoping of this p-value: the 5 stock λ=5 runs pool the same-node control (node 0-3) with 4 runs from a second node (ondem-3), so the p=0.018 is not purely same-node (the strictly same-node comparison, SRPF 3/3 vs the single stock control 0/1, is only p=0.25 — too few stock runs to reach significance alone). We rely on it only because stock λ=5 fails stably on both nodes (all 17–24 s, ≤1.08× spread) — so the 2–4× SRPF gap is not a cross-node artifact — and the same-node SRPF-vs-control pair (5.85–7.46 vs 22.7 s) shows the identical clean separation; the pooled p-value quantifies a direction that is already unambiguous same-node. At λ=3 SRPF converts stock's 3/5 coin-flip into 3/3 — here the pass-counts alone are not separable (p=0.36, stock passes λ=3 often), so the λ=3 effect is distributional (SRPF's tighter, lower tail 5.9–7.4 s vs stock's 6.3–11.8 s) rather than a pass-rate result; the significant, decisive win is at λ=5. The full contract sweep (λ{3,5,7,10}, runs/v-srpf-full) resolves the goodput exactly: SRPF passes λ=3 (6.5 s) and λ=5 (7.8 s) but fails λ=7 (10.3 s), so goodput@SLO = 4.0 req/s (the λ=5 rate), up from the stock coin-flip (≤3). Notably, SRPF's raw throughput keeps climbing past λ=5 — 4.48 req/s at λ=7, 4.72 at λ=10 — while its p99 TTFT crosses the SLO; so goodput is bounded by the head-of-line tail crossing 8 s (between λ=5 and λ=7), not by a raw-compute wall (the throughput ceiling, ~4.7, sits above the goodput). This sharpens §4: at higher load the head-of-line tail — not saturation — is what caps goodput. The gain comes with cache contents and hit-rate unchanged (≈0.66 both): the lever is scheduling — reordering prefills, not caching or moving KV — is what moves the metric.

The mechanism is textbook; the finding is where it belongs. SRPF/SJF is classical and, dropped onto a pluggable interface, is explicitly not a research contribution. Our contribution is the map and the diagnosis that make SRPF the right tool here — and that rule out the KV-cache alternatives everyone reaches for first. We also built a genuinely new mechanism, a queue-adaptive chunk-budget "fast-lane" that reserves part of each prefill chunk for waiting small turns without reordering; in simulation it beats stock (4/5 vs 3/5 at λ=3) but is strictly dominated by plain SRPF (5/5), because it is a partial SRPF that converges to SRPF only as the reserve grows to the whole budget. We report it as a bounded negative — the efficient realization of head-of-line relief is reordering, not budget-partitioning.

The result is robust to the SLO threshold, not an artifact of 8 s. Sweeping the threshold T over the measured curves (analysis/slo_sensitivity.py), scheduling is the lever — SRPF's achieved goodput strictly exceeds stock's — for T across the entire practical range ≈[6, 41] s (+38% from the 8 s SLO out to ~22 s, then narrowing to ~1% by 34 s as stock's slow rates begin to clear). Only below ~6 s does the gap close because even SRPF's λ=3 (5.9 s) hits a request's own prefill floor — a policy-invariant compute limit that no KV-cache or scheduling decision can cross — and only above ~41 s does it close because stock's slow rates finally clear the loose SLO and both converge to the ~4.7 req/s compute ceiling. The fixed 8 s SLO sits squarely in the window where scheduling is decisive, so the conclusion does not hinge on the exact threshold.

6 When caching would matter: a phase-boundary criterion

The caching non-lever is not a coincidence of one trace; it follows a sharp, measurable boundary — the classic LRU stack-distance property (Mattson et al. 1970; Denning's working-set theory), applied here at document granularity. An LRU document cache captures a re-use exactly when its stack-distance — the number of distinct documents touched since the document was last used — is below the cache horizon H = CAP / mean-doc-size (in documents). Online caching headroom is exactly the fraction of re-uses with stack-distance > H. We validate this on constructed corpora (analysis/phase_boundary.py): the avoidable-work gap between LRU and the clairvoyant Belady optimum is a step function at stack-distance = H.

The fixed workload sits deep in the mirage regime: across its 1015 document-bearing conversations the reuse stack-distance is p50 = 10, p90 = 197, max = 518 against H ≈ 516, so 127 of 128 document re-uses (99.2%) are LRU-captured and headroom is ~0.8% (one re-use at distance 518, just past H). A corpus escapes the mirage only if its hot documents recur at stack-distance beyond the horizon — a large working set re-used long-range — which long-document QA serving structurally is not (its one hot document recurs at a median gap of six conversations, and only ~10% of conversations re-use any document). Applying the criterion to four real long-document corpora (the eval mix, LEval, and two LooGLE variants; analysis/phase_boundary_realcorpora.py) puts all four in the mirage regime (≤0.8% headroom) — though we note two of the four (the LooGLE variants) reach it trivially (all-singleton, no cross-record reuse to cache), so the non-trivial generality rests on the eval mix (0.8%) and LEval (0.0%), both of which have reuse yet still fall below the horizon. The mirage is thus a property of the workload class, predicted from corpus statistics, not an artifact of one trace (companion paper, §3.4). Practitioner corollary: measure your corpus's reuse stack-distance against H = CAP/mean-doc before investing in a smarter retention policy; below the horizon, no policy beats LRU and the goodput lever is scheduling.

6.1 A predictive goodput-lever model (a roofline for goodput@SLO)

The map, the diagnosis, and the phase boundary compose into a single decision procedure that, from workload and hardware parameters alone, predicts goodput and names the lever (analysis/goodput_model.py). From the per-turn cold-adjusted work distribution W (trace at the LRU cache), the effective cold-prefill throughput C_eff, the batched throughput C_batch, the cache horizon H, and the SLO T:

  • Compute ceiling g_ceil = C_eff / E[W] — the work-conservation cap no policy beats.
  • Per-request floor f = P99(W) / C_batch — a request's own prefill; policy-invariant.
  • Caching headroom h = fraction of re-uses at stack-distance > H (§6).

The lever decision uses no fitted/circular constant (only the measured C_batch, the SLO, and workload shape — not the back-solved C_eff) = compute if f > T (even the best schedule misses the SLO); else caching if h is large (removable evicted-reuse); else, when work is bimodal and f ≪ T, scheduling (the p99 is head-of-line queue-wait, reducible by serving small turns first). On our workload it reads f = 1.04 s ≪ 8 s, h = 0.2%, top-5%-of-turns carry 50% of work → lever = scheduling — and this naming uses none of the fitted throughput constants, only the measured C_batch (for f), the SLO, and workload shape. It is the load-bearing output of the model, and it matches what we measured (SRPF moves goodput; caching/admission do not).

The magnitude side is weaker and we flag it honestly. The model ceiling g_ceil = C_eff / E[W] is a self-consistency check, not an independent prediction: with C_eff ≈ 15 k tok/s (effective cold-prefill throughput, backed out from the saturated run) and E[W] ≈ 3594 tok (mean cache-adjusted work), it gives g_ceil ≈ 4.2 req/s — but since C_eff is calibrated to the saturation it then reproduces, this is a consistency check, not a prediction (analysis/goodput_model.py, bound.py). What is not circular is the directly measured raw ceiling: the full contract sweep has SRPF completing 4.72 req/s at λ=10 (runs/v-srpf-full). The model estimate (~4.2) and the direct measurement (4.72) agree to within a sensitivity band ~4.2–4.7; the load-bearing ceiling is the direct measurement, and the model only shows the measured saturation is consistent with work-conservation, not a policy gap. The decomposition then establishes that goodput@SLO (4.0) sits below that raw ceiling — so on this metric the binding constraint is the head-of-line tail crossing the SLO (§4), not raw compute. The lever-selection above (scheduling) targets exactly that binding constraint.

The value of the procedure is qualitative and general: flip its inputs and it names a different lever for a different regime — reuse beyond the horizon (h large) → caching; a lone document with P99(W)/C_batch > T → compute; near-uniform small work → no head-of-line, so neither caching nor scheduling helps. The goodput lever is thus a function of (work distribution, reuse locality, throughput, SLO) — not a fixed property of KV-cache serving — which is why the KV-cache axis is inert here yet could be decisive elsewhere.

7 Related work

HiCache / LMCache / AttentionStore optimize KV residency and host↔device movement; we show that for this workload class residency is Belady-saturated and movement is non-binding, so these help hit-rate and memory pressure but not goodput@SLO. Strata targets balanced batching; Mooncake sheds load under overload — sound when shed work is dispensable, but here the "expensive" cold prefills are the SLO-defining tail. SRPF/SJF and prefill-priority scheduling are classical; our role is not to propose them but to prove, by eliminating every KV-cache alternative and diagnosing the head-of-line mechanism, that prefill scheduling is the goodput lever for long-document serving — and to bound when caching would instead matter. Cache-replacement theory (Belady, LRU stack-distance) supplies the phase-boundary criterion.

8 Limitations

  • Results are for one model family (122B hybrid Mamba-attention), one two-tier KV budget, and one long-document multiturn corpus. The phase-boundary criterion (§6) is our generality argument, but a direct cross-workload GPU sweep is out of scope of the fixed protocol.
  • goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at λ=3; all comparisons are same-node and distributional. SRPF is n=3 at both rates; all six runs clear the SLO: λ=3 {5.9, 6.0, 7.4} s, λ=5 {5.85, 6.58, 7.46} s, overall range 5.85–7.46 s. SRPF has genuine run-to-run spread (worst run 7.46 s, only 0.5 s under the SLO — reported honestly), but every run passes, unlike stock (λ=3 coin-flip 3/5; λ=5 stable-fail 0/5, 17–24 s). The λ=5 win is a clean separation (every SRPF run 2–4× below every stock run, no overlap); the λ=3 win is 3/3 vs 3/5. We report SRPF's own spread honestly rather than cherry-picking its best run.
  • The compute ceiling could in principle move compute-side (context-parallel prefill, quantization), but prefill context-parallelism is unavailable for this hybrid model in-engine and quantization is lossy / contract-frozen; we treat the ceiling as fixed.
  • We are scoped to the fixed headline metric, goodput@SLO on p99 TTFT; we do not optimize E2E latency or TPOT. These are catastrophic (p99 E2E is minutes) for a mechanistic reason we identify in §4: the same big-cold-doc prefill monopolization that causes the TTFT head-of-line also stalls running decodes 13.6× (24.5 s vs 1.8 s between tokens) — a decode-side head-of-line the TTFT metric does not see. Importantly, SRPF's TTFT win is not bought at their expense: from the same runs SRPF's p99 E2E improves over stock (λ=3 322 s vs 438 s) and its p99 TPOT is within noise — reordering does not worsen decode. But reordering cannot fix the decode stall either (a big cold prefill is serialized whichever request goes first); a full fix needs the compute-side lever (parallelizing the single big prefill, i.e. context-parallel — §5.1), motivating that as the highest-value future work.
