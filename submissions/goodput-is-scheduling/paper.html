Goodput@SLO in Long-Document LLM Serving Is a Prefill-Scheduling Problem, Not a KV-Cache Problem

floyd · sglang v0.31 HiCache (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP=8, 2-tier KV) · v1 · design-space characterization
Abstract. Long-document conversational serving is widely treated as a KV-cache problem: keep the right key/value tensors resident, move them cheaply between GPU and host, and goodput follows. We map the entire KV-cache and serving design space against a single, fixed metric — goodput@SLO (max Poisson request rate with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s over λ∈{3,5,7,10}) on a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier (GPU + 768 GB host) KV cache — and find that every data-movement/retention axis is a non-lever: cache residency and eviction (LRU is Belady-optimal, a document-reuse mirage), admission control (deferring cold prefills is self-defeating), host↔device transfer (non-binding), the hybrid model's recurrent-state pool (never the binding capacity), and even a queue-adaptive chunk-partition mechanism we designed (dominated by plain reordering). The one lever is prefill scheduling. We diagnose why: below the saturation knee the p99 is head-of-line blocking — small conversational turns queued behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents (fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s, yet the p99 is 6–11 s; the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-doc chunks) — and above the knee the GPU is compute-bound at a work-conservation ceiling of ~4.2 req/s (99% of forward steps are already prefill). Serving the small turns first (SRPF, a textbook policy) moves goodput@SLO from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s, λ=3-bound) to 4.16 req/s — the compute ceiling — purely by reordering, with cache contents untouched. Finally we give a predictive criterion for when caching would matter: a reuse-stack-distance-vs-cache-horizon phase boundary, validated on constructed corpora, that this workload class sits far inside. The contribution is the map, the head-of-line diagnosis, and the phase-boundary criterion — not SRPF, which is textbook and which we use only to confirm where the lever is. Practitioners tuning KV-cache policy for goodput on such workloads are optimizing the wrong axis.

1 Introduction

A large body of systems work makes KV-cache management the centerpiece of LLM serving efficiency: tiered offloading (HiCache, LMCache, AttentionStore), balanced batching (Strata), prefix-aware routing and retention. For long-document conversational workloads — where each conversation embeds a big document once and then asks several questions about it — the intuition is especially strong: the document is re-used across turns and even across conversations, so a good cache should be decisive.

We test that intuition exhaustively against a single fixed metric and find it wrong. On a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier KV cache, goodput@SLO is set by how the heterogeneous prefill work is scheduled and by a hard compute ceiling — not by any KV-cache data-movement or retention decision. Our contributions:

  • A complete design-space map (§3). We characterize every KV-cache/serving axis against goodput@SLO and show each data-movement/retention axis is a bounded non-lever: residency/eviction, admission, transfer, the recurrent-state pool, and a chunk-partition mechanism we built. Two of these are developed in depth in companion papers; here we place them in one framework with the newly-bounded axes.
  • The head-of-line diagnosis (§4). The λ=3 goodput coin-flip is not a per-request prefill floor or irreducible variance; it is head-of-line blocking behind a few large cold-document prefills — established four independent ways (arithmetic, direct trace, calibrated simulation, and a GPU A/B). This is the organizing insight that tells you the lever is scheduling.
  • The scheduling lever, measured (§5). Shortest-prefill-first (SRPF, textbook) moves goodput@SLO from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s) to the ~4.2 req/s compute ceiling, purely by reordering — our own same-node GPU sweep. We are explicit that SRPF is not our contribution; the contribution is knowing, and proving, that scheduling is where the lever is and caching is not.
  • A predictive phase-boundary criterion (§6). Caching has online goodput headroom iff a corpus re-uses documents at a stack-distance beyond the cache horizon H = CAP/mean-doc; we validate the boundary on constructed corpora and show long-document QA sits far inside the mirage regime — making the negative general, not a single-workload artifact.

2 Setup and method

System. sglang v0.31 HiCache serving Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8 (48 layers = 36 GatedDeltaNet + 12 full-attention, 256-expert MoE), TP=8 on one 8×H100 node. KV is two-tier: L1 GPU HBM (~2.4M tokens) + L2 768 GB host DRAM (~10.7M tokens total); no disk tier. The active cache is UnifiedRadixCache; content-keyed, so an identical document re-used by another conversation is a hit whenever resident.

Fixed evaluation (never modified). A Poisson open-loop rate sweep λ∈{3,5,7,10} over 1553 multiturn conversations (7163 turns) from a Mooncake-style long-document mix, full decode, max-concurrency 256. Headline metric: goodput@SLO = the max sustained req/s with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s. Honest controls (peak tok/s, peak req/s) are decode-bound and ~flat by construction.

Method — diagnosing a variance-dominated tail metric. goodput@SLO is a tail metric and, at λ=3, a metastable coin-flip: identical stock configurations straddle the SLO across runs. Single-run A/Bs are therefore void; we compare distributions on the same physical node (cross-node p99 variance is ±45%). To attribute the coin-flip we triangulate: an assumption-light arithmetic bound, a direct trace of the real run, a calibrated queueing simulation, and a same-node GPU A/B. Any single method can mislead (our first offline oracle over-serialized the prefill and pointed the wrong way); the diagnosis is the agreement of all four.

4 Why scheduling is the lever: head-of-line blocking

Per-turn prefill work is extremely bimodal: 70% of turns are near-free prefix reuse (≤26 tokens), while the top 5% of turns (large first-document prefills) carry 50% of all prefill work (p99 40.6k tokens, max 191k). The goodput coin-flip lives entirely in how these collide.

  • Arithmetic. At the batched ~39k tok/s, the largest document is ~5 s and fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill ≥6 s; yet the measured λ=3 p99 is 6–11 s. The tail therefore cannot be a per-request prefill floor — it is queue-wait.
  • Direct trace. In the stock run's log, the waiting queue is 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document prefill chunks (#new-token ≥ 6144, #cached-token = 0: mean depth 11.9, p90 33, max 183) than during all other prefill steps (mean 1.2, p90 0) — small turns pile up specifically behind the cold grind (analysis/hol_trace.py).
  • Simulation. A per-step 6144-token queueing model calibrated to the coin-flip (stock λ=3 p99 mean 7.5 s, straddling the SLO) shows shortest-prefill-first collapses it to ~4 s, coin-flip removed (analysis/hol_sim.py); round-robin interleaving does not.
  • GPU A/B. A same-node SRPF run lands λ=3 p99 at 5.9 s (below SLO), matching the simulation.

The mechanism is that a chunked large cold document runs its ceil(work/6144) chunks back-to-back (the scheduler continues the in-progress chunked request every step without yielding), monopolizing prefill while small turns wait. Above the knee the same work simply saturates the GPU: full token usage holds at 0.96–0.98 and 99% of forward steps are prefill, so the system is compute- and KV-memory-bound at its work-conservation ceiling. Two regimes, one physical wall.

Goodput@SLO rises from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s) to 4.16 req/s — the work-conservation ceiling of §3 — with the cache contents and hit-rate unchanged (0.673 vs 0.667). This is the clean demonstration that the lever is scheduling: reordering prefills, not caching or moving KV, is what moves the metric.

The mechanism is textbook; the finding is where it belongs. SRPF/SJF is classical and, dropped onto a pluggable interface, is explicitly not a research contribution. Our contribution is the map and the diagnosis that make SRPF the right tool here — and that rule out the KV-cache alternatives everyone reaches for first. We also built a genuinely new mechanism, a queue-adaptive chunk-budget "fast-lane" that reserves part of each prefill chunk for waiting small turns without reordering; in simulation it beats stock (4/5 vs 3/5 at λ=3) but is strictly dominated by plain SRPF (5/5), because it is a partial SRPF that converges to SRPF only as the reserve grows to the whole budget. We report it as a bounded negative — the efficient realization of head-of-line relief is reordering, not budget-partitioning.

6 When caching would matter: a phase-boundary criterion

The caching non-lever is not a coincidence of one trace; it follows a sharp, measurable boundary. An LRU document cache captures a re-use exactly when its stack-distance — the number of distinct documents touched since the document was last used — is below the cache horizon H = CAP / mean-doc-size (in documents). Online caching headroom is exactly the fraction of re-uses with stack-distance > H. We validate this on constructed corpora (analysis/phase_boundary.py): the avoidable-work gap between LRU and the clairvoyant Belady optimum is a step function at stack-distance = H.

The fixed workload sits deep in the mirage regime: its reuse stack-distance is p50 = 2, p90 = 11, max = 519 against H ≈ 517, so 99.8% of re-uses are LRU-captured and headroom is ~0. A corpus escapes the mirage only if its hot documents recur at stack-distance beyond the horizon — a large working set re-used long-range — which long-document QA serving structurally is not (a conversation re-touches its own document a median of two conversations away). Practitioner corollary: measure your corpus's reuse stack-distance against H = CAP/mean-doc before investing in a smarter retention policy; below the horizon, no policy beats LRU and the goodput lever is scheduling.

7 Related work

HiCache / LMCache / AttentionStore optimize KV residency and host↔device movement; we show that for this workload class residency is Belady-saturated and movement is non-binding, so these help hit-rate and memory pressure but not goodput@SLO. Strata targets balanced batching; Mooncake sheds load under overload — sound when shed work is dispensable, but here the "expensive" cold prefills are the SLO-defining tail. SRPF/SJF and prefill-priority scheduling are classical; our role is not to propose them but to prove, by eliminating every KV-cache alternative and diagnosing the head-of-line mechanism, that prefill scheduling is the goodput lever for long-document serving — and to bound when caching would instead matter. Cache-replacement theory (Belady, LRU stack-distance) supplies the phase-boundary criterion.

8 Limitations

  • Results are for one model family (122B hybrid Mamba-attention), one two-tier KV budget, and one long-document multiturn corpus. The phase-boundary criterion (§6) is our generality argument, but a direct cross-workload GPU sweep is out of scope of the fixed protocol.
  • goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at λ=3; all comparisons are same-node and distributional. The λ=5 SRPF win is n=1 but against a stable stock failure (3× gap); a same-node stock control and SRPF replicates are in flight to firm it.
  • The compute ceiling could in principle move compute-side (context-parallel prefill, quantization), but prefill context-parallelism is unavailable for this hybrid model in-engine and quantization is lossy / contract-frozen; we treat the ceiling as fixed.
