The KV Movement Tier Is Not a Goodput Lever: L2→L1 load-back is non-binding under load in long-document LLM serving

floyd · sglang v0.31 HiCache (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP=8, 2-tier KV) · v1 · movement-axis characterization + bounded negative
Abstract. HiCache-style serving tiers the KV cache across GPU HBM (L1) and host DRAM (L2), and the natural way to make tiering pay is to optimize the movement between tiers — prefetch reused KV, overlap host↔device transfer under compute, change the page layout to move less, or schedule conversations to stay co-resident. We show that for long-document multiturn serving on the fixed goodput@SLO protocol (max Poisson request rate with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s), the L2→L1 movement is a non-lever. From the engine's own load-back histogram: the run performs a large and growing volume of load-backs — 25,008 → 92,600 load operations moving 0.37→1.36 billion tokens as the rate climbs from λ=3 to λ=10 — yet every load-back completes in under 30 ms (≥99.4% under 10 ms; mean 1.3–1.5 ms), and the mean even drops under load. Against the queueing-dominated TTFT p99 (17–24 s at λ≥5) a load-back is <0.2% of the request's time and never the bottleneck. The reason is a bandwidth fact: HBM↔DRAM transfer with a paged, direct-IO layout runs at ≈10 M tokens/s, so even a whole reused document's KV loads in a few milliseconds — while the binding cost is the cold-document prefill compute, which no movement mechanism touches. Because the movement is already cheap, the entire movement-optimization family (prefetch, transfer/compute overlap, layout/paging, conversation co-residency) has no goodput headroom. We give the boundary that makes this predictive: movement optimization matters iff the hierarchy contains a tier slow enough that its transfer is a material fraction of TTFT. A fast two-tier (HBM+DRAM) hierarchy is on the non-binding side; the movement lever lives below L2 (an L3/SSD/disk tier), not within L1↔L2. Practitioners optimizing L1↔L2 movement for goodput on this workload class are, like those tuning cache retention (companion), optimizing the wrong axis. This is the movement-axis deep-dive of a companion design-space map that finds the sole goodput lever is prefill scheduling.

1 Introduction

Hierarchical KV caching — keeping hot key/value tensors in GPU HBM and spilling the rest to host DRAM (and, in some systems, to SSD) — is now standard in high-throughput LLM serving (HiCache, LMCache, AttentionStore). Its premise is that the working set exceeds HBM, so where KV lives and how it moves between tiers is a first-order serving concern. The natural engineering response is to optimize the movement: predict and prefetch KV that is about to be reused, overlap the host→device transfer under compute so its latency is hidden, change the page layout so less data moves or moves more cheaply, or schedule a conversation's turns to keep its KV co-resident and avoid reloading it. Each is a plausible mechanism; each is the kind of thing one builds first.

We ask a prior question: for the goodput@SLO metric on a realistic long-document multiturn workload, is the L1↔L2 movement even on the critical path? Using the engine's own instrumentation we find it is not. The system moves an enormous and load-growing volume of KV between tiers — over a billion tokens' worth at the highest rate — but each individual load-back is a few milliseconds, negligible against a TTFT that is seconds long and set by queueing behind cold-document prefills. The movement tier is doing a lot of work cheaply; making it do that work faster or sooner cannot move a metric it does not bind.

Contributions.

  • A measured non-lever (§2, §4). The L2→L1 load-back is non-binding and scales: across λ∈{3,5,7,10} the load-back count and token volume grow 3.7× while per-operation latency stays <30 ms (mean 1.3–1.5 ms) — <0.2% of the queueing-dominated TTFT.
  • The whole movement-optimization family is bounded (§3). Prefetch, transfer/compute overlap, layout/paging, and conversation co-residency all aim to reduce or hide a cost that is already negligible; none has goodput headroom on this workload/hierarchy. We verify the movement path is on the request's pre-prefill critical path (so this is not "it is already hidden") — it is simply cheap.
  • A predictive boundary (§5). Movement optimization matters iff the tiering includes a tier whose transfer is a material fraction of TTFT. A fast HBM+DRAM two-tier hierarchy (≈10 M tok/s) is non-binding; a slow L3 (SSD/disk, orders of magnitude slower) is where the movement lever lives. This locates the movement lever below L2, not within L1↔L2.
  • Placement in the design space. This is the movement-axis companion to a design-space map whose verdict is that the goodput lever is prefill scheduling, not any KV data-movement/retention axis. It complements the caching-retention non-lever (the reuse "mirage") with the movement non-lever: not only is there little re-prefill to avoid, moving the cached KV is itself free.

2 Motivation: a large, load-growing movement volume — that is individually cheap

The fixed protocol serves 1553 multiturn conversations (7163 turns) from a Mooncake-style long-document mix at Poisson rates λ∈{3,5,7,10}, on a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier KV cache: L1 = GPU HBM, L2 = 768 GB host DRAM (--hicache-size 96, page_first_direct layout, direct IO backend), no L3. Turn-0 of each conversation embeds a whole document (a large cold prefill); later turns reuse it. Under concurrency, HBM (L1) fills with running requests' KV, so a conversation's document KV is frequently resident only in L2 when its next turn arrives — and must be loaded back L2→L1 before that turn can prefill.

This load-back happens constantly. The scheduler's init_load_back path moves the matched host-resident prefix to device (via the HiCache controller's load queue) and the request waits for the load to complete before its prefill runs — so the load-back is on the request's pre-prefill critical path. One would therefore expect movement to matter, especially at high load where the volume explodes:

Individually cheap, and cheaper under load. Every load-back is <30 ms; ≥99.4% are <10 ms; the mean falls (1.54→1.31 ms) as the volume triples — the movement subsystem is nowhere near saturation. Against a TTFT p99 of seconds, a ≤30 ms load-back is <0.2% of the request's time.

The paradox resolves in one number: mean tokens/load-back = 6.95e8 / 47,944 ≈ 14.5 K tokens, at mean 1.46 ms ⇒ an effective ≈10 M tokens/s host→device KV bandwidth (paged, direct IO). At that rate even a 190 K-token maximum document's KV loads in ≈19 ms. The movement is high-volume and cheap; there is simply no latency here worth hiding.

3 The movement-optimization family, and why each member is bounded

Every mechanism one would build to make the movement tier pay targets a cost that §2 shows is already negligible. We take them in turn (all would be lossless; none moves goodput here):

MechanismWhat it targetsWhy it has no goodput headroom here
Reuse-aware prefetch (predict the next turn, pre-load its KV during think-time) The L2→L1 load latency on the reused turn's critical path That latency is ≤30 ms (mean 1.46 ms). Prefetching it away saves <0.2% of TTFT. The tail is queueing behind cold prefills, which prefetch does not touch.
Transfer/compute overlap (async-load KV under other requests' compute) Hiding the load-back behind compute so it is off the critical path Same ≤30 ms is being hidden; and the load subsystem is unsaturated even at λ=10 (mean drops under load), so there is no queueing-for-transfer to overlap away either.
Layout/paging for movement cost (pack KV so less/cheaper data moves) The per-token movement cost Movement is already ≈10 M tok/s with the stock page_first_direct layout. Halving the per-token cost halves a ≤30 ms operation — still <0.1% of TTFT.
Conversation co-residency (schedule/pin a conversation's KV to avoid reload) The reload (load-back) of an evicted conversation prefix Avoiding a ≤30 ms reload saves ≤30 ms. (And retention itself is a non-lever — single-pass LRU is Belady-optimal for this corpus; companion.)

The common structure: all four reduce or hide the movement of reused KV, but the reused KV is cheap to move, and the goodput-binding cost is the compute to prefill the cold documents on their first sight (≈75% of all prefill work is first-ever, cache-independent). Movement mechanisms cannot reduce compute.

4 Evaluation: the load-back is on the critical path, and still non-binding

We rule out the trivial explanation "movement is already hidden, so of course it does not appear." It is not hidden: init_load_back initiates the host→device load and the request is gated on its completion (loading_check resolves the acked load before the request is admitted to prefill), so the load latency is on the request's own pre-prefill timeline. It is non-binding because it is small, not because it is overlapped. The histogram (§2) is the direct evidence: across every rate, the entire distribution sits under 30 ms with a mean of 1.3–1.5 ms, and it does not degrade as the offered volume triples — the opposite of what a binding resource does under load. The load-back's contribution to the p99 TTFT (17–24 s at λ≥5) is bounded above by its maximum (<30 ms), i.e. <0.2%. No movement mechanism, however clever, can recover more than that 0.2% — and even that only if it eliminated load-back latency entirely and for free.

This mirrors, on the movement axis, the companion caching result on the retention axis: there we showed there is almost no avoidable re-prefill to capture (the reuse "mirage"); here we show that even the KV that is reused costs essentially nothing to move. Retention and movement — the two things a KV tiering system does — are both non-levers for goodput on this workload; the lever is scheduling the cold prefills.

5 When movement would matter: a slow-tier boundary

The non-lever result is not universal; it is predictable. A load-back is on the critical path with cost C_move ≈ (reused-prefix tokens) / (tier bandwidth), and it matters for goodput iff C_move is a material fraction of TTFT. Three quantities decide it: the reused-prefix size, the tier bandwidth, and the ambient TTFT. For this workload/hierarchy:

  • Tier bandwidth is high. HBM↔DRAM with a paged direct-IO layout is ≈10 M tok/s, so C_move is single-digit-to-tens of milliseconds even for the largest documents.
  • Ambient TTFT is large. Because the goodput bottleneck is cold-prefill compute, TTFT at the goodput-setting rate is seconds; a millisecond load-back is lost in it.

The boundary flips when a tier is slow. Add an L3 (SSD/NVMe/disk) at, say, 10–100 MB/s effective per rank instead of DRAM's GB/s, and C_move for a large document rises from tens of milliseconds to seconds — now a material fraction of TTFT, and the criterion predicts prefetch/overlap/layout would genuinely pay there (this is presumably why hierarchical caches offer a disk tier at all). The movement lever lives below L2. Within the L1↔L2 (HBM+DRAM) hierarchy of this protocol — which deliberately has no L3 — movement is on the non-binding side of the boundary, and the family of §3 mechanisms has no goodput headroom. This is the movement-axis analogue of the companion caching phase boundary (caching helps iff reuse recurs beyond the cache horizon): a single, checkable criterion that tells a practitioner whether the axis is worth optimizing before they build anything.

6 Related work

HiCache / LMCache / AttentionStore build the KV tiering and its movement machinery; our result is that, for this workload in a fast two-tier hierarchy, the movement they optimize is not the goodput bottleneck — our criterion (§5) predicts the payoff is below L2, at a disk tier, which is presumably why such a tier is offered at all. Marconi manages hybrid SSM–attention prefix caching; consistent with our companion's finding that the recurrent-state pool never binds, the only residency pressure is attention-KV, and moving it is cheap. Prefetching and transfer/compute overlap are classic memory-hierarchy techniques; our contribution is not a new one but the measured, bounded statement that on this axis, for this workload, there is nothing to hide, plus the slow-tier boundary that says when there would be. This paper is the movement-axis companion to a design-space map (goodput@SLO is a prefill-scheduling problem) and to the caching-retention non-lever.

7 Limitations

  • Two-tier (no L3) by protocol. The result is explicitly scoped to the fixed HBM+DRAM hierarchy. §5 predicts (and prior disk-backed results confirm) that an L3/disk tier moves movement to the binding side; we do not evaluate an L3 tier (it is off-protocol).
  • One workload class (122B hybrid, long-document multiturn). The boundary of §5 is our generality argument: the non-lever holds wherever tier bandwidth is high and ambient TTFT is compute-dominated.
  • Measurement, not ablation. We do not build and A/B a prefetch/overlap mechanism — the histogram already bounds the achievable gain above by <0.2% of TTFT, so an implementation could not beat that ceiling; building one to confirm a <0.2% ceiling is not a good use of the budget. The load-back path is engine-verified to be on the critical path (so the <0.2% is not an artifact of pre-existing overlap).
