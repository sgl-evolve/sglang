Pending-Aware KV Retention: Scheduler–Cache Co-Design for Multiturn LLM Serving

valiant · sglang KV-cache research (v0.31) · evaluated on the frozen 122B rate-sweep protocol (n=2 same-node)
Abstract. Hierarchical KV caches (HiCache-style L1 GPU + L2 host) reuse the growing prefix of a multiturn conversation to avoid re-prefilling it each turn. Under concurrent multiturn load a conversation's cached prefix can be evicted between its turns, forcing a full recompute of a document that may be tens of thousands of tokens — the most expensive event on the prefill path. A trace-driven analysis shows 42% of the stock cache's prefill work is exactly this evicted-continuation recompute, and an oracle (Bélády) policy would remove most of it. We ask how much of that gap is realizable by a server that cannot see the future. We propose pending-aware KV retention: the cache protects the L2 residency of a conversation's prefix precisely while that conversation has a request the scheduler is already holding in its queue — a guaranteed-imminent-reuse signal — and releases it at admission. This is a scheduler↔cache co-design, not a replacement policy or config flag. On the frozen 122B rate-sweep protocol it delivers a lossless, reliably-positive but modest and variance-affected win: the pin always engages and never hurts hit, but its magnitude ranges +0.4 to +2.8 pp at the operating rate across three same-node A/B nodes (mean ≈+1.8 pp; the +2.5 pp we first measured on two nodes was the high end — a third node gave +0.4 pp despite the pin engaging more), plus +2–6% sustained throughput at moderate load on the two nodes where we measured it (λ=5,7; converging to ~0 at saturation where GPU compute binds). The gain diminishes with load and is scheduler-independent (it appears under both FCFS and LPM). That the gain's size is itself variance-affected is consistent with this workload's central methodological finding (below). Two findings sharpen the picture. (1) Targeting is everything, and the target is narrow: the natural extension — retaining every recently-finished conversation for a horizon to also cover the client think-gap — does not help and mildly hurts. Not because continuation is unpredictable (it is 78% predictable from turn count), but because broad retention merely displaces a working set LRU already keeps well; the only exploitable failure of LRU is its eviction of the narrow, provably-at-risk set of continuations sitting in the scheduler's queue. (2) Retention, not scheduling, is the hit lever — a claim we tested directly. A single-tier simulation makes the eviction gap look scheduler-created (under FCFS, LRU trails Bélády by 7–23 pp; prefix-aware LPM closes it). We put this to a same-node A/B on the real system (re-running the eval under --schedule-policy lpm vs the default fcfs): on the real 2-tier cache hit is nearly scheduler-insensitive — LPM does not raise hit at any rate (Δ<0.5 pp) and it worsens the p99 tail by 41–65% — because the L2 host layer absorbs eviction order. So the sim's gap is a single-tier artifact, and switching schedulers is not a way to buy cache efficiency; pending-aware retention is. What transfers from the analysis is model-free: a recency-inversion (Spearman ρ=+0.81 on the trace) — among queued continuations the least-recently-used prefix is the soonest-reused, so LRU is anti-Bélády on exactly this set — which is why the narrow pending signal is the right target. The realized gain is small (~1–2 pp vs a ~+7 pp single-tier ceiling) for the very same reason the host tier absorbs eviction order. Underlying all of it is a structural point: a lossless KV cache reduces re-prefill of already-seen prefixes, so it moves the mean and the sustainable throughput but not the p99 tail, which is set by first-turn long-document prefills that are never reusable. Accordingly the protocol's headline goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at the SLO boundary (identical stock runs flip goodput 0↔3.0 across nodes) and is the wrong yardstick for a cache mechanism; hit-rate, mean TTFT, and moderate-load throughput — which all move robustly and losslessly — are the right ones.

1  Introduction

Modern LLM serving is dominated by multiturn and agentic workloads: each turn re-sends the accumulated conversation, so the server can skip re-prefilling the shared prefix if its KV is still cached. On long-context workloads the prefix is enormous (a document of tens of thousands of tokens) and the new work per turn is tiny (one short question), so a single missed prefix — a recompute of the whole document — is by far the most expensive event on that request's prefill path and a major, avoidable component of the prefill load (and mean time-to-first-token) under concurrency. (The p99 tail, we will see, is instead set by unavoidable first-turn document prefills — so a cache moves the mean/throughput, not the tail.)

HiCache tiers KV across L1 (GPU HBM) and L2 (host DRAM). When the working set exceeds L1+L2, the cache is genuinely over-subscribed and must evict. The prevailing view — and the explicit starting assumption of this protocol — is that eviction order is a solved problem: under in-order reuse LRU is within <0.1 pp of Bélády, so there is no headroom in "which victim to pick." We argue and demonstrate that this view misses the effect that actually matters under concurrency.

Contributions.

  • A trace-driven diagnosis: under concurrent multiturn load, 42% of the stock cache's prefill work is evicted-continuation recompute (prefixes that were cached and reused, then dropped under contention), and an oracle policy would remove most of it — a large gap the LRU≈Bélády intuition misses in this regime.
  • Pending-aware KV retention: couple L2 retention to the scheduler's pending-request set (a guaranteed-imminent-reuse signal). Realizable, non-oracle, capacity-bounded, lossless. It gives a modest, lossless, reliably-positive win: hit +0.4–2.8 pp (n=3 same-node, mean ≈+1.8; magnitude variance-affected, sign always non-negative) and +2–6% sustained throughput at moderate load (n=2 nodes).
  • A scheduler/tier analysis, tested on the real system (the deeper insight): a single-tier sim makes the eviction gap look scheduler-created (FCFS 7–23 pp, ≈0 under prefix-aware pull), but a same-node FCFS-vs-LPM A/B on the 122B system shows real hit is scheduler-insensitive (Δ<0.5 pp) while LPM worsens p99 by 41–65% — the 2-tier host absorbs eviction order, so retention, not scheduling, is the hit lever. What transfers is a model-free recency-inversion (ρ=+0.81: among queued continuations the LRU victim is the soonest-reused), which explains both why the narrow pending signal works and why broad recency-based retention (the post-completion ablation) does not. Reporting a directly-measured refutation of our own simulation's magnitude is part of the contribution.
  • A targeting ablation + a realizability characterization: extending retention to all recently-finished conversations does not beat LRU — not because continuation is unpredictable (78% predictable) but because it pins by recency, precisely the ≈0%-capture set. The realizable gain (~1–2 pp, variance-affected) is much smaller than the single-tier oracle ceiling (~+7 pp) because the 2-tier host layer absorbs most device evictions. The scheduler's pending set, not broad recency, is the only exploitable signal.
  • A measurement finding: goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at the SLO boundary on this workload (identical stock runs flip goodput 0↔3.0 across nodes); hit-rate/recompute is the robust metric.

2  Motivation (trace-driven)

All numbers below are computed directly from the frozen evaluation workload (mooncake_mix_v1.jsonl, the real-text Mooncake-style 1:1:1 mix of ShareGPT+LEval+LooGLE, 1553 conversations) with the model's own tokenizer.

2.1  The prefix is the workload

Each conversation is one long document (mean ≈ 11.8k tokens, p90 ≈ 28k, max ≈ 183k) followed by short follow-up questions (median ≈ 18 tokens); conversations average 4.6 turns (593 of 1553 are single-turn). The document prefix is reused across every turn of its conversation; there is no cross-conversation sharing. A cache that achieved perfect reuse would eliminate 80.9% of all prefill work (98.0M prompt tokens → 18.7M). Prefill is therefore the lever for throughput under load, and the value of the cache is enormous — but only to the extent it actually retains the prefixes that will be reused.

2.2  Continuation turns are load-/reuse-bound, and a miss is catastrophic

Because the new work per continuation turn is tiny, the turn's entire cost is decided by whether its prefix is a cache hit or a recompute. If the prefix (say a 12k-token document) has been evicted while the conversation was between turns, the server must re-prefill all 12k tokens — a spike two to three orders of magnitude larger than the turn's "real" work, inflating that turn's TTFT and, in aggregate, the prefill load and mean TTFT across the server.

Decomposition (measured). On the frozen workload the stock cache (hit 0.678) prefills 32.2M of the 99.9M prompt tokens. Of that prefill work, 18.7M is irreducible first-time compute, but 13.5M (42%) is evicted-continuation recompute — prefixes that were cached and reused before the cache dropped them under concurrency. Recovering the oracle (Bélády) hit rate (0.808) would cut prefill work by 40% (32.2M→19.2M). Because TTFT under load is queue-bound (median 1.0s, p90 3.2s, but p99 11.8s > the 8s SLO) and chunked prefill interleaves all in-flight work, cutting 22–40% of the prefill load shortens queues for every request — including the cold-document tail that sets p99. This is the lever the mechanism pulls.

Where a load cut helps — and where it cannot. The baseline TTFT is heavy-tailed at λ=3 (p50 1.0s, p90 3.2s, p99 11.8s). One might expect that, near saturation, cutting prefill demand would shrink the whole distribution (waiting time ∼ ρ/(1−ρ)). It does shrink the body — median and mean TTFT fall and sustainable throughput rises (§5). But the extreme tail does not move: it is a floor of unavoidable first-turn document prefills (a 100k-token cold prefill alone costs several seconds of compute, independent of load), which no reuse-based mechanism can remove. So a prefill-load cut moves the mean and throughput, not the cold-start p99 — a distinction §5 confirms and §5.1 turns into a measurement caveat.

2.3  The loss is not eviction order — it is scheduler-visible imminent reuse

We replay the workload's issue order (an open-loop Poisson rate-λ pull with multiturn re-enqueue, matching the benchmark harness) through a page-level cache simulator at the model's real page size, and compare LRU, Bélády (oracle MIN), and a realizable protect-pending policy that simply refuses to evict a conversation whose next turn is currently in the system. Hit rate = cached / prompt tokens (the protocol's definition).

Two facts make this actionable. First, the gap is largest exactly at the capacities that occur in practice: at nominal capacity 10.7M the effective reuse-cache is smaller because ~255 concurrently-running requests occupy L1 (measured: L1 usage ≈ 0.95–0.97 under load), placing the operating point near the +11 pp knee. Second, the winning signal is free: the scheduler already tracks which conversations have a waiting request.

This offline number is an optimistic upper bound. It assumes idealized targeting and no displacement of the rest of the working set. On the real system (§5) LRU is higher and the realized gain is ~1–2 pp (variance-affected, +0.4–2.8 across three nodes), not +7–11 pp: the 2-tier host layer already absorbs most device evictions (an evicted-from-L1 prefix still hits in L2), so only the narrow provably-at-risk queued set is worth protecting. We report the offline figure as motivation that a gap exists, and the ~1–2 pp as what is realizable.

2.4  In simulation, the eviction gap tracks the scheduler — and a recency-inversion that does transfer

Why does eviction order — which §2's premise (and the LRU≈Bélády folklore) calls a solved problem — suddenly leave 7–23 pp on the table here? Because the gap is not a property of the cache alone; it is a property of the scheduler×cache interaction — at least in a single-tier model. We explore this with the same page-level simulator, varying the request pull order, and separately confirm one model-free fact on the trace (the recency-inversion). We then test the coupling directly on the real 2-tier system in §2.5, where the hit-rate prediction does not hold (hit turns out scheduler-insensitive) — so read this subsection as the mechanism's origin and the source of the transferable inversion, not as a real-system hit result.

The mechanism: recency is inverted from reuse-distance among pending continuations. Under FCFS, a conversation's next turn joins the back of a long queue when the previous turn completes; the least-recently-active waiting conversation is the one that has queued longest, hence is pulled soonest — nearest reuse. Measured directly on the trace: at each eviction decision, the Spearman correlation between a pending conversation's recency (last-active index) and its next-use distance is ρ=+0.81 (median +0.84, n≈4900 decisions). So the prefix LRU is about to evict (its LRU victim among pending convs) is precisely the one reused soonest — LRU is anti-Bélády on exactly this set. Pinning the LRU-victim (which our mechanism does implicitly: it pins in arrival order, and the oldest-waiting = lowest-recency = highest-risk conversations fill the budget first) is therefore Bélády-aligned. A budget sweep confirms the priority is what matters: pinning the most-recently-active pending convs first (the ones LRU already keeps) captures ≈0% of the gap at any partial budget, while pinning the least-recently-active (risk-first) captures 61–92% at a 25–75% budget.

Why LPM erases the gap in simulation (and the real caveat). In the single-tier model a prefix-aware scheduler pulls the largest-matching-prefix waiting request, re-admitting a conversation's next turn while its prefix is still hot; reuse is temporally clustered and plain LRU already retains it (gap→0). This is why the sim shows no LPM gap. On the real 2-tier system, however, the host layer already absorbs eviction order, so hit is scheduler-insensitive and this sim gap does not materialize (§2.5). What survives to the real system is the model-free part — the recency-inversion above (it is measured on the trace, not the sim) — which is what makes the narrow pending signal the right target. The inversion also explains the §5 targeting ablation in one line: the rejected post-completion variant pins by recency (broad, recently-finished — including terminal conversations that never recur), spending the budget on essentially the ≈0%-capture set rather than the at-risk queued set, so it cannot help; only the queued (risk-aligned) set can.

2.5  What the real 2-tier system shows: hit is scheduler-insensitive, the tail is not

The simulation is single-tier; the coupling it predicts is a hypothesis about the real system, not a result on it. We tested it directly: a same-node A/B re-running the frozen 122B eval under --schedule-policy lpm vs the default fcfs (on-contract — the flag is not in eval.sh's frozen-knob denylist). The prediction was that LPM would raise stock hit and shrink our gap toward zero. It does not.

Interpretation — the 2-tier host absorbs eviction order, so retention (not scheduling) is the hit lever. On the real system the L2 host layer is large enough that a prefix evicted from L1 still hits in L2, so hit-rate is nearly insensitive to admission/eviction order: FCFS and LPM land within 0.5 pp at every rate. The sim's dramatic FCFS-vs-Bélády gap is thus a single-tier artifact — and it is the same reason our mechanism's realized gain (~1–2 pp) is far below the single-tier ceiling (~+7 pp): the host tier has already captured most of what eviction order could. What the scheduler does control is the p99 tail: LPM's prefix reordering starves first-turn (short-prefix) requests, inflating p99 by 41–65% at moderate load. The upshot sharpens our thesis rather than weakening it: switching schedulers is not a way to buy cache efficiency here (LPM gives no hit gain and a large tail penalty), whereas pending-aware retention delivers a genuine, lossless, always-non-negative hit gain on top of FCFS while preserving FCFS's tail. Retention is the realizable hit lever; the recency-inversion (ρ=+0.81) is why the narrow pending signal targets it correctly. (The sim remains useful as the mechanism's origin and as the reason the pending signal is Bélády-aligned; we simply do not claim its single-tier magnitudes transfer.)

3  Design

Mechanism. While a request waits in the scheduler's queue, pin the host (L2) residency of its matched prefix so a running batch cannot evict it; release the pin the instant the request is admitted (its prefix is then protected by the normal running-request lock) or aborted. Pinning is bounded by a token budget (a fraction of L2); when the pending set exceeds the budget the policy falls back to LRU for the overflow — it never deadlocks eviction and never uses more than the fixed memory budget.

Why it is novel. It is not a replacement policy (LRU/LFU/GDSF/2Q): those pick a victim from the resident set by a recency/frequency feature, and §2.4 shows those features are inverted from reuse here (the LRU victim among pending continuations is the soonest-reused, ρ=+0.81) — so no fixed-candidate recency/frequency rule closes the gap. We instead change the candidate set: an about-to-be-reused waiting prefix is removed from eviction eligibility entirely, using the scheduler's exact reuse-intent signal rather than a recency proxy. It is not Strata-style cache-aware scheduling, which overlaps unavoidable loads with compute; here we prevent the eviction that turns a cheap reload into an expensive recompute. It is not a config flag (write-through/write-back, exclusive tiering): those change what is backed up, not whether an about-to-be-reused prefix survives contention. The primitive is a reuse-intent signal flowing from scheduler to cache.

Code audit (novelty). In the stock engine, the KV of a request is lock-protected (inc_lock_ref) only at admission, i.e. once it is running; a request's own committed prefix is guarded by cache_protected_len during its execution. Nothing protects the cached prefix of a request that is still waiting in the queue — so a running batch can evict a queued continuation's document before it is scheduled, forcing a full recompute. Host-tier locking (inc_host_lock_ref) exists only on the L3 storage path (dormant in the 2-tier config). Our mechanism is the first to protect a waiting/recently-active conversation's host prefix, driven by the scheduler's pending set.

The mechanism, and one extension we tested. The mechanism itself is queued pinning: pin at enqueue, release at admission (the running-request lock then takes over). It covers the server-queue window, which deepens under load (queue p99≈95 even at λ=3). We also implemented and evaluated a natural post-completion extension: since the client caps concurrency near the server's, much of a continuation's inter-turn gap is spent behind the client semaphore, unprotected by queued pinning; the extension keeps the pin alive for a bounded horizon after admission (reusing the same pinned handle) to cover that gap. The ablation in §5 rejects this extension — broad retention displaces LRU's working set and does not help — so the shipped mechanism is queued pinning alone. Both share one host-token budget and a leak-proof release sweep; pinning is bounded and never deadlocks eviction.

4  Implementation

Active cache: UnifiedRadixCache (the hybrid-Mamba + hierarchical path; hiradix_cache.py/hi_mamba_radix_cache.py are dormant). The mechanism is ~110 lines in managers/scheduler.py: pin at _add_request_to_queue via UnifiedRadixCache.inc_host_lock_ref(last_host_node) (capturing to_dec_params() so the release mirrors the acquire, incl. the mamba component's skip_lock_node_ids); release via a tick-start sweep over pinned requests no longer in the waiting queue (leak-proof across all admit/abort paths). The post-completion extension is the same sweep keeping an admitted req's pin until a wall-clock horizon expires. All configurable by env (`VALIANT_PIN_ENABLE`, `VALIANT_PC_ENABLE`, `VALIANT_PIN_FRACTION`, `VALIANT_PC_HORIZON_S`) so every ablation is one code version. No engine data-structure changes; no new tiers; budget = fraction × L2 size. Lossless by construction: the mechanism only changes which KV is retained (reuse is bit-identical to recompute). CPU unit tests cover the pin bookkeeping and the horizon logic. Commit: d10eabb5e (evolve/valiant); ~110 LOC in managers/scheduler.py.

5  Evaluation

5.1  Methodology

Protocol (frozen). One 8×H100 node, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8 TP8, ctx 262144, 2-tier L1 (GPU, 2.35M tok) + L2 (host, 768 GB ≈ 8.4M tok), no L3. Full-decode Poisson rate sweep λ∈{3,5,7,10} with a warmup burst and no inter-rate flush (warm steady-state), max-concurrency 256, 1553-conversation mix. Headline = goodput@SLO (max sustained req/s with p99 TTFT ≤ 8s); we also report per-rate p99 TTFT (the curve) and hit rate.

A/B design. Every comparison is same-node: on a single node we run stock (all mechanism env off), then queued-only (VALIANT_PC_ENABLE=0), then +post-completion (VALIANT_PC_ENABLE=1) back-to-back, so node-to-node variance (measured at ±14% req/s / ±30% p99 across nodes) never confounds a delta. We replicate the full triple on a second node (n=2) for error bars. stock is the same binary with the mechanism disabled (not a different commit), isolating the mechanism from any incidental code overhead.

Attribution controls. (i) Peak output-token throughput is expected ~flat (decode is compute-bound); the mechanism must move the prefill-driven p99/goodput, not the decode ceiling — we report peak tok/s to show it does not change. (ii) The gain must be a mechanism, not a config flip: stock and treatment share every frozen flag (write-through, io-backend, layout, tier sizes); the only difference is which cached prefixes are retained. (iii) Not mere queue de-saturation: we show the effect via reduced recompute / higher hit rate (a cache property), and that pins activate ([valiant] pinned_reqs>0).

Metric-variance caveat (measured). We find goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at the SLO boundary: three identical stock runs on three nodes gave λ=3 p99 TTFT = 6.3s / 8.1s / 11.8s and hence goodput@SLO = 3.02 / 0 / 0 — the stock config alone flips the headline. In contrast the hit rate was stable (0.673–0.678). We therefore (a) lead with the node-independent hit-rate / recompute-reduction as the primary, reproducible evidence; (b) report p99 as a same-node delta with n≥2 (a treatment is run on the same node immediately after its stock control); and (c) treat absolute goodput@SLO as a curve-shift indicator rather than a single-run headline. This is itself a finding: a p99-tail SLO on this saturating workload needs same-node, replicated measurement to be meaningful.

Lossless gate. The mechanism only changes which KV is retained; reuse of a cached prefix is bit-identical to recomputing it, so outputs are unchanged vs the stock run by construction (verified: no change to the decode path or the KV values).

5.2  Results

Pending-aware retention (v1) is a lossless, reliably-positive, but modest and variance-affected win. Table 1 shows stock vs v1 on the first two same-node A/B pairs (nodes 0-3 and 1-2): hit rises at every rate, largest at the operating rate and tapering under saturation; sustained throughput rises 2–6% at moderate load (λ=5,7); p50 TTFT falls ~25% at λ=3. All lossless (v1 only changes which KV is retained).

A third same-node A/B (ondem-2) revises the magnitude down and reveals variance. Re-running the same A/B on a third node gave hit λ3 0.673→0.677 = +0.4 pp — far below the +2.2/+2.8 pp of the first two — despite the pin engaging more strongly there (up to 19 concurrent pins vs ≤6 on the earlier nodes). So the +2.5 pp we first reported (n=2) was the high end: across three nodes the operating-rate gain is +0.4 to +2.8 pp (mean ≈+1.8 pp). The mechanism is reliably positive — every measured A/B, under both schedulers, showed a non-negative hit change (it is lossless and only ever protects) — but its magnitude is variance-affected, exactly as this workload's other metrics are (§5.1). We therefore state the result as a modest, lossless, always-non-negative gain of order ~1–2 pp, not a precise +2.5 pp.

Throughput gain peaks at the mid-load knee (λ=5,7) and vanishes at full saturation (λ=10, GPU-compute-bound); peak decode throughput is flat (589→592 tok/s) — the mechanism moves prefill, not the decode ceiling, as expected.

Targeting ablation — the post-completion extension hurts. Extending retention to every recently-finished conversation for a 20 s horizon (to cover the client think-gap) pins ~15× more prefixes (≈70–99 vs ≤6 for queued-only) but delivers no hit gain: hit@λ3 = 0.674 / 0.673 (n=2 same-node), ≈ stock (0.678 / 0.673) and far below v1 (0.700 / 0.701). Since the combined config is v1 plus post-completion, the extension cancels v1's entire +2.5 pp gain. This is not a prediction failure — continuation is 78% predictable from turn count (only 22% of finished turns are terminal). Rather, broad retention displaces a working set LRU already keeps well: LRU retains most reused-soon KV, so reserving capacity for all recent conversations merely reshuffles it and, under the pin budget, can evict other useful KV — netting ≤0. Only the narrow set that LRU provably mishandles — continuations already waiting in the scheduler's queue — is worth protecting. The signal that matters is the pending set, not broad recency. Two clean post-completion full sweeps confirm the pattern at every rate (hit ≈ stock and below v1 at λ=3/5/7/10).

Broad pinning also destabilizes eviction (a third strike). Beyond gaining nothing, the post-completion config intermittently crashed the scheduler at high load (one of three post-completion runs, at λ=7) with a tree-consistency assertion in device eviction; the shipped queued-pin never crashed (0 of 4 stock/v1 runs, 0 of 2 v1 runs across all rates). Root cause: a host-pinned node keeps a host-only child alive, so the child's ancestor can remain a device-eviction candidate while non-empty; the write-through delete-entirely path then removes a node that still has a child, corrupting the radix tree. The long-lived pins of post-completion make this state common; the shipped mechanism (queued pinning) releases at admission, so its pins are short-lived and it ran clean across all rates in both same-node sweeps. This is a concrete instance of the general lesson: broad, long-held reuse-aware pinning interacts badly with the tiered eviction machinery, while the narrow pending-set pin does not.

The win is small because the exploitable set is small — not because of tuning. Two controls rule out cheaper explanations. (i) v1 never uses more than 9% of its pin budget at any rate (peak ≈6 concurrent pins at λ=3), so raising the budget/fraction cannot help — the gain is bounded by the size of the provably-at-risk queued set, which is inherently small. (ii) The mechanism's code is fully guarded by a single enable flag; with it off, the binary is byte-for-byte the stock path (no extra prefix match), so the +2.5 pp is the pin itself, not incidental overhead — consistent with stock (pristine) and stock (same binary, flag off) being statistically identical.

goodput@SLO is not a reliable single-run headline. v1 reduced λ=3 p99 by 2% on node 0-3 (8.13→7.98 s), which crossed the 8 s SLO (goodput 0→3.02); on node 1-2 the stock p99 was a "lucky" 6.3 s and v1's 8.0 s read as a regression. Both are the boundary noise quantified in §5.1 — the p99 tail is set by cold-document first-turn prefills (irreducible), which v1 does not target. We therefore claim the robust hit-rate / throughput result, not a goodput@SLO crossing.

Cache retention moves the mean/throughput, not the tail — by construction. v1 cuts median TTFT 26% and lifts hit and throughput, but leaves p99 essentially unchanged. This is not a shortcoming of the mechanism but a structural property of the workload: the p99 tail is dominated by first-turn prefills of long documents (mean 12k, max 183k tokens), which are first-time compute and therefore never cache-reusable by any lossless mechanism. Prefix caching — and reuse-aware retention on top of it — can only reduce re-prefill of already-seen prefixes, i.e. it optimizes the mean and the sustainable throughput, not the cold-start tail. A p99-tail SLO on this workload is thus the wrong yardstick for a cache mechanism; the right ones (hit-rate, mean TTFT, moderate-load throughput) all move, robustly and losslessly.

6  Related work

Strata (2025) attacks the data plane and scheduler: GPU-assisted I/O to defragment transfers and cache-aware scheduling to overlap load stalls with compute, making a loading-bound system compute-bound. It does not prevent the eviction that turns a reload into a recompute, and its data-plane fix (the kernel I/O path) is unavailable for hybrid-Mamba models, which are frozen to direct/page_first_direct by the Mamba host pool. Mooncake (FAST'25) and AttentionStore use a KV store and scheduling for reuse across requests; LMCache caches/streams KV; the SGLang HiCache blog contributes tiering, write policies, layer-wise load/compute overlap, and L3 prefetch staging. All treat retention as (recency-ordered) eviction plus (optional) prefetch. Our contribution is orthogonal: a scheduler-sourced reuse-intent signal that decides retention under contention, which the above leave to LRU. Classic cache-admission/replacement (TinyLFU, GDSF, 2Q, ARC) operates on recency/ frequency features; we instead use an exact, workload-structural signal (in-flight continuation).

7  Limitations & when it fails

  • Modest, variance-affected magnitude. The realizable win is modest (hit +0.4–2.8 pp across three same-node nodes, mean ≈+1.8; +2–6% throughput) — far below the oracle gap — because only the server-queue signal is safe to act on (see below). It is a real, lossless, reliably-non-negative improvement, but a small one whose exact size varies run-to-run/node-to-node like this workload's other metrics; we do not claim a precise number.
  • The client think-gap is not server-realizable. A prefix evicted during the client's think/send gap (before its next turn reaches the server) cannot be protected by a server-queue signal. The obvious fix — retain every recently-finished conversation for a horizon — hurts (§5): it spends scarce L2 on conversations that are never reused. Closing this gap would require a session-level "will-continue" hint from the client/router that the serving layer does not have.
  • Capacity. When the pending set exceeds the L2 pin budget, only a subset is protected; the gain degrades gracefully to LRU (never worse — pinning is bounded and released on admission/abort).
  • Single-turn / low-reuse workloads. If most conversations are one-shot, there is little pending reuse to protect and the mechanism is a safe no-op.
  • Metric. On this workload goodput@SLO is variance-dominated at the boundary; we report it but rely on hit-rate/throughput. A different SLO or a less bursty workload could make goodput@SLO the cleaner headline.
  • The sim's scheduler-coupling does not transfer to real hit (measured, §2.5). §2.4's single-tier simulation predicts a large FCFS eviction gap that prefix-aware scheduling closes. The same-node FCFS-vs-LPM A/B on the 122B system refutes the hit half of that prediction: real hit is scheduler-insensitive (Δ<0.5 pp at every rate), because the 2-tier host absorbs eviction order — LPM only worsens the p99 tail. We report this refutation of our own sim rather than the sim's optimistic magnitude. It does not affect the measured contribution (retention's ~1–2 pp is a real, lossless FCFS-native gain) and it sharpens the thesis (retention, not scheduling, is the hit lever). The model-free part of the analysis — the recency-inversion ρ=+0.81 that makes the pending signal well-targeted — does transfer. Remaining open item: a same-node v1-under-LPM vs v1-under-FCFS A/B (in flight) to confirm the pin's gain is scheduler-independent, as retention-is-the-lever predicts.
  • Eviction-interaction edge case. Host-pinning a deep node keeps a host-only child alive, which can leave the child's ancestor a device-eviction candidate while non-empty; the engine's write-through delete-entirely path then trips a tree-consistency assertion. The shipped queued-pin releases at admission (short-lived pins) and was stable across both full same-node sweeps; the rejected long-held post-completion variant triggered it at high load. We implemented a targeted guard (in the write-through delete-entirely path, leave a node that still has children as an evicted tombstone instead of unlinking it — a latent-invariant fix independent of our mechanism), CPU-verified; GPU-revalidation under the crash-inducing config is opportunistic given cluster contention. The shipped short-pin mechanism was already stable across all runs, so the results stand regardless of the guard.
