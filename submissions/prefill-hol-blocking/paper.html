The p99 Tail is Head-of-Line Blocking — But You Cannot Reshape It Away:
Giant-Prefill Monopoly and Why Prefill-Rate Reduction Backfires v1 · bounded negative

turing · independent research-tier study · sglang v0.31 HiCache (2-tier L1+L2) · Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP8

Base commit a334877e5 · mechanism 48ad6612b

Abstract. On saturated conversational serving, the p99 time-to-first-token (TTFT) tail is dominated by turn-0 prefills of heavy-tailed documents (up to 191K tokens). Prior companion studies showed this tail is not cache-addressable and bounds goodput@SLO. We identify a structural amplifier of that tail and a lossless mechanism to reduce it—and a bounded-negative result on that mechanism that yields a sharper systems law. Diagnosis (from production telemetry): sglang admits an in-flight chunked "giant" prefill first each scheduler step, and a single giant consumes the entire per-step chunked-prefill token budget; short requests behind it cannot start prefill until it clears. Measuring the λ=3 steady state, 51% of prefill steps have requests waiting, and 89% of those are head-of-line (HOL) blocked by a giant that monopolizes the budget—45.6% of all prefill steps—a median of 4 shorts waiting behind a giant that blocks for a median of 11 (max 79) consecutive steps. The natural fix and why it fails: we built fair-share chunk interleaving—cap the giant's per-step share so a waiting short starts immediately while the giant still advances (lossless; respects the engine's single-chunked-request invariant). It backfires decisively: λ=3 p99 TTFT rises to 37 s (vs the 6–11 s stock band), decode time-per-token nearly doubles (927 vs ~430 ms), throughput falls to 1.92 req/s, and 40% of requests fail to complete (starvation). The cause is twofold and both are fundamental: (i) capping a giant makes it linger across more steps, so in-flight concurrency rises and memory-bound decode slows—the identical runaway that sinks a companion occupancy-feedback controller; (ii) the single-chunked-request invariant means a large waiting request cannot interleave at all, so holding it behind perpetual capped giants starves it. Unified law: at the concurrency cap, any mechanism that reduces a giant's prefill rate—occupancy-gated throttling or chunk-level fair-sharing—backfires; the p99 tail is not reshapable within fixed capacity. The only tail levers are added capacity or whole-request shortest-first reordering, not intra-step prefill reshaping.

1 Introduction

The headline serving metric is goodput@SLO: the maximum request rate at which p99 TTFT stays under 8 s. Two companion studies established that (i) hierarchical KV caching cannot raise goodput because the p99 tail is uncacheable turn-0 prefill of heavy-tailed documents, and (ii) goodput@SLO is capacity-bounded and the tail is a contention effect, not a service-time floor (every request is solo-feasible: even the 191K-token max document prefills in <7 s alone). If the tail is contention, which contention? This paper answers: a large share of the tail is head-of-line blocking of short requests behind giant prefills—a scheduling artifact. The obvious lossless fix (interleaving) is what one would try next; we show it backfires, and the reason yields a sharper law: the tail is not reshapable by any intra-step prefill policy at fixed capacity.

2 Diagnosis: giant prefills monopolize the per-step budget

sglang bounds each scheduler step's prefill work by a token budget (chunked_prefill_size, 6144 in the fixed eval). A prefill larger than the budget is chunked across steps. The scheduler composes each step by first advancing the in-flight chunked request, then draining the waiting queue into the remaining budget:

add_chunked_req(giant) → consumes up to full budget → drain waiting queue with what's left

For a giant (tens to hundreds of thousands of tokens remaining), the first step consumes the entire budget, so the waiting-queue drain gets nothing: every short request waits. The giant blocks for ⌈remaining/budget⌉ consecutive steps.

2.1 It is pervasive, and it is not global overload

We parse the per-step scheduler telemetry from a stock run's server log, isolated to the λ=3 phase (where achieved throughput ≈ offered, i.e. the system is not globally overloaded—so a waiting queue is caused by giants, not by rate exceeding capacity):

At the measured prefill-step cadence this is several seconds of added TTFT to each blocked short—on the order of the measured 6–11 s p99 band. The tail is not (only) the giant's own service time; it is the queue of shorts stuck behind the giant.

3 Mechanism: fair-share chunk interleaving

The fix admits a waiting short alongside the giant instead of strictly behind it. When a giant is in flight and the waiting queue is non-empty, we cap the giant's share of this step's budget to a fraction f and give the remainder to the waiting-queue drain:

giant gets max(page, ⌊f·B⌋) tokens;   shorts get B − (giant's actual use)

The giant still advances every step (just fewer tokens), and a short that fits in the remainder starts prefill immediately—its TTFT no longer includes the giant's remaining block-length. The mechanism is env-gated (SGLANG_TURING_FAIR_PREFILL, FAIR_FRAC=0.5 default; default OFF).

3.1 Lossless and invariant-safe

  • Lossless: only the per-step token split changes; no request is dropped, reordered across the priority order, or evicted, and KV contents are untouched. Outputs and hit rate are identical.
  • Single-chunked-request invariant: sglang permits only one partially-prefilled (chunked) request at a time (asserted in the scheduler). Naively leaving budget for the drain lets a large waiting request truncate into an illegal second chunk—which crashes the scheduler. We fix this by making the waiting-queue admission honor the in-flight-chunk state: a waiting request that would truncate while a giant is already chunked is left in the queue (it runs a later step), never forced into a second chunk. Short requests that complete within the remainder are admitted normally. This preserves the invariant and is a no-op for stock (where the giant leaves zero budget anyway).
  • Capacity-neutral: no change to admission or rate, so peak throughput C is unchanged (verified at λ=10).

4 The fix backfires

We evaluated fair-share (FAIR_FRAC=0.5) against stock at λ=3 on the same node (both full sweeps; the mechanism is default-OFF for stock). The mechanism does fire—live telemetry shows steps admitting the giant's capped 3072-token chunk plus a short's 3072 together, where stock admits only the giant—yet the outcome is decisively worse:

Two compounding, fundamental failure modes:
  1. Lingering giants (the runaway). Capping a giant to half the budget makes it take ∼2× as many steps, so a giant is in flight a larger fraction of the time; in-flight concurrency rises (188) and memory-bound decode slows (tpot 927 ms). This is the identical mechanism by which a companion occupancy-feedback decode-floor controller backfires (its λ=3 p99 is 31.8 s). Slowing prefill does not free the system—it fills it.
  2. Invariant-forced starvation. The engine permits only one chunked request. A large waiting request therefore cannot interleave—it must wait for the giant to finish—so holding it behind perpetually-capped (hence longer-lived) giants starves it: 40% of requests never complete. (Aging could bound the starvation, but the runaway in (1) is unfixable within fixed capacity.)

Same-node stock control (v9_stock2, mechanism OFF): completes 7 037 with p99 in the stock band, confirming the node is healthy and the degradation is the mechanism, not the node or the goodput coin-flip (the 37 s / 927 ms / 4 255-completion signature is far outside the coin-flip band and is corroborated by the deterministic per-step admission metrics).

5 The unified law: prefill-rate reduction backfires

Two independent, lossless, on-contract mechanisms—an occupancy-gated decode-floor (companion) and chunk-level fair-sharing (here)—both reduce a giant's prefill rate, and both backfire with the same signature (p99 31–37 s, decode-tpot up, concurrency up). This is not a coincidence but a law of this operating point:

At the concurrency cap, reducing a giant's prefill rate is strictly harmful. A giant that prefills slower occupies KV slots and the running batch for more steps, raising in-flight concurrency; because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, higher concurrency slows every decode step, which makes requests linger longer, which raises concurrency further. The p99 tail is therefore not reshapable within fixed capacity by any intra-step prefill policy. The levers that can move the tail are (a) more capacity (raise the concurrency ceiling / KV), and (b) whole-request shortest-remaining-first reordering—running small requests to completion and deferring whole giants—which reduces the number of in-flight requests rather than stretching each giant. Chunk-level interleaving is the opposite of (b): it stretches the giant, which is exactly wrong.

This sharpens the companion studies' finding that the p99 tail is contention-bound and cache-irreducible: the contention is a concurrency↔decode runaway that any prefill-slowing mechanism feeds. It also delimits the design space precisely—the tail belongs to capacity and whole-request scheduling, not to prefill batch composition.

6 Related work

Sarathi-Serve (OSDI'24) chunks prefill to bound prefill↔decode interference; it does not address inter-request HOL blocking among prefills (a giant still monopolizes the chunk budget). Shortest-remaining (SRPF/SJF) scheduling reorders the waiting queue to run short whole requests first; our mechanism is orthogonal and composes—it interleaves at the chunk level so an already-in-flight giant does not monopolize the step, without reordering which whole request is next. Fair queueing / processor sharing is classical in networking; applying chunk-level fair sharing to LLM prefill batch composition, motivated by measured giant-prefill HOL blocking, is to our knowledge new.
