Defer, Don't Throttle: Why Fair Prefill-Budget Sharing Cannot Fix the Head-of-Line Goodput Tail

kleinrock · sglang KV-cache / serving, v0.31 · goodput@SLO rate sweep, Qwen3.5-122B, 2-tier · a diagnosis + a bounded-negative result (same-node A/Bs, n≥3, per-request attribution)
Abstract. On an open-loop, heavy-tailed conversational serving workload, the p99 time-to-first-token (TTFT) tail that defines goodput@SLO is widely assumed to be the cold prefill of the large documents themselves. With direct per-request attribution (15,549 requests) we show this is wrong: the tail is inter-request head-of-line (HOL) blocking — tiny requests stranded behind the serialized chunked prefill of a handful of huge cold documents, and the HOL victims suffer a worse tail than the documents that block them (tiny <1K-token requests: p99 26.9–30.5 s vs the heavy documents' own 20.4–23.3 s). Because the tail is HOL it is schedulable — deferring the few heavy documents past the percentile (the classical shortest-remaining-prefill policy, used here only as a reference target) clears the SLO. The natural engineering instinct, though, is to avoid deferring anyone: keep every request making progress and simply share the prefill budget fairly. Our main result is that this instinct is wrong, and demonstrably so on this workload. We design a fair, non-starving, opt-in, lossless mechanism — a reserved short-prefill lane that holds back a slice of the per-iteration budget so short requests prefill alongside an in-flight heavy document — and show, in a clean same-node ablation, that it fails at every reserve size (monotone-harmful: +22% at 1024, +72% at 2048, n=3; no crossover) and, pushed to R≥3072, destabilizes the server (a KV-pool-leak crash at 3072; an outright throughput collapse at 4096). The mechanism is diagnosed: throttling the heavy document makes it occupy the prefill pipeline for more iterations (≈32→94), maximizing the blocking window — the opposite of deferral. We further show the benefit is specific to deferral by prefill size: a genuine reordering by a different key (longest-output-first) does not help. Finally, bridging a companion result, we show deferral does not merely lower the knee median but collapses the goodput@SLO cold-start coin-flip (λ=3 run-to-run σ/m 0.53→0.04, a ~13× variance reduction, 0/5→5/5) — the metric's knee variance is itself a schedulability artifact. This yields a crisp, transferable design principle — defer, don't throttle: to protect the goodput tail of a heavy-tailed prefill workload you must defer the few heavy documents past the percentile; fair budget-sharing cannot substitute, and pushed for fairness it destabilizes the server. Contributions: the per-request HOL diagnosis; the reserved-lane bounded negative with its destabilization failure modes; the deferral-by-prefill-size specificity; and the coin-flip-collapse bridge. (Shortest-remaining-prefill scheduling itself is classical and is not claimed as a contribution here.)

1 Introduction

Tiered-KV LLM servers under an open-loop Poisson rate sweep are scored by goodput@SLO: the maximum request rate sustaining a p99 TTFT deadline (here 8 s). On a realistic conversational workload — a Mooncake-style 1:1:1 mix (ShareGPT+LEval+LooGLE), 1553 conversations, 7163 turns, Qwen3.5-122B on 8×H100, 2-tier KV (GPU HBM + 768 GB host) — the headline never passes the SLO under stock scheduling at any swept rate. The natural assumption is that this is fundamental: a first-turn document can be up to 192K tokens, so its cold prefill alone approaches the deadline and the tail looks irreducible.

That assumption is wrong, and correcting it exposes a sharp and non-obvious design boundary. We first diagnose the tail, then map the space of fixes and find that the intuitively "fair" fix is exactly the one that backfires.

Contributions.

  • Diagnosis (§3). Direct per-request attribution shows the p99 tail is not the heavy documents but the head-of-line victims queued behind them: tiny requests (<1K tokens, whose own prefill is <0.3 s) have a higher p99 TTFT than the heavy documents themselves. A 27-token request with a 27 s TTFT is, definitionally, pure queueing.
  • A bounded negative (§4.2) — the core result. We design and evaluate a fair, non-starving, lossless alternative to deferral (a reserved short-prefill lane that keeps the heavy document progressing while sharing budget with short requests) and show it fails at every reserve size with no crossover, and destabilizes the server (throughput collapse + OOM) when pushed. Fair budget-sharing is not merely suboptimal; it is counter-productive, and we explain why.
  • Specificity (§4.3). The lever is deferral by remaining prefill specifically: a genuine reordering by a different key (longest-output-first) does not help — ruling out "any reordering works."
  • A design principle (§4.4) — defer, don't throttle — and its load bound (§5.2): deferral reduces the tail ~70% at every load but clears the SLO only below severe saturation (λ≤5).
  • A coin-flip-collapse bridge (§5.3). A companion paper shows goodput@SLO is a cold-start coin-flip at the knee for caching. We show deferral does not merely shift the knee median — it collapses that run-to-run variance ~13× (σ/m 0.53→0.04, 0/5→5/5), so the metric's knee variance is itself a schedulability artifact: a well-ordered queue is faster and reliable.
On scope and prior art. The reference policy that does fix the tail here is shortest-remaining-prefill-first (SRPF), a classical shortest-job-first variant; that reordering can raise LLM serving goodput is known. We therefore do not claim SRPF as a contribution — it appears only as the reference target establishing that the tail is deferrable (§4.1). Our contributions are the per-request HOL diagnosis, the negative result on fair budget-sharing (a mechanism we built specifically to avoid deferral), and the resulting necessary-condition. A companion paper (prefill-slo-tail) develops the orthogonal result that this same metric is a variance-dominated coin-flip for caching comparisons; here we treat the scheduling of the tail.

2 Motivation: a heavy-tailed cold-prefill workload, serialized

2.1 The workload

The first turn of each conversation prefills a document with a strongly heavy-tailed length (median 16.7K, p99 65.6K, max 192,542 tokens); follow-up turns add only ~16 new tokens and reuse the first turn's KV (hit rate ~0.66 is entirely intra-conversation reuse). The offered work is thus a mixture of a few very large cold prefills and many tiny requests — the classic setting for head-of-line pathology.

2.2 One chunked prefill at a time (code-verified)

sglang admits at most one chunked (partially prefilled) request at a time. Each scheduler iteration has a 6144-token prefill budget; when a chunked request is in flight, PrefillAdder.add_chunked_req claims min(rem_chunk_tokens, rem_total_tokens) — the entire 6144 for a large document mid-prefill — driving the remaining budget to zero, so the admission loop returns NO_TOKEN for every other waiting request. A 192K-token document therefore monopolizes the prefill pipeline for ⌈192542/6144⌉≈32 consecutive iterations; a request arriving in that window waits ~32×(iteration time) for its first token — independent of its own (possibly negligible) prefill cost. This is inter-request head-of-line blocking, distinct from a request's own prefill time, and it is the mechanism we attribute the tail to in §3.

3 The tail is head-of-line blocking, not the heavy documents

We instrument the load generator to dump per-request (prompt length, TTFT) pairs (env-gated, KLEINROCK_PERREQ_DUMP, off by default) and analyze stock (FCFS) runs at λ=3 and λ=5 (15,549 requests total).

The head-of-line victims suffer more than the documents that block them. Tiny requests (<1K tokens, own prefill <0.3 s) have a higher p99 TTFT than the heavy documents themselves: 26.9 s (λ=3) / 30.5 s (λ=5) for tiny requests versus 20.4 s / 23.3 s for documents ≥50K tokens. 27–40% of the p99 tail are tiny requests, and hundreds of them exceed the 8 s SLO purely from queueing. A <1K-token request cannot spend 22 s in its own prefill — that TTFT is definitionally HOL delay.

Because the p99 tail is queueing behind a few heavy documents (only ~3–4 documents exceed 100K tokens, so they lie beyond the 99th percentile), it is schedulable: reordering which requests wait can move it. The rest of the paper asks which class of fix works — and finds the answer is narrow.

4 The design axis: defer, don't throttle

All experiments are same-node A/Bs (one exclusive 8×H100 node, warm steady state, byte-identical flags except the scheduling knob), K=3–5 replicates, at the frozen contract (1553 conversations, max-concurrency 256, 8 s SLO). We report the p99 TTFT distribution and the goodput pass-count; node heterogeneity is controlled by holding the node fixed within each comparison.

4.1 Reference target: deferral clears the tail

To establish that the HOL tail is deferrable at all, we use the classical shortest-remaining-prefill-first (SRPF) policy — sort the waiting queue by uncached tokens ascending, so the few heavy documents go to the back. This is a reference point, not our contribution (SRPF is a textbook shortest-job-first variant, and that request reordering raises LLM goodput is known). At λ=5 it turns reliable-fail into pass and defines the achievable target for any fix:

λ=5, same nodenp99 TTFT med (ms)mean TTFTgoodput passreq/sE2E mean
FCFS (stock)52389120640/54.1045.3 s
deferral (SRPF ref.)5698311835/54.0845.1 s

Deferral cuts p99 TTFT ~71% at equal throughput and unchanged end-to-end latency (mean ~45 s for both arms, within 1%; if anything marginally lower under deferral): the heavy documents' first token is deferred but their E2E (decode-dominated) is unaffected, so this is a near-Pareto reordering, not a starvation artifact. The question this paper answers is whether a fairer mechanism — one that never defers anyone — can reach the same target. It cannot.

4.2 The bounded negative: a fair reserved lane fails at every size

The engineering instinct against SRPF is that it defers heavy documents unboundedly. So we asked: can a fair, non-starving mechanism capture the p99 target without deferring them? We designed one — a reserved short-prefill lane: while a heavy document is mid-prefill, hold back a slice R of the 6144-token per-iteration budget so short waiting requests prefill alongside it. The heavy document still makes progress every iteration (no starvation); the knob is opt-in and R=0 is byte-identical stock (lossless). It does not work, and a size sweep shows the failure is systematic — monotone, with no crossover:

At λ=3 the 2048 reserve is likewise harmful (8.1→19.5 s, +141%, n=3), so the negative holds across load. The reason is instructive and is the crux of the paper:

Throttling maximizes the blocking window. Sharing budget with the heavy document keeps it running but slower, so it occupies the prefill pipeline for proportionally more iterations (≈94 at R=4096 vs 32 at R=0) — the exact opposite of deferral, which removes it from the pipeline entirely. A thin reserve trickles a few victims through but lengthens the congestion window; a larger reserve starves the pipeline of forward progress while the backlog grows, until the accumulated KV destabilizes the server — a KV-pool-leak invariant violation at R=3072, an outright throughput collapse (and, in an earlier run, an OOM) at R=4096. Throttling-while-running never beats FCFS (neutral at best, at the thinnest reserve) and is far worse than deferral.

4.3 The lever is deferral by prefill size, not reordering in general

Is any queue reordering enough, or deferral-by-prefill-size specifically? A longest-output-first (LOF) policy is a genuine reordering, but by predicted output length — orthogonal to prefill size, so it does not defer the heavy documents. On a fully same-node triple (λ=5, n=3 each, distributions disjoint):

policy (λ=5, one node, n=3)p99 TTFT med (ms)goodput pass
FCFS226580/3

deferral (SRPF ref., by remaining prefill)66593/3

LOF fails exactly like FCFS (indeed slightly worse); only deferral-by-prefill-size passes. Reordering in general is not the lever; deferring the heavy prefills specifically is.

4.4 The principle: defer, don't throttle

Combining §4.1–4.3: the goodput tail of a heavy-tailed prefill workload is fixed by deferring the few heavy documents past the percentile (an E2E-neutral reordering), and by nothing else we could construct. Keeping them progressing while sharing budget — the "fair" alternative — forfeits the benefit at every size and, pushed harder, destabilizes the server; reordering by a non-prefill key does not help. Defer, don't throttle.

5 Evaluation details

5.1 Losslessness and honest controls

Every policy here is lossless: they reorder or split the prefill token budget but compute every token of every request, in order within a request, producing identical outputs (the reserve knob is byte-identical stock at R=0). The decode-bound controls are ~flat across all arms (peak tok/s, request throughput ~4.1 req/s for FCFS and deferral), confirming the effects are scheduling of first-token latency, not throughput changes. The reserve negative's throughput drop (3.6→2.0) is itself the failure mode, not a measurement of a different ceiling.

5.2 Across load: the deferral benefit is consistent but bounded to λ≤5

The reference deferral policy's relative tail reduction is consistent across the sweep, but converts to a goodput pass only below severe saturation:

λ (same node, n=3, deferral vs FCFS)FCFS p99deferral p99Δdeferral goodput
3 (knee, n=5)22.0 s (coin-flip 9.7–35.9)6.7 s−70%5/5 pass (variance collapses, §5.3)
5 (saturation)23.9 s7.0 s−71%pass
7 (severe saturation)33.3 s9.6 s−71%fail

At λ=7 the offered load (7 req/s) far exceeds the ~4.6 req/s compute ceiling, so the queue grows unboundedly; deferral still cuts p99 71% (33.3→9.6 s) but the backlog keeps even the reordered short requests above the 8 s SLO. So the goodput@SLO ceiling of the scheduling lever is ≈λ=5: the tail is reduced everywhere but the SLO is cleared only below severe saturation. Fixing λ=7 needs provisioning or admission control, not reordering — a boundary on the whole scheduling axis, not just on our negative.

5.3 Deferral collapses the knee coin-flip, not just the median

Our companion paper (prefill-slo-tail) establishes that at the queueing knee (λ=3) goodput@SLO is a cold-start coin-flip for caching: identical stock runs straddle the SLO with large run-to-run variance, so the binary pass/fail verdict is dominated by metastability rather than by the cache. A question bridges that result to this paper's scheduling lever: does deferral merely shift the knee median under the SLO, or does it collapse the metastable variance — making goodput reliable, not just passing? We answer with a same-node K=5 A/B at λ=3 (fcfs vs the deferral reference; raw runs runs/v9-defer-knee):

λ=3, same node, n=5p99 TTFT samples (ms)medianσ/mgoodput
fcfs (stock)9671, 12265, 22035, 34132, 3587022.0 s0.530/5

Deferral cuts the knee median p99 −70% (Fisher exact p=0.008; the distributions are disjoint — srpf max 6.76 s < fcfs min 9.67 s) and collapses the run-to-run variance ∼13× (σ/m 0.53→0.04): the fcfs spread is 3.7× (a wide straddle of the SLO), the deferral spread just 1.09× (tight, entirely under it). p50, hit rate, and request throughput are flat (lossless, throughput-neutral). So the goodput@SLO knee coin-flip is a schedulability artifact: a well-ordered (deferred) queue is not only faster but ~13× more predictable.

This completes, rather than contradicts, the companion paper: the coin-flip is real and caching is blind to it, but it is caused by the head-of-line queue's sensitivity to arrival ordering (§3), which deferral removes. The metric is movable and stabilizable by scheduling yet invariant to caching — precisely why caching must be evaluated on stable metrics, not goodput@SLO. At the knee, deferral converts an unreliable pass/fail into a reliable pass. (SRPF remains a cited reference here, not a contribution.)

6 Related work

Request reordering for LLM goodput. Shortest-remaining / shortest-job-first scheduling is classical (SJF/SRPT; Harchol-Balter), and reordering has been reported to raise LLM serving goodput. We use it only as the reference deferral policy (§4.1); our contributions are the HOL diagnosis and the negative that a fair budget-sharing alternative cannot substitute for it.

Chunked prefill (Sarathi-Serve, OSDI'24; vLLM). Splits long prefills into token-budget chunks and mixes decode to eliminate prefill→decode stalls. It does not reserve budget for other waiting prefills; a single large chunked prefill can still monopolize the budget across its chunks — exactly the inter-prefill HOL we diagnose. Our reserved-lane negative is the direct test of "fair-share that budget instead of reordering," and shows it backfires.

Fair queuing / DRR / VTC (OSDI'24). The reserved lane is fair-queuing-flavored at the prefill-token-budget level; fair-share LLM schedulers target inter-client fairness. Our finding is that fairness is precisely the wrong objective for this tail: the goodput lever requires the un-fair deferral of the heavy documents, and enforcing progress-fairness on the prefill budget is counter-productive.

FastServe. Preemptive MLFQ with skip-join to bound request-level HOL via preemption. Our mechanisms are non-preemptive (admission-order / budget-split only); we show non-preemptive deferral suffices while non-preemptive throttling does not — a cheaper regime than preemption when it applies.

Mooncake (FAST'25); disaggregated prefill/decode. Separate prefill/decode pools across nodes; orthogonal to single-node intra-iteration prefill budgeting, which is where the HOL and our negative live.

7 Limitations & when it does not hold

  • The destabilization points (R≥3072) are not per-point replicated. At 3072 only rep 1 completed before a pool-leak crash; at 4096 the first rep collapsed and never completed. The robust bounded negative is anchored on the replicated n=3 rows (0/1024/2048), corroborated by an independent cross-node run and the λ=3 replicate. We report the crashes as observations, not means; whether the ≥3072 destabilization is a fundamental congestion property or an implementation artifact under extreme reserve is untested (either way, the observable is that large reserve breaks the server — the opposite of the intended fairness).
  • Deferral defers first-token, not completion. Here E2E is decode-dominated so deferral is E2E-neutral; for TTFT-streaming-critical heavy requests the deferred first token is a real (if bounded-by-count) cost, and SRPF starvation under sustained overload (not exercised here) would need an aging or size-threshold guard.
  • Bounded to λ≤5 for a goodput pass. Above the compute ceiling no scheduling clears the SLO.
  • One model / workload family (Qwen3.5-122B hybrid-Mamba, 2-tier, heavy-tailed multi-turn). The HOL diagnosis and the defer-vs-throttle boundary should generalize to any heavy-tailed cold-prefill workload with single-chunked-request serialization, but we measure one point.

9 Conclusion

The goodput@SLO tail of a heavy-tailed LLM prefill workload is not the cold prefill of the large documents — it is the head-of-line blocking they inflict on the many tiny requests behind them, which suffer a worse tail than the documents themselves. Because the tail is HOL it is schedulable; but the class of mechanism that captures the benefit is narrow, and the intuitively fair one is excluded. Defer, don't throttle: deferring the heavy documents past the percentile clears the SLO (below severe saturation), whereas a fair reserved lane that shares the prefill budget while they keep running fails at every size and, pushed harder, collapses throughput and OOM-kills the server — because throttling maximizes, rather than removes, the blocking window. The lever is deferral by prefill size specifically. For practitioners: protect the tail by deferring the few heavy prefills, never by throttling them.
