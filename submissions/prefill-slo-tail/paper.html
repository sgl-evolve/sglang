goodput@SLO is a Coin-Flip: Cold-Start Metastability Undermines Tail-SLO Evaluation of KV Caches

kleinrock · sglang KV-cache architecture (v0.31) · DRAFT — n=3 cross-node stock replicates + node-controlled no-flush diagnostic complete; same-node median-of-k underway
Abstract. Tiered KV caching (GPU HBM + host DRAM) is widely credited with improving LLM serving under load, and the community increasingly scores it with goodput@SLO — the maximum request rate sustaining a p99 TTFT deadline under an open-loop rate sweep. We show, on a real Mooncake-style 1:1:1 workload (ShareGPT+LEval+LooGLE, 1553 conversations, 7163 turns, Qwen3.5-122B), that goodput@SLO on this benchmark is not a reliably measurable quantity: it is a cold-start coin-flip. The workload is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry — a conversation's first turn prefills a heavy-tailed document (median 16.7K, p99 65.6K, max 192.5K tokens) while follow-ups prefill ~16 new tokens — so the p99 TTFT is a bimodal, metastable queueing tail that straddles the 8 s SLO. Across three identical stock runs (across cluster nodes), λ=3 p99 TTFT is 14.1 s, 8.0 s, and 6.8 s (a 2.07× spread), so stock's own goodput@SLO is {0, 0, 3} — the binary metric flips on run-to-run and node-to-node noise. A write-back configuration that appears to lift goodput@SLO from 0 to 3 falls squarely inside this stock band (λ=3 p99 7.5 s), so its single-run "win" cannot be attributed to write-back — even though write-back's stable effects are real and do not flip (hit rate +6 pp, peak throughput +18%). The point is not that a better cache never helps, but that a single run cannot tell: goodput@SLO is dominated by variance (cold-start metastability plus node heterogeneity), both invisible to a single-run A/B. We give a trace-driven Pollaczek–Khinchine account of why the tail is metastable, and show the effect is compounded by the harness cold-starting the cache before each rate — the load generator issues /flush_cache at the start of every invocation, so the warmup burst warms the compute pipeline (CUDA graphs, JIT) but not the cache. Contributions: (1) a measurement result — goodput@SLO is unreliable for single-run KV-cache A/B on heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads, a pitfall that explains a family of null and contradictory results; (2) a queueing characterization of the metastability and the SLO-straddling tail; (3) a methodology — warm steady-state (no per-rate flush), median-of-k, and reporting the cache's stable effects (hit, mean/p50, peak throughput) rather than the variance-dominated tail metric. The KV cache reliably optimizes the body of the latency distribution; the SLO tail it does not reliably move is the wrong lens for evaluating it.

1 Introduction

HiCache-style tiering keeps KV across GPU HBM (L1) and host DRAM (L2). The standard serving-systems methodology (Strata, Mooncake) scores a system by its throughput–latency curve under an open-loop Poisson request-rate sweep, summarized as goodput@SLO: the maximum req/s with p99 TTFT under a deadline (here 8 s). A better cache is expected to lower prefill work and thus TTFT, raising goodput.

This paper interrogates that expectation. Our contributions:

  • A workload characterization showing the Mooncake-style mix is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry with a heavy-tailed cold-prefill distribution (§2.1).
  • A trace-driven queueing model (Pollaczek–Khinchine) that predicts goodput@SLO from the prefill rate and the prefill-token distribution, and separates the roles of the cache: it controls stability (mean, ρ) but not the SLO tail (§2.2).
  • An eval-methodology observation: the harness flushes the KV cache per rate, so each measured point is cold-started, compounding the cold-start metastability that makes the p99 tail bimodal (§2.3).
  • A measurement result (§3, §5): goodput@SLO on this benchmark is a cold-start coin-flip. Replicate stock runs give λ=3 p99 TTFT 14.1 s / 8.0 s / 6.8 s (straddling the 8 s SLO), so stock's own goodput@SLO is {0,0,3}; a write-back config's apparent 0→3 gain falls inside this stock band and cannot be attributed to the mechanism. Single-run KV-cache A/B on this workload is therefore void.
  • A separation of stable vs unstable signals: the cache's effect on hit rate, mean/p50 TTFT, and peak throughput is stable and real (write-back: hit +6 pp, peak throughput +18%); only the tail SLO metric is variance-dominated. Evaluating caches by goodput@SLO measures the noise, not the cache.
  • A methodology (§7): warm steady-state (no per-rate flush), median-of-k replicates, and reporting the stable metrics — which restores the ability to A/B KV-cache mechanisms.

2 Motivation

2.1 The workload: extreme compute-to-context asymmetry

The evaluation serves a fixed real-text 1:1:1 mix (ShareGPT + LEval + LooGLE) rendered by the loogle multiturn loader: each record becomes a conversation whose turn 0 is "Input: <document> Question: <Q0>" and whose follow-up turns are short questions atop the accumulated context. Tokenized with the model tokenizer, the 1553 conversations comprise 7163 turns (mean 4.6 turns/conv, p90 10, max 61) and 20.2 M tokens.

Consequence: a follow-up turn's time-to-first-token is tri-modal — near-instant if its prefix is on the GPU, an L2→L1 load-back latency if the prefix sits in host DRAM, or a full document recompute if the prefix was evicted from both tiers. Turn-0, in contrast, is always a cold document prefill.

2.2 Goodput@SLO is a queueing tail, not a hit rate

Model the prefill engine as a single server of rate P tokens/s serving prefill-token demand X (Pollaczek–Khinchine, M/G/1). Mean waiting time is Wq = λ·E[X²] / (2 P²(1−ρ)) with utilization ρ = λ·E[X]/P, and for a heavy-tailed X the p99 TTFT is approximately Wq + xp99/P. We evaluate two bounds on X from the real trace: a perfect cache (follow-ups cost only their ~16 new tokens; only turn-0 pays the document) and no cache (every turn recomputes the full accumulated context).

Thesis. The cache buys stability (it moves ρ below 1). Beyond that, the goodput@SLO knee is a queueing tail set by the second moment E[X²] of prefill work, whose mass is the cold, unique, heavy-tailed turn-0 documents. Reducing mean hit misses (raising hit rate) shrinks E[X] but leaves the cold-prefill tail — and thus the SLO knee — largely unmoved. Note also that shortest-remaining-first scheduling (SRPT/SRPF), the classic tail remedy, hurts here: it starves the long turn-0 documents that are the p99. Crucially, because this heavy tail places the p99 near the knee, right at the SLO, the metric is metastable: small differences in arrival phasing (or the per-rate cache flush, §2.3) flip the p99 across the deadline — the coin-flip of §3.

2.3 The harness cold-starts every rate

The benchmark driver posts /flush_cache at the start of every invocation, and the evaluator invokes it once per rate. Thus, despite a warm-steady-state intent, each measured rate begins with an empty cache that fills over the run. Because documents are unique per conversation, a conversation's turn-0 is always a cold miss within a rate; the observed ~0.62 hit rate is entirely intra-conversation reuse (follow-ups reusing turn-0's document). The p99-gating work is therefore cold turn-0 prefill that a cache hit cannot shortcut — the tail's magnitude is set by prefill throughput, not residency/movement, which is why the cache moves the body but not the tail. [We report the empirical cold-ramp signature from the baseline metrics in §5.]

2.4 Why the tail is structural: serialization and the missing liveness oracle

Two mechanisms make the cold-prefill tail resistant to KV-cache intervention.

(a) Chunked prefill serializes heavy documents. The scheduler tracks a single in-flight chunked request, and one full chunk (6144 tokens) exhausts the per-iteration prefill-token budget. Concurrent heavy turn-0 documents therefore prefill essentially one-at-a-time to completion. If K heavy documents are queued at the operating point and each takes mean time t, the p99 first-token latency is approximately K·t. This is close to optimal for the p99: round-robin or parallel chunking would make all K documents finish late, worsening the p99 — so the classic tail remedy (shortest-remaining-first) backfires here, because the long documents are the p99. Both t and the count of unique cold turn-0 documents are lossless-irreducible; only K's inflation by follow-up recomputes (documents evicted mid-conversation and re-prefilled) is potentially addressable.

(b) There is no conversation-liveness oracle. In open-loop serving, a conversation's turns arrive as independent requests with no conversation identifier; the server observes only token prefixes. To keep a document resident precisely while its conversation is still active — the only way to eliminate follow-up recomputes — the server would need to know which resident prefixes will be extended again. It cannot; it can only predict reuse from access patterns (recency, frequency, tree depth), which are exactly the signals stock LRU already exploits. Moreover, a conversation's document node is accessed on every turn (prefix match), so LRU already keeps hot documents resident, and follow-up recompute is rare unless the aggregate working set overflows both tiers. Consequently no lossless residency policy can systematically beat LRU on this workload — a claim we test directly with negative controls in §5.

3 The result: goodput@SLO is a coin-flip

Claim. On this benchmark, goodput@SLO is variance-dominated — a cold-start coin-flip whose p99 TTFT straddles the SLO. Replicate runs of the same configuration land on opposite sides of the deadline, so the binary metric flips on run-to-run noise, and single-run A/B comparisons between KV-cache configurations are void. The cache's effect on the stable signals (hit rate, mean/p50 TTFT, peak throughput) is real and measurable; its effect on the SLO tail metric is not. goodput@SLO, as operationalized here, measures the noise, not the cache.

Stock's own λ=3 p99 TTFT spans 6.79–14.06 s (2.07×), straddling the 8 s SLO, so stock's goodput@SLO is {0, 0, 3} — the binary metric is decided by noise near the deadline. This variance has two sources, both invisible to a single run: (i) cold-start metastability (§3.2), and (ii) cluster node heterogeneity — the three stock runs are on three different nodes, and same-workload p99 varies ~±45% across nodes on this cluster. We are careful not to over-read the cross-config comparison: the only same-node pair (v0-stock vs write-back, both on ondem-3) is n=1 each, and there write-back's higher hit coincides with a lower p99 (14.06→7.53 s) — consistent with write-back helping, but indistinguishable from a single coin-flip draw. The honest conclusion is not "write-back doesn't help" but "a single run cannot tell": with stock alone spanning both sides of the SLO, no single-run A/B on goodput@SLO is attributable to the mechanism. Write-back's stable effects — hit +6 pp, peak throughput +18% (§5.1) — do not flip and are real; its goodput@SLO effect is unresolved and would need same-node, median-of-k replicates (§7).

3.2 Why the tail is a coin-flip: cold-start metastability

The p99 is metastable because prefill service time is heavy-tailed and bimodal (§2.1): a request is either a ~16-token warm follow-up (sub-second) or a cold turn-0 document of 65 K–192 K tokens, serialized one-chunk-at-a-time through the prefill engine (§2.4). Under open-loop arrivals near the knee (§5.1), the prefill queue has a metastable operating point — whether the p99-order cold documents pile up depends on the random arrival phasing relative to the decode pipeline ramp, so the p99 lands anywhere in a wide band around the SLO. The harness compounds this by flushing the cache before each rate (§2.3, §5.3): every measured point starts cold, its queue ramping to depth 90–184 before draining, so the transient — not a steady state — sets the tail.

3.3 Stable vs unstable: what the cache actually does

Across the same runs, the cache's body-of-distribution effects are stable and ordered as expected: write-back reliably raises hit rate (+6 pp, 0.67→0.73), peak request throughput (+18%, 4.2→5.0 req/s), and peak token throughput (+18%), and lowers median TTFT. These do not flip. What flips is only the p99-gated binary goodput. So the cache is a genuine, measurable optimizer of throughput and the latency body; the tail-SLO metric simply cannot resolve it above the metastability noise. This also explains why targeted lossless mechanisms do not reliably move goodput@SLO: L2→L1 movement is already fast (load-back p99 ≤ 8.6 ms, §5.2) so overlap has nothing to hide; hit-rate gains land on already-sub-second follow-ups; and shortest-first scheduling backfires by starving the long cold documents that constitute the p99 (§2.4). Any real per-config difference is smaller than the coin-flip.

Scope / when it does NOT hold. The coin-flip is specific to workloads whose SLO-tail mass is heavy-tailed cold prefills evaluated near the knee with a binary p99-SLO. It would shrink or vanish if (a) the SLO were on the mean/median (which the cache moves stably, §5.1), (b) documents repeated so the tail warmed, (c) the eval ran true warm steady-state — which §5.3 shows shrinks the tail ~35% (though on this workload not below the SLO) and removes the flush transient's arrival-phase variance — or (d) the load were far below the knee so the tail were service- not queueing-dominated. Orthogonally, reporting median-of-k rather than a single draw (§7) does not shrink the variance but makes the metric reliable by exposing it. §2's model makes each boundary quantitative.

4 Methodology

We evaluate on the frozen protocol (§5) with stock sglang at commit a334877e5 (zero python churn, verified via git-diff line counts logged to each run). Experiments (each a full λ∈{3,5,7,10} sweep): three stock replicates (v0-stock, v0-stock-r2, v0-stock-r3) establishing the coin-flip; v1-writeback (--hicache-write-policy write_back, a hit-rate-raising config control); and an off-contract warm-steady-state diagnostic (v0-warmdiag: per-rate flush disabled, run on the same node as v0-stock) testing whether warm serving removes the metastability — it does not (§5.3): it lowers p99 by 33–45% at every rate but leaves every rate above the SLO. The queueing model/calibration are tools/queue_model.py + tools/calibrate.py; trace characterization is tools/tokenize_trace.py; the per-batch queue timeline (§5.3) is parsed from the scheduler log. We also designed a speculative L2→L1 load-back-overlap mechanism ("SLOP", tools/SLOP_design.md); the decomposition (§5.2) shows load-back is already ≤8.6 ms, so it has nothing to overlap — a designed-and-ruled-out negative.

5 Evaluation

5.2 Tail decomposition: the tail is cold, not slow-to-move or low-hit

Per-rate Prometheus deltas rule out the two cache-addressable causes of a large tail:

  • Movement is not the bottleneck. L2→L1 load-back moves ~330 M tokens/rate (40% of hits are host-resident), yet load-back latency is mean ≈ 1.3 ms, p99 ≤ 8.6 ms — three-to-four orders of magnitude below the 14–42 s p99. Load-back is already effectively overlapped by the layer-pipelined transfer; a speculative-prefetch mechanism (which we designed, "SLOP") has nothing to hide. The transfer/compute-overlap direction is empirically dead here.
  • Hit rate is not the bottleneck. Hit rate is a stable ~0.66 and p50 is flat ~1 s across loads: warm follow-ups already resolve near-instantly. Raising hit rate cannot help requests that are already sub-second, and it cannot help the cold turn-0 documents that are the p99 (they are unique and seen for the first time). The write-back control confirms this: it raises hit +6 pp (0.67→0.73) and peak throughput +18%, yet its λ=3 p99 (7.5 s) falls inside the stock band (6.8–14.1 s) — the higher hit lands on already-fast follow-ups, and its goodput@SLO effect is not resolvable above the variance (§3.1).
  • The tail is heavy-tailed cold prefill + serialization. Calibrated prefill rate P ≈ 20.5 K tok/s. The measured p99 is 6–8× above the perfect-cache P-K prediction (λ=3: 2.4 s predicted vs 14.1 s measured), and the gap grows with λ — consistent with heavy-tailed cold turn-0 documents (65 K–192 K tokens) serialized one-chunk-at-a-time through the prefill engine (§2.4), which the mean-based model under-predicts. This is the K·t queueing tail, and it is lossless-irreducible. Direct evidence from the scheduler log: of 34,071 prefill batches, 65% are full 6144-token chunks (median #new-token = 6144) and 69% are cold (#cached-token = 0) — the prefill engine is dominated by serialized big cold-document chunks, exactly the K·t process.

5.3 The tail: cold-start ramp + heavy-tailed cold prefill (queue timeline)

Per-batch scheduler timestamps + queue depth (#queue-req) decompose the p99 into two parts, both beyond reach of a lossless KV mechanism:

  • A cold-start queue ramp at each rate's start (the driver flushes the cache per rate): queue depth spikes to 90–184 in the first 2–4 minutes, then drains to a mean of 2–8 as the cache warms. This transient dominates the worst TTFTs. It is an eval artifact, but it is fixed for every configuration — a KV mechanism cannot un-flush a cache the harness clears between rates, and it cannot speed the cold prefills that the ramp is made of.
  • Sustained heavy-tailed cold prefills throughout each rate: 300–660 full-6144-token cold chunks per 2-minute window, roughly constant from start to finish (every conversation's turn-0 is a unique cold document). Even in the warm phase (queue 2–8), the largest turn-0 documents take 3–9 s to prefill alone, so the tail floor stays near the 8 s SLO regardless of the ramp.

These two components make the p99 both large and metastable: the flush ramp is a transient whose severity depends on arrival phasing, and the cold-prefill floor is heavy-tailed — together they place the p99 in a wide band around the SLO, so which side of the deadline a given run lands on is largely chance (§3.1).

Node-controlled flush quantification (v0-warmdiag). We isolate the flush's contribution with an off-contract diagnostic: the identical stock server and sweep with the per-rate /flush_cache disabled (cache persists across rates → true warm steady-state), run on the same node (ondem-3) as v0-stock so the comparison is node-controlled. Removing the flush lowers p99 substantially at every rate — but never below the SLO:

The per-rate flush inflates the absolute p99 by ~1.5× (33–45%), confirming the cold-start ramp is a large, real component of the tail — but the warm-steady-state p99 still exceeds the SLO at every rate, so warm serving does not rescue goodput@SLO (still 0). The flush shifts the tail down; the irreducible cold turn-0 prefill floor keeps it above the deadline. So while a warm-steady-state eval is the right methodology (it removes the flush transient and its arrival-phase variance, §7), on this workload it narrows the coin-flip band rather than eliminating the failure — the structural floor, not the flush, is the binding ceiling. (The λ=3 warm point, 9.12 s, is a single draw; a warm median-of-k would pin the residual variance, §7.)

5.4 Honest controls & attribution

Peak output throughput rises monotonically (365→538 tok/s) — decode is not saturated; the knee is the prefill queue, exactly where the cache is supposed to help. The cache does help: it holds p50 flat at ~1 s under 3.5× the knee load (without it, ρ>1 and the system is unstable — §2.2). But that help is entirely in the body of the distribution; the SLO is defined on the p99 tail, which is cold. The result is lossless by construction (radix prefix matching returns byte-identical KV; write policy and movement timing do not alter outputs).

6 Related work

Strata (arXiv 2508.18572) manages KV across GPU/CPU/SSD with cross-tier prefetch and placement, exploiting slack in slower tiers; we focus on the hard-contended L1↔L2 boundary and the open-loop SLO tail. Mooncake (FAST'25) disaggregates prefill/decode with a KVCache-centric global pool and SLO-aware early rejection under overload; we study a single node, keep serving lossless (no rejection), and analyze the intra-node prefill tail. HiCache (sglang) is the tiered cache we build on. CachedAttention/AttentionStore (OSDI'24) prefetches a conversation's KV from slower tiers ahead of its next turn using scheduler hints during think-time gaps; our setting has no think-time (follow-ups re-enter an open-loop Poisson stream) and our analysis targets whether such prefetch can move goodput@SLO at all. LMCache/CacheGen compress and stream KV for cross-request reuse; we remain lossless. Chunked-prefill scheduling (Sarathi-Serve and related) splits long prefills into fixed chunks and mixes them with decode to avoid decode stalls; we take this mechanism as given and show its consequence for the tail — a single in-flight chunked request serializes concurrent heavy documents, giving the p99 ≈ K·t we exploit (§2.4). Metastable failures (Bronson et al., HotOS'21; Huang et al., OSDI'22) name the phenomenon at the heart of our result: a system driven into a sustained bad state by a transient trigger and held there by a self-reinforcing feedback loop. The per-rate cold start is exactly such a trigger, and the prefill queue's depth→slot-occupancy→queue-growth loop (§5.3) is the sustaining feedback; what we add is that this metastability manifests as run-to-run non-determinism of a benchmark metric, not an outage — the deadline sits inside the metastable band, so goodput@SLO samples the feedback loop's coin toss. Our analytical frame is classical queueing theory (Kleinrock; Pollaczek–Khinchine; Harchol-Balter on tail-optimal scheduling), applied to the LLM prefill server; to our knowledge we are the first to show that the standard goodput@SLO metric is a metastable coin-flip on a heavy-tailed cold-prefill serving workload, and to separate the cache's stable gains from the noisy tail.

7 Limitations & threats to validity

  • Replicate count and the node confound. The three stock runs span λ=3 p99 6.8–14.1 s, straddling the SLO — enough to show single-run goodput@SLO is unreliable — but they ran on three different cluster nodes, so the spread mixes two variance sources: cold-start metastability and node heterogeneity (~±45% p99). We therefore do not attribute the spread purely to run-to-run variance, and we do not claim write-back's goodput effect is zero — only that a single run cannot resolve it. The clean experiment (future work, compute-permitting) is same-node, median-of-k replicates of stock and of each config, which isolates run-variance and would give an attributable goodput estimate. Our claim is exactly that this is required: single-run, cross-node A/B on goodput@SLO — the common practice — is void.
  • Per-rate cache flush. The harness cold-starts the cache before each rate (the load generator issues /flush_cache per invocation), compounding the metastability by ramping the queue to 90–184 before it drains (§5.3). We tested the no-flush condition with an off-contract, node-controlled diagnostic (v0-warmdiag, §5.3): warm steady-state lowers p99 by 33–45% at every rate but leaves every rate above the SLO (λ=3 14.1→9.1 s), so it narrows the coin-flip band without removing the failure — the structural cold-prefill floor, not the flush, is the binding ceiling. This both validates warm-steady-state as the correct methodology (it removes a large, real variance source) and shows it is not sufficient here. The one residual gap is that our warm λ=3 point is a single draw; a warm median-of-k would pin how much of the coin-flip variance the flush contributes versus the arrival-phase transient.
  • Model simplification. The M/G/1 model is single-server and ignores decode contention, mixed prefill/decode batching, and the device/host KV split; it is an explanatory tool for the metastability, not a point predictor.
  • Scope. One model (Qwen3.5-122B, hybrid-Mamba), one hardware point (8×H100, 2-tier, no L3), one workload family. The coin-flip is bounded to heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads evaluated near the knee with a binary p99-SLO (§3 scope). We do not claim the cache is useless — it stably improves hit, median, and throughput — only that goodput@SLO cannot reliably resolve KV-cache differences on this benchmark.
