goodput@SLO is a Coin-Flip: Cold-Start Metastability Undermines Tail-SLO Evaluation of KV Caches

kleinrock · sglang KV-cache architecture (v0.31) · measurement study — cross-node (n=3) + same-node median-of-k (stock, write-back & warm) + no-flush diagnostic + a first-principles minimal model
Abstract. Tiered KV caching (GPU HBM + host DRAM) is widely credited with improving LLM serving under load, and the community increasingly scores it with goodput@SLO — the maximum request rate sustaining a p99 TTFT deadline under an open-loop rate sweep. We show, on a real Mooncake-style 1:1:1 workload (ShareGPT+LEval+LooGLE, 1553 conversations, 7163 turns, Qwen3.5-122B), that goodput@SLO on this benchmark is not a reliably measurable quantity: it is a cold-start coin-flip. The workload is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry — a conversation's first turn prefills a heavy-tailed document (median 16.7K, p99 65.6K, max 192.5K tokens) while follow-ups prefill ~16 new tokens — so the p99 TTFT is a bimodal, metastable queueing tail that straddles the 8 s SLO. Across three identical stock runs (across cluster nodes), λ=3 p99 TTFT is 14.1 s, 8.0 s, and 6.8 s (a 2.07× spread), so stock's own goodput@SLO is {0, 0, 3} — the binary metric flips on run-to-run and node-to-node noise. A same-node control — K=5 replicates of the identical stock configuration on one fixed node — is even starker: λ=3 p99 spans 6.5–25.8 s (a 4.0× spread, two pass / three fail), so the flip persists and widens with the node held constant, isolating run-to-run cold-start metastability (not node heterogeneity) as the cause; it reproduces on every one of three nodes tested (each node's λ=3 p99 straddles the 8 s SLO). A write-back configuration that appears to lift goodput@SLO from 0 to 3 falls squarely inside this stock band (λ=3 p99 7.5 s), so its single-run "win" cannot be attributed to write-back — even though write-back's stable effects are real and do not flip (hit rate +6 pp, peak throughput +18%). Running the same-node K=5 protocol for write-back too, even median-of-k cannot separate stock from write-back on goodput@SLO (both pass 2 of 5; 0.4σ), though on the same runs the stable metrics separate the two configs at 7–9σ (hit +7 pp, median TTFT −64 ms) — the information to rank the caches is present all along, and the binary tail metric simply discards it. The point is not that a better cache never helps, but that a single run cannot tell — and even median-of-k cannot tell on the tail: goodput@SLO is dominated by variance (cold-start metastability plus node heterogeneity), both invisible to a single-run A/B. We give a trace-driven Pollaczek–Khinchine account of why the tail is metastable, and show the effect is compounded by the harness cold-starting the cache before each rate — the load generator issues /flush_cache at the start of every invocation, so the warmup burst warms the compute pipeline (CUDA graphs, JIT) but not the cache. Contributions: (1) a measurement result — goodput@SLO is unreliable for single-run KV-cache A/B on heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads, a pitfall that would render such single-run A/B comparisons null or contradictory; (2) a queueing characterization of the metastability and the SLO-straddling tail; (3) a methodology and a reusable diagnostic — a workload- agnostic σ/m test for when goodput@SLO is trustworthy at all, warm steady-state (no per-rate flush), same-node median-of-k, and (since even median-of-k cannot resolve the tail here) reporting the cache's stable effects (hit, mean/p50, peak throughput) and the p99 distribution rather than the variance-dominated binary metric. The KV cache reliably optimizes the body of the latency distribution; the SLO tail — which we find is movable by request scheduling (SRPF: λ=5 fail→pass) yet blind to caching (§5.6) — is the wrong lens for evaluating the cache.

1 Introduction

HiCache-style tiering keeps KV across GPU HBM (L1) and host DRAM (L2). The standard serving-systems methodology (Strata, Mooncake) scores a system by its throughput–latency curve under an open-loop Poisson request-rate sweep, summarized as goodput@SLO: the maximum req/s with p99 TTFT under a deadline (here 8 s). A better cache is expected to lower prefill work and thus TTFT, raising goodput.

This paper interrogates that expectation. Our contributions:

  • A workload characterization showing the Mooncake-style mix is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry with a heavy-tailed cold-prefill distribution (§2.1).
  • A trace-driven queueing model (Pollaczek–Khinchine) that predicts goodput@SLO from the prefill rate and the prefill-token distribution, and separates the roles of the cache: it controls stability (mean, ρ) but not the SLO tail (§2.2). A minimal transient Monte-Carlo model, parameterized only by the measured document distribution, reproduces the coin-flip from first principles and maps its phase boundary (a coin-flip band at ρ ≈ 0.5–0.7), showing it is generic to the workload class, not an sglang artifact (§5.5).
  • An eval-methodology observation: the harness flushes the KV cache per rate, so each measured point is cold-started, compounding the cold-start metastability that makes the p99 tail bimodal (§2.3).
  • A measurement result (§3, §5): goodput@SLO on this benchmark is a cold-start coin-flip. Cross-node stock runs give λ=3 p99 TTFT 14.1 / 8.0 / 6.8 s (straddling the 8 s SLO), and a same-node control (K=5 replicates on one fixed node) is starker still — λ=3 p99 spans 6.5–25.8 s (4.0×, two pass / three fail) — so the flip persists with the node held constant, isolating run-to-run metastability as the cause. A write-back config's apparent 0→3 gain falls inside this stock band and cannot be attributed to the mechanism. Single-run KV-cache A/B on this workload is therefore void.
  • A separation of stable vs unstable signals: the cache's effect on hit rate, mean/p50 TTFT, and peak throughput is stable and real (write-back: hit +6 pp, peak throughput +18%); only the tail SLO metric is variance-dominated. Evaluating caches by goodput@SLO measures the noise, not the cache.
  • A methodology + a reusable diagnostic (§4, §7): a workload-agnostic σ/m variance-domination test — trust goodput@SLO only when its run-to-run p99 σ is small versus the SLO margin m = |SLO − median(p99)| — plus warm steady-state and same-node median-of-k. Since we find even median-of-k cannot resolve KV configs on the tail here (§3.1), the reliable comparison uses the cache's stable metrics (hit, median TTFT, throughput) and the p99 distribution, not a single binary deadline.

2 Motivation

2.1 The workload: extreme compute-to-context asymmetry

The evaluation serves a fixed real-text 1:1:1 mix (ShareGPT + LEval + LooGLE) rendered by the loogle multiturn loader: each record becomes a conversation whose turn 0 is "Input: <document> Question: <Q0>" and whose follow-up turns are short questions atop the accumulated context. Tokenized with the model tokenizer, the 1553 conversations comprise 7163 turns (mean 4.6 turns/conv, p90 10, max 61) and 20.2 M tokens.

Consequence: a follow-up turn's time-to-first-token is tri-modal — near-instant if its prefix is on the GPU, an L2→L1 load-back latency if the prefix sits in host DRAM, or a full document recompute if the prefix was evicted from both tiers. Turn-0, in contrast, is always a cold document prefill.

2.2 Goodput@SLO is a queueing tail, not a hit rate

Model the prefill engine as a single server of rate P tokens/s serving prefill-token demand X (Pollaczek–Khinchine, M/G/1). Mean waiting time is Wq = λ·E[X²] / (2 P²(1−ρ)) with utilization ρ = λ·E[X]/P, and for a heavy-tailed X the p99 TTFT is approximately Wq + xp99/P. We evaluate two bounds on X from the real trace: a perfect cache (follow-ups cost only their ~16 new tokens; only turn-0 pays the document) and no cache (every turn recomputes the full accumulated context).

Thesis. The cache buys stability (it moves ρ below 1). Beyond that, the goodput@SLO knee is a queueing tail set by the second moment E[X²] of prefill work, whose mass is the cold, unique, heavy-tailed turn-0 documents. Reducing mean hit misses (raising hit rate) shrinks E[X] but leaves the cold-prefill tail — and thus the SLO knee — largely unmoved by caching. [Correction, added post-submission — §2.4 and §5.6.] An earlier version of this paper argued that shortest-remaining-first scheduling would also hurt "because the long documents are the p99." A direct same-node A/B refutes that: SRPF is a large win on this tail (λ=5 p99 23.9 s→7.0 s, 0/5→5/5 pass). The tail is schedulable by request reordering, even though it stays beyond the reach of KV caching. This sharpens the thesis rather than weakening it: goodput@SLO is movable by scheduling yet unresolvable for caching. Crucially, because this heavy tail places the p99 near the knee, right at the SLO, the metric is metastable for the caching comparisons this paper concerns: small differences in arrival phasing (or the per-rate cache flush, §2.3) flip the p99 across the deadline — the coin-flip of §3.

2.3 The harness cold-starts every rate

The benchmark driver posts /flush_cache at the start of every invocation, and the evaluator invokes it once per rate. Thus, despite a warm-steady-state intent, each measured rate begins with an empty cache that fills over the run. Because documents are unique per conversation, a conversation's turn-0 is always a cold miss within a rate; the observed ~0.62 hit rate is entirely intra-conversation reuse (follow-ups reusing turn-0's document). The p99-gating work is therefore cold turn-0 prefill that a cache hit cannot shortcut — the tail's magnitude is set by prefill throughput, not residency/movement, which is why the cache moves the body but not the tail. [We report the empirical cold-ramp signature from the baseline metrics in §5.]

2.4 Why the tail is structural: serialization and the missing liveness oracle

Two mechanisms make the cold-prefill tail resistant to KV-cache intervention.

(a) Chunked prefill serializes heavy documents. The scheduler tracks a single in-flight chunked request, and one full chunk (6144 tokens) exhausts the per-iteration prefill-token budget. Concurrent heavy turn-0 documents therefore prefill essentially one-at-a-time to completion, and requests that arrive during that window wait behind them — head-of-line blocking. The cold turn-0 prefill work itself (the per-document time t and the count of unique cold documents) is lossless-irreducible: no KV cache can shortcut it, which is this paper's point about caching. [Correction, added post-submission.] An earlier version further claimed this serialization made the p99 "close to optimal" and that shortest-remaining-first (SRPF) would backfire "because the long documents are the p99." That was an unverified prediction and it is wrong. A direct same-node A/B (§5.6) shows SRPF is a large win: at λ=5 it cuts p99 TTFT from 23.9 s (0/5 pass) to 7.0 s (5/5 pass, Fisher p≈0.008) and mean TTFT by 43%. The reason: the workload has only ~3–4 documents above 100K tokens, so the heavy documents lie beyond the 99th percentile; the p99 is the head-of-line victims queued behind their serialized prefill, and SRPF reorders those victims ahead (deferring the few heavy documents past the p99). So the tail is schedulable by request reordering — it is not irreducible in general. What remains irreducible is only the caching lever: caching cannot move this tail, because the gating work is cold turn-0 prefill. (SRPF is a known technique, not a contribution of this paper; we report it only to correct the record and to sharpen the caching claim.)

(b) There is no conversation-liveness oracle. In open-loop serving, a conversation's turns arrive as independent requests with no conversation identifier; the server observes only token prefixes. To keep a document resident precisely while its conversation is still active — the only way to eliminate follow-up recomputes — the server would need to know which resident prefixes will be extended again. It cannot; it can only predict reuse from access patterns (recency, frequency, tree depth), which are exactly the signals stock LRU already exploits. Moreover, a conversation's document node is accessed on every turn (prefix match), so LRU already keeps hot documents resident, and follow-up recompute is rare unless the aggregate working set overflows both tiers. Consequently no lossless residency policy can systematically beat LRU on this workload — a claim we test directly with negative controls in §5.

3 The result: goodput@SLO is a coin-flip

Claim. On this benchmark, goodput@SLO is variance-dominated — a cold-start coin-flip whose p99 TTFT straddles the SLO. Replicate runs of the same configuration land on opposite sides of the deadline, so the binary metric flips on run-to-run noise, and single-run A/B comparisons between KV-cache configurations are void. The cache's effect on the stable signals (hit rate, mean/p50 TTFT, peak throughput) is real and measurable; its effect on the SLO tail metric is not. goodput@SLO, as operationalized here, measures the noise, not the cache.

Stock's own λ=3 p99 TTFT spans 6.79–14.06 s (2.07×), straddling the 8 s SLO, so stock's goodput@SLO is {0, 0, 3} — the binary metric is decided by noise near the deadline. This variance has two sources, both invisible to a single run: (i) cold-start metastability (§3.2), and (ii) cluster node heterogeneity — the three stock runs are on three different nodes, and same-workload p99 varies ~±45% across nodes on this cluster. We are careful not to over-read this cross-config, cross-node comparison (n=1 each, different nodes); below we resolve it properly with same-node median-of-k for both stock and write-back. The honest conclusion is not "write-back doesn't help" but "a single run cannot tell": with stock alone spanning both sides of the SLO, no single-run A/B on goodput@SLO is attributable to the mechanism. Write-back's stable effects — hit +6 pp, peak throughput +18% (§5.1) — do not flip and are real.

Same-node replication resolves the node confound. The four runs above sit on different nodes, so their spread mixes cold-start metastability with node heterogeneity (§7). To isolate the two we ran a median-of-k study: K=5 replicates of the identical stock configuration, back-to-back on one node (ondem-3, the same node as v0-stock). Server, workload, configuration, and node are all fixed; each replicate is an independent cold-cache λ=3 draw (the load generator flushes at the start of every run). Because λ≥5 reliably fails the SLO (§5.1), goodput@SLO is decided entirely by λ=3, so the spread of these five same-node p99 values is the goodput coin-flip with node held constant.

Node, workload, and configuration are constant, so this isolates run-to-run cold-start metastability as the coin-flip's cause and removes the node-heterogeneity confound from the central claim. Two details confirm the mechanism: (i) the p50 TTFT is flat at ~0.54 s across all five draws while the p99 swings 4× — the variance lives entirely in the tail (the cache serves the body identically every run), the signature of a metastable queue transient rather than a steady-state difference; and (ii) the run-to-run standard deviation (8.0 s) exceeds the distance from the median to the SLO (1.4 s), the formal condition for a variance-dominated metric that a single run cannot resolve. This same-node coin-flip reproduces on a second node (node1-2: K=5 λ=3 p99 6.5–21.9 s, median 7.2 s, 3/5 pass, σ/m = 7.8), so it is a general property, not an artifact of ondem-3 (§7). Cross-node heterogeneity (§7) is an additional variance source on top of this.

Does median-of-k let us A/B a config that a single run cannot? Yet write-back is unambiguously the better cache on every stable axis (hit +7 pp, 0.66→0.73; p50 −64 ms) and even has a tighter tail (std 8.0→2.7 s; it never produced stock's 25.8 s catastrophic draw). All three real, consistent improvements are invisible to the binary goodput@SLO. This validates the paper's thesis by construction: the recommended median-of-k does not rescue goodput@SLO for KV-cache A/B — the tail stays too variance-dominated even at K=5 — so a correct evaluation must report the cache's stable effects (hit, median TTFT, throughput) and, if the tail matters, its variance, rather than a single binary deadline. Write-back is itself a coin-flip on goodput@SLO (2 / 5 pass, straddling the SLO), confirming the phenomenon is a property of the workload, not of any one configuration.

Taking the same K=5 same-node runs and comparing stock to write-back on each metric — separation measured in pooled standard deviations — the stable metrics are not variance-dominated and cleanly discriminate the two caches, exactly where goodput@SLO cannot:

The information to rank the caches is present in the measurement all along — the binary tail metric simply discards it. This is the constructive core of the paper: evaluate KV caches on their stable, low-variance effects (§4's σ/m ≪ 1 metrics), which resolve precisely the comparison the coin-flipping tail cannot.

3.2 Why the tail is a coin-flip: cold-start metastability

The p99 is metastable because prefill service time is heavy-tailed and bimodal (§2.1): a request is either a ~16-token warm follow-up (sub-second) or a cold turn-0 document of 65 K–192 K tokens, serialized one-chunk-at-a-time through the prefill engine (§2.4). Under open-loop arrivals near the knee (§5.1), the prefill queue has a metastable operating point — whether the p99-order cold documents pile up depends on the random arrival phasing relative to the decode pipeline ramp, so the p99 lands anywhere in a wide band around the SLO. The harness compounds this by flushing the cache before each rate (§2.3, §5.3): every measured point starts cold, its queue ramping to depth 90–184 before draining, so the transient — not a steady state — sets the tail.

3.3 Stable vs unstable: what the cache actually does

Across the same runs, the cache's body-of-distribution effects are stable and ordered as expected: write-back reliably raises hit rate (+6 pp, 0.67→0.73), peak request throughput (+18%, 4.2→5.0 req/s), and peak token throughput (+18%), and lowers median TTFT. These do not flip. What flips is only the p99-gated binary goodput. So the cache is a genuine, measurable optimizer of throughput and the latency body; the tail-SLO metric simply cannot resolve it above the metastability noise. This also explains why the two caching levers we can target do not reliably move goodput@SLO: L2→L1 movement is already fast (load-back p99 ≤ 8.6 ms, §5.2) so overlap has nothing to hide; and hit-rate gains land on already-sub-second follow-ups. [Correction, added post-submission — see §5.6.] An earlier version claimed scheduling could not help either — that shortest-first "backfires." A direct A/B refutes this: SRPF cleanly raises goodput@SLO at λ=5 (0/5→5/5 pass, p99 23.9→7.0 s). Note this is consistent with, not contrary to, our σ/m diagnostic (§4): λ=5 is in the low-variance reliable-fail regime (σ/m<1), so a large effect is resolvable there; it is only small effects near the knee — where the caching comparisons live and σ/m>1 — that goodput@SLO cannot resolve. The corrected claim is therefore sharper: goodput@SLO can resolve a large scheduling effect in its low-variance regime, but cannot resolve the caching effects this paper concerns (they are small and sit at the coin-flip knee). That is why caching must be evaluated on stable metrics — the measurement contribution here — and why goodput@SLO is movable by scheduling yet blind to caching. Any real per-cache-config difference is smaller than the coin-flip.

Scope / when it does NOT hold. The coin-flip is specific to workloads whose SLO-tail mass is heavy-tailed cold prefills evaluated near the knee with a binary p99-SLO. It would shrink or vanish if (a) the SLO were on the mean/median (which the cache moves stably, §5.1), (b) documents repeated so the tail warmed, (c) the eval ran true warm steady-state — which §5.3 shows shrinks the tail ~35% (though on this workload not below the SLO) and removes the flush transient's arrival-phase variance — or (d) the load were far below the knee so the tail were service- not queueing-dominated. Orthogonally, reporting median-of-k rather than a single draw (§7) does not shrink the variance but makes the metric reliable by exposing it. §2's model makes each boundary quantitative. Finally, the coin-flip is not an artifact of the specific 8 s SLO: by the σ/m criterion (§4) it arises for any deadline set within ∼σ of the operating median, and one places the SLO at the operating knee — where the median sits — precisely to measure capacity. A deadline far from the knee is reliable but uninformative (it always passes or always fails); the regime where goodput@SLO is used is exactly the regime where it is a coin-flip.

4 Methodology

We evaluate on the frozen protocol (§5) with stock sglang at commit a334877e5 (zero python churn, verified via git-diff line counts logged to each run). Experiments (each a full λ∈{3,5,7,10} sweep): three stock replicates (v0-stock, v0-stock-r2, v0-stock-r3) establishing the coin-flip; a same-node median-of-k control (v0-medk: K=5 stock λ=3 draws back-to-back on one node, ondem-3), which isolates run-variance from node heterogeneity and shows the flip persists at a fixed node (§3.1); v1-writeback (--hicache-write-policy write_back, a hit-rate-raising config control); and an off-contract warm-steady-state diagnostic (v0-warmdiag: per-rate flush disabled, run on the same node as v0-stock) testing whether warm serving removes the metastability — it does not (§5.3): it lowers p99 by 33–45% at every rate but leaves every rate above the SLO. The queueing model/calibration are tools/queue_model.py + tools/calibrate.py; trace characterization is tools/tokenize_trace.py; the per-batch queue timeline (§5.3) is parsed from the scheduler log. We also designed a speculative L2→L1 load-back-overlap mechanism ("SLOP", tools/SLOP_design.md); the decomposition (§5.2) shows load-back is already ≤8.6 ms, so it has nothing to overlap — a designed-and-ruled-out negative.

A reusable diagnostic: is your goodput@SLO trustworthy? Our results yield a simple test any evaluator can apply before reporting goodput@SLO, on any workload. Measure the run-to-run standard deviation σ of the gating-rate p99 TTFT with a small same-node median-of-k (k≈5), and compare it to the margin m = |SLO − median(p99)| at that rate. If σ ≳ m, goodput@SLO is variance-dominated: a single run — and even median-of-k — cannot resolve configuration differences, and the metric should be replaced by the stable metrics (hit rate, median TTFT, throughput) plus, if the tail matters, the p99 distribution rather than a binary deadline. In our data the gating rate is λ=3, and every configuration we measured fails the test: stock-flush has σ = 8.0 s against m = 1.4 s (σ/m ≈ 5.7, deeply variance-dominated); the tighter write-back config σ = 2.7 s against m = 3.1 s (σ/m ≈ 0.9, on the edge); and even warm-steady-state stock (no per-rate flush) σ = 3.2 s against m = 2.3 s (σ/m ≈ 1.4, still variance-dominated) — warm serving lowers σ/m from 5.7 to 1.4 but does not clear the σ/m < 1 bar (§5.3). The test is workload-agnostic — it follows from the p99 being a metastable queueing tail (§2.2) and needs only k measurements, no model — and it "flips safe": it certifies goodput@SLO as trustworthy only when the deadline sits many run-to-run standard deviations from the typical p99, i.e. far from the queueing knee.

5.2 Tail decomposition: the tail is cold, not slow-to-move or low-hit

Per-rate Prometheus deltas rule out the two cache-addressable causes of a large tail:

  • Movement is not the bottleneck. L2→L1 load-back moves ~330 M tokens/rate (40% of hits are host-resident), yet load-back latency is mean ≈ 1.3 ms, p99 ≤ 8.6 ms — three-to-four orders of magnitude below the 14–42 s p99. Load-back is already effectively overlapped by the layer-pipelined transfer; a speculative-prefetch mechanism (which we designed, "SLOP") has nothing to hide. The transfer/compute-overlap direction is empirically dead here.
  • Hit rate is not the bottleneck. Hit rate is a stable ~0.66 and p50 is flat ~1 s across loads: warm follow-ups already resolve near-instantly. Raising hit rate cannot help requests that are already sub-second, and it cannot shortcut the cold turn-0 prefill that drives the p99 (those documents are unique and seen for the first time; whether they are the p99 themselves or the head-of-line work their victims wait behind — §5.6 — caching cannot touch it). The write-back control confirms this: it raises hit +6 pp (0.67→0.73) and peak throughput +18%, yet its λ=3 p99 (7.5 s) falls inside the stock band (6.8–14.1 s) — the higher hit lands on already-fast follow-ups, and its goodput@SLO effect is not resolvable above the variance (§3.1).
  • The tail is heavy-tailed cold prefill + serialization. Calibrated prefill rate P ≈ 20.5 K tok/s. The measured p99 is 6–8× above the perfect-cache P-K prediction (λ=3: 2.4 s predicted vs 14.1 s measured), and the gap grows with λ — consistent with heavy-tailed cold turn-0 documents (65 K–192 K tokens) serialized one-chunk-at-a-time through the prefill engine (§2.4), which the mean-based model under-predicts. This is the K·t queueing tail, and it is lossless-irreducible. Direct evidence from the scheduler log: of 34,071 prefill batches, 65% are full 6144-token chunks (median #new-token = 6144) and 69% are cold (#cached-token = 0) — the prefill engine is dominated by serialized big cold-document chunks, exactly the K·t process.

The metastability is localized to the prefill (TTFT) tail. Across the five same-node runs of §3.1, the coin-flip is concentrated exactly where the mechanism predicts. The gating TTFT p99 has coefficient of variation CV = 0.65 (3.98× spread), whereas the decode-influenced tail metrics are ~3× steadier: mean E2E latency CV = 0.22 (1.9× spread) and P99 inter-token latency CV = 0.19 (1.6×). The prefill queue — not decode — is the metastable component, so it is the TTFT-p99-gated goodput@SLO specifically that coin-flips. This both corroborates the K·t prefill-serialization mechanism (§2.4) and answers the metric-generality question: the instability is a prefill-tail property, most acute for precisely the metric the community uses to score KV caches.

5.3 The tail: cold-start ramp + heavy-tailed cold prefill (queue timeline)

Per-batch scheduler timestamps + queue depth (#queue-req) decompose the p99 into two parts, both beyond reach of a lossless KV mechanism:

  • A cold-start queue ramp at each rate's start (the driver flushes the cache per rate): queue depth spikes to 90–184 in the first 2–4 minutes, then drains to a mean of 2–8 as the cache warms. This transient dominates the worst TTFTs. It is an eval artifact, but it is fixed for every configuration — a KV mechanism cannot un-flush a cache the harness clears between rates, and it cannot speed the cold prefills that the ramp is made of.
  • Sustained heavy-tailed cold prefills throughout each rate: 300–660 full-6144-token cold chunks per 2-minute window, roughly constant from start to finish (every conversation's turn-0 is a unique cold document). Even in the warm phase (queue 2–8), the largest turn-0 documents take 3–9 s to prefill alone, so the tail floor stays near the 8 s SLO regardless of the ramp.

These two components make the p99 both large and metastable: the flush ramp is a transient whose severity depends on arrival phasing, and the cold-prefill floor is heavy-tailed — together they place the p99 in a wide band around the SLO, so which side of the deadline a given run lands on is largely chance (§3.1).

Node-controlled flush quantification (v0-warmdiag). Warm-steady-state is therefore a necessary but insufficient part of the fix on this workload — the residual cold turn-0 prefill floor keeps the tail metastable. 5.4 Honest controls & attribution

Peak output throughput rises monotonically (365→538 tok/s) — decode is not saturated; the knee is the prefill queue, exactly where the cache is supposed to help. The cache does help: it holds p50 flat at ~1 s under 3.5× the knee load (without it, ρ>1 and the system is unstable — §2.2). But that help is entirely in the body of the distribution; the SLO is defined on the p99 tail, which is cold. The result is lossless by construction (radix prefix matching returns byte-identical KV; write policy and movement timing do not alter outputs).

5.5 Generality, and the limits of replication

Is the coin-flip specific to sglang, or a generic consequence of open-loop scheduling of heavy-tailed cold prefills? We test this with a minimal transient-queue Monte-Carlo (tools/coinflip_sim.py) that strips away the KV cache, decode interleaving, and batching, keeping only the hypothesized mechanism: Poisson(λ₀) arrivals of cold documents whose prefill demand is drawn from the measured turn-0 token distribution, served by a single FCFS server of the calibrated rate P = 20.5 K tok/s (chunked prefill serializes concurrent heavy docs, §2.4), starting from an empty/cold queue. This is a forward prediction: it is parameterized only by the independently-measured document distribution and P — nothing is fit to the observed coin-flip. We draw 200 independent arrival realizations per load and record the distribution of λ=3-style p99 TTFT (the variance-domination ratio σ/m of §4 uses m = |SLO − median|).

The minimal model reproduces the coin-flip from first principles. There is a distinct coin-flip band at ρ ≈ 0.5–0.7 where the p99 straddles the SLO (σ/m > 1, pass fraction strictly between 0 and 1), bracketed by a reliable-pass regime below and a reliable-fail regime above — a phase diagram for when goodput@SLO is unreliable. Both the existence of the coin-flip and its magnitude match the measurement: in the band the model's p99 spans ~6–22 s with median ~7–10 s, versus the measured same-node medk (median 9.4 s, 6.5–25.8 s, §3.1). Because the model contains no cache, scheduler, or model-specific detail, the coin-flip is a generic property of the workload class — heavy-tailed cold prefills scheduled open-loop near the queueing knee — not an artifact of sglang or this model.

And the real system exhibits the predicted phase diagram. The contract's rate set (λ ∈ {3, 5, 7, 10}) samples only the coin-flip band (λ=3) and the reliable-fail regime (λ ≥ 5); the reliable-pass regime the model predicts below the band is never measured by the eval. An off-contract low-λ diagnostic (same stock server, node 1-2; tools/phasemap_eval.sh, an off-contract rate probe like warmdiag/medk — the headline eval is unchanged) fills it in: at λ=1.5 the p99 TTFT is 3.36 and 3.35 s (n=2) — comfortably below the SLO and, unlike the λ=3 coin-flip, tight (the two draws differ by 16 ms). So the real system reproduces all three regimes the model predicts — reliable-pass (λ=1.5, p99 ≈ 3.35 s, low variance) → coin-flip band (λ=3, p99 6.1–36.7 s straddling the SLO) → reliable-fail (λ ≥ 5, §5.1) — and, decisively, the run-to-run variance collapses away from the knee exactly as the σ/m diagnostic (§4) requires: goodput@SLO is variance-dominated only inside the band. This is the measured phase diagram, matching the forward model's, on the real serving system.

How many replicates would rescue goodput@SLO? The model says: unboundedly many, near the knee. We use the model to ask what the empirical triad could not: how large must the median-of-k be for a reliable (95%-reproducible) goodput@SLO classification? Because a median-of-k estimate's sampling error shrinks only as ∼1/√k while the quantity being classified is the sign of (median − SLO), the required k grows as the operating median approaches the deadline and diverges at it:

This unifies the methodology: median-of-k rescues goodput@SLO only when the operating median is comfortably away from the SLO (σ/m ≪ 1, small k); when the operating point sits at the knee — precisely where goodput@SLO is used to characterize a serving system — no practical number of replicates suffices, because one cannot reliably classify the sign of a quantity whose true value is zero. This is exactly why K=5 could not resolve write-back (§3.1): its median sits near the SLO. The only reliable recourse there is to report the cache's stable metrics (§3.1 capstone), not the tail deadline. Scope/honesty: this is an illustrative minimal model, not a point predictor — it omits decode contention and the multiturn/cache structure, and models the heavy prefill load with turn-0 documents only, so the exact ρ that the frozen eval occupies is approximate (the eval's turn-0-only arrival rate sits just below the band; the real prefill load, including follow-up tokens, pushes it in). The claim is qualitative and mechanistic: the coin-flip band exists and is generic.

The measurement corroborates the divergence. We check the model's central claim against real data by bootstrapping the required median-of-k directly from the same-node stock draws (deterministic, tools/required_k_empirical.py). On the two nodes we replicated, the node whose operating median sits nearer the SLO needs more replicates — exactly the model's prediction. node1-2 (n=5, median 7.2 s — just 0.8 s below the SLO, σ/m = 7.0, 3/5 passing) is unresolved even at k = 25: sitting at the knee, no practical number of replicates yields a reproducible verdict. ondem-3 sits further out (n=9, median 14.1 s, 2/9 passing, σ/m ≈ 1.5) and does resolve — to goodput=0 — but only at k ≈ 9. Either way, this pinpoints why the same-node K=5 experiments (§3.1) could not separate the configurations: five replicates is below what either operating point needs.

The empirical required-k is itself sampling-sensitive — and a controlled experiment pins down why. An earlier ondem-3 server session (n=6, 2/6 passing) placed the node nearer the knee with a bootstrap k ≈ 23; adding a second same-node session whose three draws happened to land in the 24–30 s fail regime (0/3) moved the pooled operating point deeper into failure and dropped the estimate to k ≈ 9. We initially wondered whether this cross-session shift was an additional, launch-level variance layer. A controlled experiment settles it in the negative: three back-to-back server launches in one exclusive allocation on one node (N=3 sessions × K=3 stock λ=3 draws each, tools/session_var_analyze.py) show that within-session run-to-run variance overwhelmingly dominates. The three session means are nearly identical (14.6, 17.7, 17.4 s), yet every session spans the full coin-flip range (6.1–22.1, 7.7–36.7, 12.8–26.7 s); the across-session variance component is just 2% of the within-session component (σacross = 1.4 s vs σwithin = 11.5 s). There is no separable launch-level layer. The earlier cross-allocation difference was within-session sampling (a single session can draw three high values by chance, as session 2 does here) plus node state, not a genuine server-launch effect — so we retract that tentative third layer. This reinforces the core result: the coin-flip is fundamentally run-to-run, which is exactly why required-k, like goodput@SLO itself, is hard to pin down — a single server's draws already straddle the SLO. The small per-node pools (n = 5–9) make the exact k coarse; the robust, direction-of-effect result — nearer the SLO needs more replicates, and no practical k rescues the knee — is what carries.

5.6 Correction: the tail is schedulable (SRPF A/B)

After the initial submission we directly measured shortest-remaining-prefill-first (SRPF) scheduling, which §2.4 had predicted (without measuring) would backfire. The prediction was wrong. Same-node A/B on one node, warm steady-state, byte-identical flags except --schedule-policy srpf (raw runs runs/v3-srpf-ab):

SRPF turns λ=5 from reliable-fail (0/5) to pass (5/5, every replicate under the SLO), cutting p99 TTFT ~71% and mean TTFT ~43%, at equal request throughput (Fisher exact p≈0.008; the two distributions are disjoint). SRPF also sharply improves the knee (λ=3: median 6.7 s, 2/3 pass, 6.2–8.1 s) — vs fcfs's wide coin-flip of §3 (0/3, 10.8–34 s) — lowering it and tightening the spread to ~1.3× (fcfs 3.2×); it largely stabilizes the knee, though one replicate still grazed the SLO. We headline λ=5, where n=5 (5/5). It improves the whole TTFT distribution (mean, p90, p99), so this is genuine shortest-job-first flow-time reduction, not a p99 artifact. The mechanism: with only ~3–4 turn-0 documents above 100K tokens, the heavy documents lie beyond the 99th percentile, so the fcfs p99 is the head-of-line victims queued behind their serialized prefill; SRPF reorders those victims ahead (deferring the few heavy documents past the p99). This corrects §2.4's "the long documents are the p99" and §3.3's "shortest-first backfires." Two caveats keep it consistent with the paper's thesis: (i) it is resolvable because λ=5 is the low-variance reliable-fail regime (σ/m<1, §4) and the effect is large — near the knee (λ=3) the coin-flip persists; (ii) SRPF is a known technique (shortest-job-first; cf. Sarathi/Harchol-Balter, §6), not a contribution of this paper. The load-bearing claim — that KV caching cannot move this tail and so must be evaluated on stable metrics — is unchanged and, if anything, sharpened: goodput@SLO is movable by scheduling yet blind to caching.

The benefit requires deferral, not fairness. SRPF's win comes from deferring the few heavy documents' first-token latency — their end-to-end latency is essentially unchanged (mean E2E 44.7 vs 45.0 s; E2E is decode-dominated, E2E ≫ TTFT). We asked whether a fair, non-starving alternative could capture the same p99 win without deferring the heavy documents: a reserved short-prefill lane that, while a heavy document is mid-prefill, holds back a slice of the per-iteration chunk budget so short waiting requests prefill alongside it (the heavy document still makes progress every iteration). It does not work. On the same-node A/B (raw runs runs/v4-shortlane-ab, v5-shortlane-l3), a 2048-token reserve worsens the p99 at both loads — λ=5: 24.2 s→42.4 s (+75%, n=3, disjoint distributions); λ=3: 8.1 s→19.5 s (+141%, n=3) — while improving the median (−19 to −25%). The reason is instructive: a thin reserve trickles a few victims through but keeps the heavy document occupying the pipeline (now over more iterations), so it lengthens the congestion window instead of clearing it. A reserve-size ablation (same node, λ=5, raw runs/v6-shortlane-abl) confirms the failure is fundamental, not a thin-regime artifact, and that there is no crossover: reserve=1024 is neutral (p99 22.3 vs 23.3 s), reserve=2048 harmful, and reserve=4096 catastrophic (p99 ≈100 s, request throughput collapsing 3.6→2.0 req/s, and the server itself OOM-killed mid-run as the congestion backlog exhausted memory — only one replicate completed). Larger reserves throttle the heavy document harder, so it occupies the prefill pipeline for proportionally more iterations (≈94 at reserve=4096) — the opposite of SRPF, which defers the document rather than running it slowly. Throttling-while-running is strictly worse than both FCFS and SRPF. The goodput lever is therefore inseparable from deferring the heavy documents' first token (an E2E-neutral reordering); a mechanism that keeps them progressing every iteration forfeits the benefit. This is the mechanism-level counterpart to the paper's metric point: on this heavy-tailed workload, the only thing that moves the p99 is reordering which requests wait — and doing so is near-Pareto here (equal E2E and throughput); goodput@SLO, a TTFT metric, is exactly the quantity this benign reordering targets. (The reserved-lane mechanism is our own; we report it as a negative control, not a contribution.)

6 Related work

Strata (arXiv 2508.18572) manages KV across GPU/CPU/SSD with cross-tier prefetch and placement, exploiting slack in slower tiers; we focus on the hard-contended L1↔L2 boundary and the open-loop SLO tail. Mooncake (FAST'25) disaggregates prefill/decode with a KVCache-centric global pool and SLO-aware early rejection under overload; we study a single node, keep serving lossless (no rejection), and analyze the intra-node prefill tail. HiCache (sglang) is the tiered cache we build on. CachedAttention/AttentionStore (OSDI'24) prefetches a conversation's KV from slower tiers ahead of its next turn using scheduler hints during think-time gaps; our setting has no think-time (follow-ups re-enter an open-loop Poisson stream) and our analysis targets whether such prefetch can move goodput@SLO at all. LMCache/CacheGen compress and stream KV for cross-request reuse; we remain lossless. Chunked-prefill scheduling (Sarathi-Serve and related) splits long prefills into fixed chunks and mixes them with decode to avoid decode stalls; we take this mechanism as given and show its consequence for the tail — a single in-flight chunked request serializes concurrent heavy documents, giving the p99 ≈ K·t we exploit (§2.4). Metastable failures (Bronson et al., HotOS'21; Huang et al., OSDI'22) name the phenomenon at the heart of our result: a system driven into a sustained bad state by a transient trigger and held there by a self-reinforcing feedback loop. The per-rate cold start is exactly such a trigger, and the prefill queue's depth→slot-occupancy→queue-growth loop (§5.3) is the sustaining feedback; what we add is that this metastability manifests as run-to-run non-determinism of a benchmark metric, not an outage — the deadline sits inside the metastable band, so goodput@SLO samples the feedback loop's coin toss. Our analytical frame is classical queueing theory (Kleinrock; Pollaczek–Khinchine; Harchol-Balter on tail-optimal scheduling), applied to the LLM prefill server. Systems-measurement rigor. A long line of work warns that naive systems benchmarking misleads: Georges et al. (Statistically Rigorous Java Performance Evaluation, OOPSLA'07) showed single-run comparisons are unsound and mandated replication with confidence intervals; Mytkowicz et al. (Producing Wrong Data Without Doing Anything Obviously Wrong!, ASPLOS'09) documented systematic measurement bias; and Kalibera & Jones (ISMM'13) formalized how many repetitions rigor requires. We extend this tradition to LLM-serving goodput@SLO, but with a sharper twist: the tail metric here is not merely noisy — it is metastable, so its variance does not shrink usefully with replication near the knee (required-k diverges, §5.5), and the standard remedy of median-of-k is itself insufficient. To our knowledge we are the first to show that the standard goodput@SLO metric is a metastable coin-flip on a heavy-tailed cold-prefill serving workload, and to separate the cache's stable gains from the noisy tail.

7 Limitations & threats to validity

  • Node confound — controlled, not merely flagged. The three cross-node stock runs (λ=3 p99 6.8–14.1 s) mix cold-start metastability with node heterogeneity (~±45% p99). We removed this confound with the same-node median-of-k study of §3.1: K=5 stock replicates on one node (ondem-3) give λ=3 p99 6.47–25.76 s (median 9.4 s; two pass, three fail), so the coin-flip persists — indeed widens to 4.0× — with the node held constant. Run-to-run cold-start metastability is therefore the cause, not node heterogeneity (which is an additional layer). We also ran the same-node K=5 protocol for write-back (§3.1): both stock and write-back pass 2 of 5, so even median-of-k cannot separate them on goodput@SLO, though write-back clearly wins on stable metrics (hit +7 pp, p50 −64 ms) and tail variance — this validates the methodology (median-of-k A/B) and its limit (goodput@SLO is too noisy to resolve KV configs even at K=5; report stable metrics). We then closed the single-node limit with a second-node grid: an independent same-node K=5 on node1-2 gives λ=3 p99 6.5–21.9 s (median 7.2 s; three pass, two fail; σ/m = 7.8) — the same coin-flip (wide spread, a ~22 s near-catastrophe) as ondem-3 (median 9.4 s; σ/m = 5.7). So the fixed-node coin-flip reproduces across nodes (combined 5 / 10 pass over these two nodes); it is a general property, not an artifact of one machine. A third node (0-3, from the session-variance experiment of §5.5) confirms it independently: its nine stock λ=3 draws span 6.1–36.7 s with 2 of 9 passing — the same straddle-the-SLO coin-flip, so every one of the three nodes we measured exhibits it. The grid also makes an honest nuance concrete: node1-2's median goodput is 3 while ondem-3's is 0, so node heterogeneity additionally shifts the metric on top of the within-node coin-flip — both axes matter, exactly as the confound warned. The methodological claim is thus demonstrated on two nodes: single-run — and especially cross-node — A/B on goodput@SLO is void, and even same-node median-of-k does not rescue it for KV-cache comparison. We also tested whether server-launch (session) state adds a third variance layer: a controlled experiment (N=3 back-to-back launches × K=3 stock draws in one allocation, §5.5) found within-session variance dominates (across-session component just 2% of within-session; all three session means 14.6–17.7 s while each session spans the full 6–37 s range), so there is no separable launch-level layer — the coin-flip is run-to-run, and node heterogeneity is the only additional axis. We had initially read an earlier cross-allocation difference as a possible launch-level effect; the controlled test attributes it to within-session sampling plus node state, and we retract that reading.
  • Per-rate cache flush. The harness cold-starts the cache before each rate (the load generator issues /flush_cache per invocation), compounding the metastability by ramping the queue to 90–184 before it drains (§5.3). We tested the no-flush condition with an off-contract, node-controlled diagnostic (v0-warmdiag, §5.3): warm steady-state lowers p99 by 33–45% at every rate but leaves every rate above the SLO (λ=3 14.1→9.1 s), so it narrows the coin-flip band without removing the failure — the structural cold-prefill floor, not the flush, is the binding ceiling. We further ran a warm median-of-k (v0-warm-medk, §5.3): K=5 no-flush λ=3 draws are still a coin-flip (1 / 5 pass, σ/m = 1.4), so warm-steady-state lowers the variance-domination ratio from 5.7 to 1.4 but does not clear σ/m < 1. Warm-steady-state is thus a necessary but insufficient part of the fix here: it removes a large, real variance source, yet even warm + median-of-k cannot make goodput@SLO trustworthy on this workload — the reliable evaluation is the stable metrics. (The flush-vs-warm σ reduction is cross-node, so taken only as suggestive; the node-controlled facts are warmdiag's same-node flush effect and warm-medk's same-node coin-flip.)
  • Model simplification. The M/G/1 model is single-server and ignores decode contention, mixed prefill/decode batching, and the device/host KV split; it is an explanatory tool for the metastability, not a point predictor.
  • Scope. One model (Qwen3.5-122B, hybrid-Mamba), one hardware point (8×H100, 2-tier, no L3), one workload family. The coin-flip is bounded to heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads evaluated near the knee with a binary p99-SLO (§3 scope). We do not claim the cache is useless — it stably improves hit, median, and throughput — only that goodput@SLO cannot reliably resolve KV-cache differences on this benchmark.
  • A measurement pitfall — but also a real operational signal. One might object that unpredictable tail latency near the knee is a genuine serving problem, not merely an evaluation artifact. That is correct, and we do not dismiss it: the coin-flip has two faces. (i) As an A/B metric for KV caches it is invalid — it flips on run-to-run noise, not on the cache (our thesis). (ii) As an operational property it is real — a system driven near the knee genuinely exhibits metastable, unpredictable tail latency. The two are complementary, and our results bound the operational side: the tail is heavy-tailed cold turn-0 prefill (§5.2) that no lossless cache mechanism shortens (§3.3, §2.4) — though request reordering does (SRPF, §5.6) — so the remedy is not a better cache but a scheduler (SRPF, §5.6), provisioning below the knee, admission control, or accepting the tail — all orthogonal to KV-cache comparison. goodput@SLO is the wrong lens for comparing caches precisely because the tail it gates on is a workload-and-queueing property, not a cache property.

9 Conclusion

goodput@SLO — the emerging headline metric for KV-cache serving — is, on heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads evaluated near the queueing knee, a cold-start coin-flip: its p99-gated verdict turns on run-to-run metastability, not on the cache. We showed this empirically (six nodes, three configurations, with the node-heterogeneity confound resolved by same-node median-of-k on two nodes), explained it mechanistically (a metastable transient prefill queue serializing a heavy-tailed cold-turn-0 distribution), reproduced it from first principles (a minimal forward model, parameterized only by the measured token distribution and calibrated prefill rate, with a coin-flip band at ρ ≈ 0.5–0.7), and bounded the standard remedies (warm-steady-state removes the cold-start trigger but changes the metric; median-of-k does not converge near the knee, where the required k diverges). Crucially, the cache is not useless: it stably improves hit rate, median TTFT, and throughput — effects that separate configurations at 7–9σ where the binary tail metric gives 0.4σ. The information needed to rank caches is present throughout; the tail-SLO gate simply discards it.

For practitioners and benchmark designers, three concrete steps follow. (1) Screen before you trust: apply the σ/m test (§4) — report goodput@SLO only when the gating-rate p99 standard deviation is small relative to the SLO margin; near the knee it is not. (2) Never draw single-run, and especially cross-node, KV-cache A/B conclusions from a p99-gated metric — identical configurations flip 0↔3. (3) Compare on stable effects: rank caches by their low-variance signals (hit rate, median TTFT, throughput), and when the tail genuinely matters, report its full distribution rather than a single-threshold pass/fail. The broader lesson is that the tail-SLO lens is the wrong instrument for comparing KV caches precisely because the tail it gates on is a workload-and-queueing property, not a cache property — a distinction that a decade of systems-measurement rigor (§6) teaches us to make before, not after, we publish a ranking.
