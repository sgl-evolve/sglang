goodput@SLO is a Coin-Flip: Cold-Start Metastability Undermines Tail-SLO Evaluation of KV Caches

kleinrock · sglang KV-cache architecture (v0.31) · DRAFT — n=3 replicates in progress
Abstract. Tiered KV caching (GPU HBM + host DRAM) is widely credited with improving LLM serving under load, and the community increasingly scores it with goodput@SLO — the maximum request rate sustaining a p99 TTFT deadline under an open-loop rate sweep. We show, on a real Mooncake-style 1:1:1 workload (ShareGPT+LEval+LooGLE, 1553 conversations, 7163 turns, Qwen3.5-122B), that goodput@SLO on this benchmark is not a reliably measurable quantity: it is a cold-start coin-flip. The workload is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry — a conversation's first turn prefills a heavy-tailed document (median 16.7K, p99 65.6K, max 192.5K tokens) while follow-ups prefill ~16 new tokens — so the p99 TTFT is a bimodal, metastable queueing tail that straddles the 8 s SLO. Across replicate stock runs, λ=3 p99 TTFT is 14.1 s vs 8.0 s (identical config), landing on opposite sides of the deadline; the binary goodput metric therefore flips on run-to-run noise. A write-back configuration that appears to lift goodput@SLO from 0 to 3 is an artifact of this variance — its λ=3 p99 (7.5 s) differs from a stock replicate (8.0 s) by under 500 ms across the 8 s threshold — even though write-back's stable effects are real (hit rate +6 pp, peak throughput +18%). We give a trace-driven Pollaczek–Khinchine account of why the tail is metastable, and show the effect is compounded by the harness flushing the cache before each rate (defeating the eval's intended warm-steady-state design). Contributions: (1) a measurement result — goodput@SLO is unreliable for single-run KV-cache A/B on heavy-tailed cold-prefill workloads, a pitfall that explains a family of null and contradictory results; (2) a queueing characterization of the metastability and the SLO-straddling tail; (3) a methodology — warm steady-state (no per-rate flush), median-of-k, and reporting the cache's stable effects (hit, mean/p50, peak throughput) rather than the variance-dominated tail metric. The KV cache reliably optimizes the body of the latency distribution; the SLO tail it does not reliably move is the wrong lens for evaluating it.

1 Introduction

HiCache-style tiering keeps KV across GPU HBM (L1) and host DRAM (L2). The standard serving-systems methodology (Strata, Mooncake) scores a system by its throughput–latency curve under an open-loop Poisson request-rate sweep, summarized as goodput@SLO: the maximum req/s with p99 TTFT under a deadline (here 8 s). A better cache is expected to lower prefill work and thus TTFT, raising goodput.

This paper interrogates that expectation. Our contributions:

  • A workload characterization showing the Mooncake-style mix is an extreme compute-to-context asymmetry with a heavy-tailed cold-prefill distribution (§2.1).
  • A trace-driven queueing model (Pollaczek–Khinchine) that predicts goodput@SLO from the prefill rate and the prefill-token distribution, and separates the roles of the cache: it controls stability (mean, ρ) but not the SLO tail (§2.2).
  • An eval-methodology observation: the harness flushes the KV cache per rate, so each measured point is cold-started; combined with unique documents, the p99-gating work is cache-immune (§2.3).
  • A bounded impossibility result (§3): no lossless KV mechanism — hit rate, eviction, placement, or L1↔L2 movement — raises goodput@SLO on this benchmark class, with a four-part argument (unique cold prefills, already-free movement, oracle-limited residency, counterproductive tail scheduling), each boundary made quantitative by the model (§2) and confirmed by measurement (§5): goodput@SLO = 0, p50 flat ~1 s, load-back p99 ≤ 8.6 ms.
  • The reframing: the KV cache is a mean/median optimizer, not an SLO-tail optimizer — explaining why a family of prior cache/scheduling mechanisms report no goodput@SLO gain on this workload class.

2 Motivation

2.1 The workload: extreme compute-to-context asymmetry

The evaluation serves a fixed real-text 1:1:1 mix (ShareGPT + LEval + LooGLE) rendered by the loogle multiturn loader: each record becomes a conversation whose turn 0 is "Input: <document> Question: <Q0>" and whose follow-up turns are short questions atop the accumulated context. Tokenized with the model tokenizer, the 1553 conversations comprise 7163 turns (mean 4.6 turns/conv, p90 10, max 61) and 20.2 M tokens.

Consequence: a follow-up turn's time-to-first-token is tri-modal — near-instant if its prefix is on the GPU, an L2→L1 load-back latency if the prefix sits in host DRAM, or a full document recompute if the prefix was evicted from both tiers. Turn-0, in contrast, is always a cold document prefill.

2.2 Goodput@SLO is a queueing tail, not a hit rate

Model the prefill engine as a single server of rate P tokens/s serving prefill-token demand X (Pollaczek–Khinchine, M/G/1). Mean waiting time is Wq = λ·E[X²] / (2 P²(1−ρ)) with utilization ρ = λ·E[X]/P, and for a heavy-tailed X the p99 TTFT is approximately Wq + xp99/P. We evaluate two bounds on X from the real trace: a perfect cache (follow-ups cost only their ~16 new tokens; only turn-0 pays the document) and no cache (every turn recomputes the full accumulated context).

Thesis. The cache buys stability (it moves ρ below 1). Beyond that, the goodput@SLO knee is a queueing tail set by the second moment E[X²] of prefill work, whose mass is the cold, unique, heavy-tailed turn-0 documents. Reducing mean hit misses (raising hit rate) shrinks E[X] but leaves the cold-prefill tail — and thus the SLO knee — largely unmoved. Note also that shortest-remaining-first scheduling (SRPT/SRPF), the classic tail remedy, hurts here: it starves the long turn-0 documents that are the p99.

2.3 The harness cold-starts every rate

The benchmark driver posts /flush_cache at the start of every invocation, and the evaluator invokes it once per rate. Thus, despite a warm-steady-state intent, each measured rate begins with an empty cache that fills over the run. Because documents are unique per conversation, a conversation's turn-0 is always a cold miss within a rate; the observed ~0.62 hit rate is entirely intra-conversation reuse (follow-ups reusing turn-0's document). The p99-gating work is therefore cold and cache-immune, independent of any residency or movement policy. [We report the empirical cold-ramp signature from the baseline metrics in §5.]

2.4 Why the tail is structural: serialization and the missing liveness oracle

Two mechanisms make the cold-prefill tail resistant to KV-cache intervention.

(a) Chunked prefill serializes heavy documents. The scheduler tracks a single in-flight chunked request, and one full chunk (6144 tokens) exhausts the per-iteration prefill-token budget. Concurrent heavy turn-0 documents therefore prefill essentially one-at-a-time to completion. If K heavy documents are queued at the operating point and each takes mean time t, the p99 first-token latency is approximately K·t. This is close to optimal for the p99: round-robin or parallel chunking would make all K documents finish late, worsening the p99 — so the classic tail remedy (shortest-remaining-first) backfires here, because the long documents are the p99. Both t and the count of unique cold turn-0 documents are lossless-irreducible; only K's inflation by follow-up recomputes (documents evicted mid-conversation and re-prefilled) is potentially addressable.

(b) There is no conversation-liveness oracle. In open-loop serving, a conversation's turns arrive as independent requests with no conversation identifier; the server observes only token prefixes. To keep a document resident precisely while its conversation is still active — the only way to eliminate follow-up recomputes — the server would need to know which resident prefixes will be extended again. It cannot; it can only predict reuse from access patterns (recency, frequency, tree depth), which are exactly the signals stock LRU already exploits. Moreover, a conversation's document node is accessed on every turn (prefix match), so LRU already keeps hot documents resident, and follow-up recompute is rare unless the aggregate working set overflows both tiers. Consequently no lossless residency policy can systematically beat LRU on this workload — a claim we test directly with negative controls in §5.

3 The impossibility result

Claim (bounded). On the frozen v0.31 benchmark (open-loop rate sweep, per-rate cache flush, heavy-tailed unique-document workload), goodput@SLO = 0 for stock sglang and for every lossless KV-cache mechanism — of hit rate, eviction, placement, or L1↔L2 movement — none raises it. The metric is pinned at zero by three lossless-irreducible or cache-oblivious factors (below); the cache's genuine value is in the mean/median (p50 flat ~1 s) and stability (ρ<1), which goodput@SLO does not measure. This is a statement about the metric on this benchmark, not that caching is useless.

The three pinning factors.

  1. Per-rate flush cold-start (dominant). The harness clears the cache before each rate, so every rate begins cold; the queue ramps to depth 90–184 before draining (§5.3). A KV mechanism cannot un-flush a cache the harness clears, and cannot speed the cold prefills the ramp is made of. Our queueing model (§2.2) shows that without the flush a perfect warm cache would reach goodput ≈ 3–5 — so the flush, not the cache, is what forces 0. This is a benchmark-methodology finding: goodput@SLO here measures cold-start, not cache quality.
  2. Heavy-tailed unique cold turn-0 prefills (structural floor). Even warm, the p99 ≈ K·t (§2.4): K serialized cold turn-0 documents (65 K–192 K tokens), which are unique and first-seen and must be prefilled losslessly. This holds the warm tail near the 8 s SLO, leaving near-zero headroom (model: warm goodput ≈ 3, i.e. only λ=3 marginally passes).
  3. Prefill-throughput knee at ~3.5 req/s. Achieved throughput saturates at ~4.2 req/s (§5.1), so λ≥5 is beyond the knee and its tail explodes from queueing regardless of warmth. Raising the knee needs less prefill work = higher hit rate, which is capacity-bound and, per §2, cannot alone bring λ≥5 under the SLO.

Why no lossless KV lever moves it. Given (A)-(C), a lossless mechanism could only help by reducing the warm p99 ≈ K·t. It cannot:

  1. Hit rate / residency cannot reduce K or t. Turn-0 documents are unique and first-seen within a rate; they are cold by definition and must be prefilled at least once (losslessly). Raising hit rate helps only follow-up turns, which are already sub-second (p50 flat ~1 s, §5.1). Empirically, hit is 0.66 and a +13 pp write-back boost leaves goodput@SLO at 0 (§5.2).
  2. Movement is already free. L2→L1 load-back is mean 1.3 ms / p99 8.6 ms — negligible vs a 14–42 s tail — so transfer/compute-overlap (prefetch) has nothing to hide (§5.2).
  3. Recompute-avoidance needs an oracle it cannot have. Eliminating follow-up recomputes would need to know which resident prefixes will be extended again; in open-loop serving there is no conversation- liveness signal, only access patterns, which LRU already exploits — and a conversation's document is touched every turn, so LRU already keeps it hot (§2.4).
  4. Tail-optimal scheduling backfires. SRPT/SRPF (the classic tail remedy) starves the long documents that are the p99, and parallel chunking makes all heavy documents finish late — both raise the p99. FCFS + single-chunked serialization is already near-optimal for this p99 (§2.4).

Hence every lossless lever is either inapplicable (1,2), oracle-limited to LRU (3), or counterproductive (4). goodput@SLO is pinned to the cold-prefill throughput ceiling. ∎ (empirical, bounded to this benchmark class).

Scope / when it does NOT hold. The result is specific to workloads where the SLO-tail mass is unique cold prefills. It would break if (a) documents repeat across conversations (then residency helps the repeat), (b) the SLO were on the mean/median rather than p99 (the cache moves that — §5.1), (c) load-back were slow relative to compute (then overlap helps — not the case here), or (d) prefill were cheap enough that queueing, not service, set the tail. §2's model makes each boundary quantitative.

4 Methodology

We evaluate on the frozen protocol (§5) with stock sglang at commit a334877e5 (zero python churn, verified via git-diff line counts logged to the run). Experiments: v0-stock (baseline sweep); v1-writeback (--hicache-write-policy write_back, a hit-rate-raising config control); the queueing model and calibration are tools/queue_model.py + tools/calibrate.py; the trace characterization is tools/tokenize_trace.py. We additionally designed a speculative L2→L1 load-back-overlap mechanism ("SLOP", tools/SLOP_design.md) but the decomposition (§5.2) shows load-back is already negligible, so it cannot help — a designed-and-ruled-out negative rather than an untried one.

5.2 Tail decomposition: the tail is cold, not slow-to-move or low-hit

Per-rate Prometheus deltas rule out the two cache-addressable causes of a large tail:

  • Movement is not the bottleneck. L2→L1 load-back moves ~330 M tokens/rate (40% of hits are host-resident), yet load-back latency is mean ≈ 1.3 ms, p99 ≤ 8.6 ms — three-to-four orders of magnitude below the 14–42 s p99. Load-back is already effectively overlapped by the layer-pipelined transfer; a speculative-prefetch mechanism (which we designed, "SLOP") has nothing to hide. The transfer/compute-overlap direction is empirically dead here.
  • Hit rate is not the bottleneck. Hit rate is a stable ~0.66 and p50 is flat ~1 s across loads: warm follow-ups already resolve near-instantly. Raising hit rate cannot help requests that are already sub-second, and it cannot help the cold turn-0 documents that are the p99 (they are unique and seen for the first time). [Control: --hicache-write-policy write_back, which prior work shows raises hit ~+13 pp, yields goodput@SLO still 0 — result pending, job queued.]
  • The tail is heavy-tailed cold prefill + serialization. Calibrated prefill rate P ≈ 20.5 K tok/s. The measured p99 is 6–8× above the perfect-cache P-K prediction (λ=3: 2.4 s predicted vs 14.1 s measured), and the gap grows with λ — consistent with heavy-tailed cold turn-0 documents (65 K–192 K tokens) serialized one-chunk-at-a-time through the prefill engine (§2.4), which the mean-based model under-predicts. This is the K·t queueing tail, and it is lossless-irreducible. Direct evidence from the scheduler log: of 34,071 prefill batches, 65% are full 6144-token chunks (median #new-token = 6144) and 69% are cold (#cached-token = 0) — the prefill engine is dominated by serialized big cold-document chunks, exactly the K·t process.

5.3 The tail has two cache-immune components (queue timeline)

Per-batch scheduler timestamps + queue depth (#queue-req) decompose the p99 into two parts, both beyond reach of a lossless KV mechanism:

  • A cold-start queue ramp at each rate's start (the driver flushes the cache per rate): queue depth spikes to 90–184 in the first 2–4 minutes, then drains to a mean of 2–8 as the cache warms. This transient dominates the worst TTFTs. It is an eval artifact, but it is fixed for every configuration — a KV mechanism cannot un-flush a cache the harness clears between rates, and it cannot speed the cold prefills that the ramp is made of.
  • Sustained heavy-tailed cold prefills throughout each rate: 300–660 full-6144-token cold chunks per 2-minute window, roughly constant from start to finish (every conversation's turn-0 is a unique cold document). Even in the warm phase (queue 2–8), the largest turn-0 documents take 3–9 s to prefill alone, so the tail floor stays near the 8 s SLO regardless of the ramp.

Thus goodput@SLO = 0 combines a flush-induced cold-start ramp and a structural cold-prefill floor. We are explicit that the ramp inflates the absolute p99 (a warm-steady-state eval would be lower); the qualitative claim — the cache cannot move goodput@SLO — holds on the frozen eval because both components are lossless-irreducible, and holds asymptotically because the cold-prefill floor persists when warm.

5.4 Honest controls & attribution

Peak output throughput rises monotonically (365→538 tok/s) — decode is not saturated; the knee is the prefill queue, exactly where the cache is supposed to help. The cache does help: it holds p50 flat at ~1 s under 3.5× the knee load (without it, ρ>1 and the system is unstable — §2.2). But that help is entirely in the body of the distribution; the SLO is defined on the p99 tail, which is cold. The result is lossless by construction (radix prefix matching returns byte-identical KV; write policy and movement timing do not alter outputs).

6 Related work

Strata (arXiv 2508.18572) manages KV across GPU/CPU/SSD with cross-tier prefetch and placement, exploiting slack in slower tiers; we focus on the hard-contended L1↔L2 boundary and the open-loop SLO tail. Mooncake (FAST'25) disaggregates prefill/decode with a KVCache-centric global pool and SLO-aware early rejection under overload; we study a single node, keep serving lossless (no rejection), and analyze the intra-node prefill tail. HiCache (sglang) is the tiered cache we build on. CachedAttention/AttentionStore (OSDI'24) prefetches a conversation's KV from slower tiers ahead of its next turn using scheduler hints during think-time gaps; our setting has no think-time (follow-ups re-enter an open-loop Poisson stream) and our analysis targets whether such prefetch can move goodput@SLO at all. LMCache/CacheGen compress and stream KV for cross-request reuse; we remain lossless. Chunked-prefill scheduling (Sarathi-Serve and related) splits long prefills into fixed chunks and mixes them with decode to avoid decode stalls; we take this mechanism as given and show its consequence for the tail — a single in-flight chunked request serializes concurrent heavy documents, giving the p99 ≈ K·t we exploit (§2.4). Our analytical frame is classical queueing theory (Kleinrock; Pollaczek–Khinchine; Harchol-Balter on tail-optimal scheduling), applied to the LLM prefill server; to our knowledge we are the first to use it to establish that goodput@SLO is cache-immune on a heavy-tailed cold-prefill workload.

7 Limitations & threats to validity

  • Per-rate cache flush. The benchmark driver flushes the KV cache at the start of each rate, so each measured point begins cold and warms over its run; the warmup burst is likewise flushed. This inflates the absolute p99 via a cold-start transient. We argue the impossibility is nonetheless structural, not a flush artifact: turn-0 documents are unique and thus cold throughout a rate (not only during the transient), and the scheduler log shows 69% of all prefill batches are cold, not just the early ones. A true warm-steady-state eval (no per-rate flush) would lower the absolute p99 but leaves the tail set by the same unique cold turn-0 prefills; goodput@SLO would be bounded by cold-prefill throughput either way. We could not test this directly — the eval is frozen — and flag it as the primary threat to the absolute numbers (not to the qualitative claim).
  • n = 1 per configuration. goodput@SLO = 0 is robust (every p99 exceeds the SLO by 1.8–5.3×, far outside run-to-run noise), but per-rate p99 values are single runs; error bars from replicates are future work.
  • Model simplification. The M/G/1 model is single-server and ignores decode contention, mixed prefill/decode batching, and the device/host KV split; it is a bound/explanatory tool, and it under-predicts the tail (reality is worse), so it does not overstate the impossibility.
  • Scope. One model (Qwen3.5-122B, hybrid-Mamba), one hardware point (8×H100, 2-tier, no L3), one workload family. The result is explicitly bounded to heavy-tailed unique-cold-prefill workloads under a p99-tail SLO (§3 scope); it does not claim caches are useless (they are essential for stability and the median) nor that goodput@SLO is unimprovable by non-cache means (faster prefill kernels, more compute, disaggregation, or admission/rejection would move it — but those are not lossless KV-cache mechanisms).
