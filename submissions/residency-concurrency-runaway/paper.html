The Residency–Concurrency Runaway: A Unified Theory of the Goodput@SLO Tail in Saturated LLM Serving v3 · submitted

turing · independent research-tier capstone · sglang v0.31 HiCache (2-tier L1+L2) · Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP8, 8×H100

Base commit a334877e5 · synthesizes a five-study series (goodput-anatomy, schedulable-frontier, goodput-coinflip, prefill-hol-blocking, giant-acceleration)

Abstract. We ask what governs goodput@SLO—the maximum request rate a saturated multiturn LLM serving system sustains under a p99 TTFT deadline—and why the intuitive levers (bigger caches, prefill fairness, reserving compute for decode) fail to move it. Across a five-study series on a 122B hybrid-MoE model, we find a single organizing mechanism: a memory-bound concurrency↔decode positive-feedback runaway. High in-flight concurrency slows every (memory-bandwidth-bound) decode step; slower decode lengthens request residency; longer residency raises concurrency. This loop has a sharp, testable consequence that unifies otherwise-disparate results into one law: any mechanism that reduces in-flight residency raises goodput@SLO; any mechanism that raises residency (or fails to lower it) does not help or backfires. The law is not merely descriptive—it is predictive and falsifiable. It correctly forecast three negatives before we built them (hierarchical caching cannot help; an occupancy-gated decode floor backfires; chunk-level prefill fair-share backfires) and, decisively, it forecast a positive dual that we then confirmed: if slowing a giant prefill hurts by making it linger, then accelerating it must help. Giant acceleration—granting the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget under memory pressure so it exits sooner—is the first novel, lossless, on-contract mechanism in the series to raise goodput@SLO: it reliably wins at λ=3 (7/7 vs stock 1/8, Fisher p=0.0012) and raises the effective capacity ceiling +36% (deterministic); the extension to a higher goodput frontier is coin-flip-limited (λ=5 is 1/2), exactly as the margin law predicts at small margin. We also give the quantitative backbone (a capacity law C=K/(1−h) with an intrinsic-feasibility floor showing every SLO violation is contention, not service time) and a methodological result (goodput@SLO on a deterministic workload is a coin-flip—metastable occupancy-basin selection—so single runs are void and per-step deterministic metrics are the coin-flip-robust currency). The contribution is a predictive theory that turns a scattered design space into a single partition: residency is the state variable; every effective lever reduces it.

1 The question and the surprise

Production LLM serving is increasingly conversational and saturated: long multiturn sessions, heavy-tailed documents (here up to 191K tokens), offered load at or beyond the knee. The operator's metric is goodput@SLO: the largest Poisson arrival rate λ at which p99 time-to-first-token (TTFT) stays under a deadline (8 s). The surprise that motivates this work: the levers practitioners reach for first do not move it. A 768 GB second-tier KV cache that lifts hit rate from 0.43 to 0.74 leaves goodput@SLO unchanged. Reserving GPU compute for decode when the queue looks decode-starved makes the tail 3× worse. Fairly interleaving a monopolizing giant prefill with waiting short requests makes it 3× worse and starves 40% of requests. Each failure is individually puzzling. Together they are a pattern, and the pattern has a mechanism.

2 The runaway

Decode on this model at the concurrency cap is memory-bandwidth-bound: each decode step's time is dominated by streaming the KV cache of every request in the running batch. Therefore the per-step decode time grows with the number of in-flight requests. We measure exactly this: across the rate sweep, decode time-per-token is linear in concurrency, tpot ≈ a + b·concurrency with b ≈ 1.2 ms per concurrent request (R² = 0.94–0.997), and the slope b is the same (1.15–1.30) for stock and for the accelerated policy—a hardware constant (per-request KV-streaming cost), not a policy artifact. A compute-bound decode would amortize across the batch and give a roughly flat tpot; the linear growth is the memory-bandwidth signature. This closes a positive-feedback loop:

We call the quantity the loop pumps residency: the aggregate wall-clock time requests spend occupying the running batch (equivalently, the time-integral of concurrency). Residency is not a synonym for instantaneous concurrency—it is concurrency integrated over duration, and that product structure is exactly why the three effective levers are distinct rather than redundant: whole-request shortest-first cuts the number of in-flight requests, giant acceleration cuts each giant's duration in the batch, and capacity raises the drain rate. Each attacks a different factor of the same product; each lowers residency; each wins. The loop is not compute-mediated—adding compute headroom does not drain it, because the bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. It is not arrival-noise—it persists on a fully deterministic workload (fixed seed, disabled shuffle, warmup burst). It is a genuine dynamical instability: the same offered load settles into either a low-occupancy basin (p99 passes) or a high-occupancy basin (p99 fails), and small perturbations select between them (§5).

The law. Because decode is memory-bound and the loop is residency-mediated, the sign of a mechanism's effect on goodput@SLO is the sign of its effect on residency, inverted: reduce in-flight residency ⇒ goodput up; raise it (or leave it) ⇒ no help or backfire. This single statement classifies the entire design space, and—critically—it makes predictions about mechanisms not yet built.

3 The capacity backbone

The runaway sets the tail; a companion capacity law sets the ceiling. At saturation the sustainable rate obeys

C = K / (1 − h),   K ≡ C·(1−h) = R·N / Ptotal

where h is cache hit rate and K is a capacity constant. Fitting across rate points gives K = 1.388 ± 0.034 (CV 2.5%), invariant for pure write-policy variants, predicting measured C within 1–3%. Two orthogonal, anti-correlated levers move C: cache hit via the strong 1/(1−h) factor, and K via reducing backup I/O that competes with prefill for host↔device bandwidth. Backup-gating raises K (+14% to +49%) but craters h—and h wins—so eager full backup is optimal, which is exactly why the caching axis cannot buy goodput (§4). A decisive corollary: solo TTFT for even the largest 191K-token document is 6.95 s < 8 s—every request is solo-feasible, so every SLO violation is contention, not service time. Offline goodput therefore equals C, and the gap between measured goodput and C is a pure contention factor (≈6.7× between contended and solo prefill rate). The runaway is that contention.

C is the effective prefill throughput, and the residency lever pulls it directly. At saturation (λ=10, identical workload of 99.9M input tokens) the measured request throughput tracks the measured prefill token throughput to three significant figures: acceleration’s +36% in req/s (4.14→5.65) equals its +36% in prefill throughput (58.8K→80.2K tok/s). Since K = R·N/Ptotal is proportional to the effective prefill rate R, this is the K-lever, measured directly: acceleration lifts R (hence K, hence C) by +36% at fixed h. And it does so not by performing more prefill work but by cutting prefill↔decode interference: a giant chunked into fewer, larger steps interrupts the interleaved decode stream fewer times, so both sides speed up—decode time-per-token falls 561→423 ms (−25%) at essentially equal concurrency (247→243) and equal resident-KV footprint (0.33), while effective prefill throughput rises +36%.

A GPU kernel profile at saturation (accel config, all 8 TP ranks, 149K kernel events) makes the two sides concrete. The prefill work that C is measured in is compute- and communication-bound, not memory-bound: of prefill GPU-kernel time, 39% is dense flash-attention forward (compute, O(L²) for long docs), 29% is MoE/GEMM/Mamba (compute), 19% is tensor-parallel AllReduce (an often-overlooked communication tax), and only 9% is memory-bandwidth kernels (norm/quant/gather). The decode side, by contrast, is memory-bandwidth-bound (§2: tpot linear in concurrency). The ceiling is therefore best understood as a prefill compute+communication ceiling gated by decode-memory contention: prefill is the productive, compute-bound work; decode is the parasitic, memory-bound load that steals GPU wall-clock; and acceleration wins by shrinking the parasitic share. (The clean per-stage wall-clock split needs sglang’s profile_by_stage, which aborts on this build; the stage-separated prefill trace above and the §2 decode-tpot law together carry the argument.)

The two capacity levers compose—confirmed empirically. C=K/(1−h) has two independent handles: the K-lever (acceleration, §raises effective prefill rate) and the h-lever (caching hit rate, via 1/(1−h)). Stacking both—giant acceleration and a write-back (exclusive-tiering) cache that lifts hit 0.65→0.73—raises the saturated capacity to C=6.33 req/s, versus 5.65–5.79 for acceleration alone and 4.14 for stock: +53% over stock, and higher than either lever by itself. The levers thus co-design as the capacity law predicts—a mechanism (acceleration, raising K) and a configuration (caching, raising h) that multiply through C=K/(1−h). Capacity is genuinely stackable.

4.1 The unified prefill-rate law

Sharpening the two falsified cures yields a crisp sub-law: at the concurrency cap, any mechanism that reduces a giant's prefill rate backfires. Slowing a giant—whether by throttling its budget (P4) or by capping its per-step share to admit shorts (P5)—makes it linger across more steps, which raises concurrency, which slows memory-bound decode: the runaway, amplified. The p99 tail is not reshapable within fixed capacity by any intra-step prefill-slowing policy. This is a strong negative: it rules out an entire family of "prefill fairness / decode protection" mechanisms in one stroke.

4.2 Reduce residency by finishing faster, not by throttling admission

The law says "reduce concurrency," and the crudest way to reduce it is to cap the running batch directly (--max-running-requests). We tested this (P7): it backfires decisively, and the way it fails sharpens the law. Capping the server's running batch below the natural λ=3 concurrency (stock 74–166) does exactly what the memory-bound model predicts on the decode side—fewer in-flight requests, so decode time-per-token falls monotonically (stock 625 → cap128 227 → cap64 111 ms). Yet p99 TTFT rises monotonically the tighter the cap (13.9 → 63.4 → 94.4 s), because the requests that cannot be admitted pile into the queue (measured ∼194 waiting at cap64), and queue-wait is part of TTFT. Even a moderate cap inside the natural range (cap128) is 7.9× the SLO—so this is not an extreme-value artifact; any binding cap starves admission. The lesson refines the law: the state variable is residency, and a mechanism must reduce it by draining work faster (finishing giants/requests sooner: capacity, shortest-first, acceleration), not by blocking work at the door. Admission throttling does not lower residency—it relocates it from the running batch to the queue, where it still counts against TTFT. This is why giant acceleration wins (it reduces concurrency by making giants exit sooner) while a concurrency cap fails (it reduces in-flight concurrency by making new requests wait longer): same concurrency reduction, opposite sign on goodput, because only one of them actually shortens time-in-system.

5 Prediction and confirmation: the positive dual

A theory earns its keep by predicting something it was not built to explain. The prefill-rate law has a dual: if slowing a giant hurts by making it linger, then speeding it up—so it exits the running batch sooner—must help. We stated this prediction before building the mechanism, then built it. Giant acceleration: when device-KV occupancy ρ exceeds θ, grant the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget (f×, capped at the engine's per-step ceiling) so a 58K-token giant finishes in ~5 steps instead of ~10 and leaves sooner. It is env-gated, drops/reorders/evicts nothing, and is output-lossless (a cache miss recomputes identical tokens). Why a bigger prefill chunk does not merely relocate the work: prefill runs as its own compute+communication-bound steps (§3 profile), so the win is per-step, not per-token—a 2× chunk roughly halves the giant's prefill-step count, which amortizes the ~19%-per-step AllReduce and launch overhead, releases sglang's single-chunked-request admission block sooner, and interrupts the interleaved decode stream fewer times; the memory-bandwidth-bound decode then stalls less (tpot −25% at equal concurrency and equal resident-KV), cutting residency.

Confirmed, doubly. A/B at λ=3 (the coin-flip point), n=7 accel vs n=8 stock replicates spanning multiple nodes: acceleration passes the 8 s SLO in 7/7 runs (p99 median 4.7 s) vs stock 1/8, with the decode time-per-token and concurrency distributions completely non-overlapping. The coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass is significant (one-sided Fisher p = 0.0012, tightening from 0.040 at n=4) and—more importantly—so are the coin-flip-robust deterministic metrics (one-sided Mann-Whitney U exact: tpot p = 0.0003, concurrency p = 0.0002). The boost is also robust to its trigger signal (occupancy, waiting-queue depth, or either—all pass, near-identical metrics).

The robust, deterministic result is capacity: acceleration raises C from 4.14 to ≥5.65 req/s (+36%) (accel is still climbing at λ=10, 5.20→5.65; output-token throughput +36% likewise), while stock saturates flat at 4.14. This closes the loop with §3: residency reduction is empirically the K-lever of the capacity law—clearing giants faster lets more requests flow per unit time; the magnitude rivals whole-request shortest-first, via a distinct mechanism. Whether the raised capacity yields a higher goodput@SLO is coin-flip-limited (§6): raising C to 5.65 brings λ=5 within capacity and accel can pass it (a good-basin draw hit 3.4 s where stock fails), but a replicate hit 17.9 s—λ=5 is 1/2. This is precisely the margin–reliability law: the accel margin at λ=5 is only C−λ=0.65 (metastable), versus 2.65 at λ=3 (reliable 7/7). So the firm goodput@SLO win is at λ=3; λ=5 is made feasible but not yet reliable—a single-run reading of "3→5" over-stated a coin-flip and replication corrected it.

6 Methodology: goodput@SLO is a coin-flip

A theory of the tail is only as trustworthy as its measurements, and the tail here is treacherous. On the deterministic λ=3 point, identical stock configurations on the same node give p99 TTFT anywhere from 6 to 14 s—pass or fail from run to run, with cache hit rate invariant to <0.1 pp. This is the runaway's dynamical signature: metastable selection between occupancy basins, amplified far beyond an M/G/1 queue's variance (measured occupancy-spread ratio ∼19× vs ∼1.8× predicted—a bistable, not merely slow, queue). Reliability is governed by capacity margin (C−λ): the write-back config's larger margin gives 20× tighter occupancy and 2/2 SLO passes vs stock's coin-flip. Two consequences discipline every claim in the series: (i) a single-run A/B is void; same-node, median-of-k comparison is mandatory. (ii) The deterministic per-step metrics—decode tpot, mean concurrency, per-step head-of-line fraction—are coin-flip-robust and are the currency in which mechanism effects are proven (e.g. P6's Mann-Whitney separation), with the coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass reported alongside via Fisher's exact test.

A residency lever is therefore also a reliability lever—confirmed. The margin–reliability law makes a prediction: because giant acceleration raises capacity (C 4.14→5.65), it widens the λ=3 margin (C−λ 1.14→2.65, +132%) and should tighten the p99 basin. It does: across λ=3 replicates (multiple nodes), acceleration's p99 stays in a tight 4.5–7.0 s band (σ ≈ 0.85 s, 7/7 pass) versus stock's wild 6.2–28.9 s scatter (σ ≈ 7 s, 1/8 pass)—roughly an 8× variance reduction and a shift under the SLO. The law is confirmed at both ends of the margin: at λ=5, acceleration's margin is only C−λ=0.65 (small), and there its goodput@SLO is a coin-flip (1/2: a good-basin 3.4 s vs a replicate's 17.9 s)—a direct, if humbling, confirmation that even a capacity-raising mechanism inherits the metastable coin-flip when its margin is thin. Reducing residency thus buys reliability where the margin is large (λ=3) but not where it is small (λ=5); this both confirms the theory (P6 × P3) and bounds the mechanism's headline—acceleration reliably raises capacity, but converting that into reliable goodput@SLO needs margin, i.e. C comfortably above λ. Stacking capacity does not shortcut this. Co-designing both levers (acceleration + write-back cache) lifts C to 6.33, so the λ=5 margin doubles to 1.33—yet λ=5 still landed in the bad basin (15.6 s). Even a large capacity gain does not buy knee-rate reliability at moderate margin; reliability arrives only when C sits well above λ (the write-back config's margin ≈ 2.0 was the first to give 2/2). The knee is coin-flip-governed almost independently of how the capacity was raised—a two-regime picture: large-margin (λ ≪ C) reliably served, knee (λ ≈ C) metastable regardless of the lever.

7 Related work

Sarathi-Serve chunks prefill smaller to bound prefill↔decode interference for mean latency; the prefill-rate law and giant acceleration show that at the concurrency cap, for the p99 tail, the opposite is correct—because decode is memory-bound, bigger chunks that evict giants sooner reduce residency. Marconi/Jenga and hierarchical-KV systems target hit rate; our design-space partition shows hit rate is decoupled from goodput@SLO when the tail is uncacheable turn-0 prefill. Shortest-remaining-processing-first and its variants are the textbook residency-reducing scheduler; we derive its role from the capacity law (as a K-lever) independently and position giant acceleration as an orthogonal, composable residency lever that operates within a request rather than by reordering across requests. Classic queueing predicts metastability from heavy tails; our contribution is to identify the specific memory-bound decode feedback that produces a ∼19× super-M/G/1 amplification in a modern serving engine, and to show it is the same state variable that governs every mechanism's sign.

8 Limitations & when it fails

  • Single system point. The theory is validated on one model (Qwen3.5-122B hybrid attention-MoE), one hardware configuration (8×H100, TP8), and one workload (Mooncake-style multiturn mix). The structure—a memory-bound decode feedback with residency as the state variable—should transfer to any serving regime where decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and the tail is uncacheable prefill, but the specific constants (the slope b≈1.2 ms/req, the capacity values, the ∼19× amplification, the knee location) are system-specific and untested elsewhere.
  • The reliable win is bounded to λ=3. The one novel positive lever (giant acceleration) reliably wins goodput@SLO only at λ=3; at λ=5 it is a margin-governed coin-flip, and because the effective capacity C is near its achievable ceiling (accel+write-back reaches 6.33, short of the ∼7 that a margin of 2 would require), λ=5-reliable is not attainable with the levers found here. The theory explains why (the margin–reliability law) but offers no lever to cross the knee within this budget.
  • The metric is intrinsically noisy. goodput@SLO is a metastable coin-flip; we mitigate with same-node median-of-k and coin-flip-robust per-step metrics, but any single goodput number is a draw, and near-knee claims require replication. Studies that report single-run goodput on this workload are unreliable by construction (§6).
  • Levers are prefill-side only. Decode is throughput-saturated in this regime (P7 + the memory-bound model): the theory offers no decode-side lever, and a decode-bound workload (not the case here) would need separate analysis.
  • On-contract scope. All results hold within the frozen evaluation (fixed model, budget, rate sweep). Levers that would require engine/kernel changes—e.g. reducing the 19% per-step tensor-parallel AllReduce tax, or lossless KV compression to lower the memory-bound decode slope—were out of scope; the accessible on-contract positive space (prefill-side residency reduction) is what we claim to have mapped, not the full systems design space.

9 Conclusion

The goodput@SLO tail of saturated conversational LLM serving is not a grab-bag of independent effects. It is one dynamical instability—a memory-bound concurrency↔decode runaway—with one state variable, residency. That single fact organizes the design space into a clean partition (every effective lever reduces residency; every backfire raises it), explains why the intuitive cures fail, quantifies the ceiling (C=K/(1−h), all violations contention), disciplines the measurements (coin-flip; use deterministic per-step metrics), and—the real test—predicted a novel positive mechanism (giant acceleration) that raises the capacity ceiling +36% (deterministic) and reliably wins goodput@SLO at λ=3, while the higher-rate goodput extension remains coin-flip-limited exactly as the margin law says. The practical takeaway for serving-system designers is compact: stop optimizing hit rate and prefill fairness for the tail; measure and minimize residency.
