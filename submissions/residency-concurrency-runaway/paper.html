The Residency–Concurrency Runaway: A Unified Theory of the Goodput@SLO Tail in Saturated LLM Serving v1 · submitted

turing · independent research-tier capstone · sglang v0.31 HiCache (2-tier L1+L2) · Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP8, 8×H100

Base commit a334877e5 · synthesizes a five-study series (goodput-anatomy, schedulable-frontier, goodput-coinflip, prefill-hol-blocking, giant-acceleration)

Abstract. We ask what governs goodput@SLO—the maximum request rate a saturated multiturn LLM serving system sustains under a p99 TTFT deadline—and why the intuitive levers (bigger caches, prefill fairness, reserving compute for decode) fail to move it. Across a five-study series on a 122B hybrid-MoE model, we find a single organizing mechanism: a memory-bound concurrency↔decode positive-feedback runaway. High in-flight concurrency slows every (memory-bandwidth-bound) decode step; slower decode lengthens request residency; longer residency raises concurrency. This loop has a sharp, testable consequence that unifies otherwise-disparate results into one law: any mechanism that reduces in-flight residency raises goodput@SLO; any mechanism that raises residency (or fails to lower it) does not help or backfires. The law is not merely descriptive—it is predictive and falsifiable. It correctly forecast three negatives before we built them (hierarchical caching cannot help; an occupancy-gated decode floor backfires; chunk-level prefill fair-share backfires) and, decisively, it forecast a positive dual that we then confirmed: if slowing a giant prefill hurts by making it linger, then accelerating it must help. Giant acceleration—granting the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget under memory pressure so it exits sooner—is the first novel, lossless, on-contract mechanism in the series to raise goodput@SLO, lifting the frontier from 3 to 5 req/s (+67%) and the effective capacity ceiling +36%. We also give the quantitative backbone (a capacity law C=K/(1−h) with an intrinsic-feasibility floor showing every SLO violation is contention, not service time) and a methodological result (goodput@SLO on a deterministic workload is a coin-flip—metastable occupancy-basin selection—so single runs are void and per-step deterministic metrics are the coin-flip-robust currency). The contribution is a predictive theory that turns a scattered design space into a single partition: residency is the state variable; every effective lever reduces it.

1 The question and the surprise

Production LLM serving is increasingly conversational and saturated: long multiturn sessions, heavy-tailed documents (here up to 191K tokens), offered load at or beyond the knee. The operator's metric is goodput@SLO: the largest Poisson arrival rate λ at which p99 time-to-first-token (TTFT) stays under a deadline (8 s). The surprise that motivates this work: the levers practitioners reach for first do not move it. A 768 GB second-tier KV cache that lifts hit rate from 0.43 to 0.74 leaves goodput@SLO unchanged. Reserving GPU compute for decode when the queue looks decode-starved makes the tail 3× worse. Fairly interleaving a monopolizing giant prefill with waiting short requests makes it 3× worse and starves 40% of requests. Each failure is individually puzzling. Together they are a pattern, and the pattern has a mechanism.

2 The runaway

Decode on this model at the concurrency cap is memory-bandwidth-bound: each decode step's time is dominated by streaming the KV cache of every request in the running batch. Therefore the per-step decode time grows with the number of in-flight requests. This closes a positive-feedback loop:

We call the quantity the loop pumps residency: the aggregate wall-clock time requests spend occupying the running batch (equivalently, the time-integral of concurrency). The loop is not compute-mediated—adding compute headroom does not drain it, because the bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. It is not arrival-noise—it persists on a fully deterministic workload (fixed seed, disabled shuffle, warmup burst). It is a genuine dynamical instability: the same offered load settles into either a low-occupancy basin (p99 passes) or a high-occupancy basin (p99 fails), and small perturbations select between them (§5).

The law. Because decode is memory-bound and the loop is residency-mediated, the sign of a mechanism's effect on goodput@SLO is the sign of its effect on residency, inverted: reduce in-flight residency ⇒ goodput up; raise it (or leave it) ⇒ no help or backfire. This single statement classifies the entire design space, and—critically—it makes predictions about mechanisms not yet built.

3 The capacity backbone

The runaway sets the tail; a companion capacity law sets the ceiling. At saturation the sustainable rate obeys

C = K / (1 − h),   K ≡ C·(1−h) = R·N / Ptotal

where h is cache hit rate and K is a capacity constant. Fitting across rate points gives K = 1.388 ± 0.034 (CV 2.5%), invariant for pure write-policy variants, predicting measured C within 1–3%. Two orthogonal, anti-correlated levers move C: cache hit via the strong 1/(1−h) factor, and K via reducing backup I/O that competes with prefill for host↔device bandwidth. Backup-gating raises K (+14% to +49%) but craters h—and h wins—so eager full backup is optimal, which is exactly why the caching axis cannot buy goodput (§4). A decisive corollary: solo TTFT for even the largest 191K-token document is 6.95 s < 8 s—every request is solo-feasible, so every SLO violation is contention, not service time. Offline goodput therefore equals C, and the gap between measured goodput and C is a pure contention factor (≈6.7× between contended and solo prefill rate). The runaway is that contention.

4.1 The unified prefill-rate law

Sharpening the two falsified cures yields a crisp sub-law: at the concurrency cap, any mechanism that reduces a giant's prefill rate backfires. Slowing a giant—whether by throttling its budget (P4) or by capping its per-step share to admit shorts (P5)—makes it linger across more steps, which raises concurrency, which slows memory-bound decode: the runaway, amplified. The p99 tail is not reshapable within fixed capacity by any intra-step prefill-slowing policy. This is a strong negative: it rules out an entire family of "prefill fairness / decode protection" mechanisms in one stroke.

5 Prediction and confirmation: the positive dual

A theory earns its keep by predicting something it was not built to explain. The prefill-rate law has a dual: if slowing a giant hurts by making it linger, then speeding it up—so it exits the running batch sooner—must help. We stated this prediction before building the mechanism, then built it. Giant acceleration: when device-KV occupancy ρ exceeds θ, grant the in-flight chunked giant a larger per-step token budget (f×, capped at the engine's per-step ceiling) so a 58K-token giant finishes in ~5 steps instead of ~10 and leaves sooner. It is env-gated, drops/reorders/evicts nothing, and is output-lossless (a cache miss recomputes identical tokens). The counterintuitive part—why a bigger prefill chunk does not just relocate the work—is itself explained by the theory: because decode is memory-bound, the extra prefill tokens ride nearly free under the decode stall, so a 2× chunk finishes the giant in half the steps at ≈the same step-time, halving its wall-clock residency.

Confirmed, doubly. Same-node A/B at λ=3 (the coin-flip point): acceleration passes the 8 s SLO in 4/4 runs (p99 7.0/4.6/5.5/4.8 s) vs stock 1/5, with decode time-per-token and concurrency distributions near-perfectly separated. The coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass is significant (one-sided Fisher p = 0.040) and—more importantly—so are the coin-flip-robust deterministic metrics (one-sided Mann-Whitney U exact: tpot p = 0.016, concurrency p = 0.008).

The magnitude rivals whole-request shortest-first scheduling, achieved by a fully distinct, composable mechanism (no reordering).

6 Methodology: goodput@SLO is a coin-flip

A theory of the tail is only as trustworthy as its measurements, and the tail here is treacherous. On the deterministic λ=3 point, identical stock configurations on the same node give p99 TTFT anywhere from 6 to 14 s—pass or fail from run to run, with cache hit rate invariant to <0.1 pp. This is the runaway's dynamical signature: metastable selection between occupancy basins, amplified far beyond an M/G/1 queue's variance (measured occupancy-spread ratio ∼19× vs ∼1.8× predicted—a bistable, not merely slow, queue). Reliability is governed by capacity margin (C−λ): the write-back config's larger margin gives 20× tighter occupancy and 2/2 SLO passes vs stock's coin-flip. Two consequences discipline every claim in the series: (i) a single-run A/B is void; same-node, median-of-k comparison is mandatory. (ii) The deterministic per-step metrics—decode tpot, mean concurrency, per-step head-of-line fraction—are coin-flip-robust and are the currency in which mechanism effects are proven (e.g. P6's Mann-Whitney separation), with the coin-flip-sensitive SLO-pass reported alongside via Fisher's exact test.

7 Related work

Sarathi-Serve chunks prefill smaller to bound prefill↔decode interference for mean latency; the prefill-rate law and giant acceleration show that at the concurrency cap, for the p99 tail, the opposite is correct—because decode is memory-bound, bigger chunks that evict giants sooner reduce residency. Marconi/Jenga and hierarchical-KV systems target hit rate; our design-space partition shows hit rate is decoupled from goodput@SLO when the tail is uncacheable turn-0 prefill. Shortest-remaining-processing-first and its variants are the textbook residency-reducing scheduler; we derive its role from the capacity law (as a K-lever) independently and position giant acceleration as an orthogonal, composable residency lever that operates within a request rather than by reordering across requests. Classic queueing predicts metastability from heavy tails; our contribution is to identify the specific memory-bound decode feedback that produces a ∼19× super-M/G/1 amplification in a modern serving engine, and to show it is the same state variable that governs every mechanism's sign.

8 Conclusion

The goodput@SLO tail of saturated conversational LLM serving is not a grab-bag of independent effects. It is one dynamical instability—a memory-bound concurrency↔decode runaway—with one state variable, residency. That single fact organizes the design space into a clean partition (every effective lever reduces residency; every backfire raises it), explains why the intuitive cures fail, quantifies the ceiling (C=K/(1−h), all violations contention), disciplines the measurements (coin-flip; use deterministic per-step metrics), and—the real test—predicted a novel positive mechanism (giant acceleration) that raises the frontier +67% and the capacity ceiling +36%. The practical takeaway for serving-system designers is compact: stop optimizing hit rate and prefill fairness for the tail; measure and minimize residency.
