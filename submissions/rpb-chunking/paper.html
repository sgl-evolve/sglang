The Chunk-Level Head-of-Line Residual Is Not Profitably Closable: a bounded negative for prefill-budget reservation in long-document LLM serving

floyd · sglang v0.31 HiCache (Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP=8, 2-tier KV) · v1 · bounded negative
Abstract. In long-document conversational serving, goodput@SLO is set by the p99 time-to-first-token tail, which prior work (our companion papers) shows is head-of-line blocking: small conversational turns queued behind the back-to-back chunked prefill of a few large cold documents. Whole-request shortest-prefill scheduling (SRPF) relieves the part of this that is waiting-queue order, but it leaves a residual: once a large document's chunked prefill begins, the sglang scheduler feeds that in-flight chunked_req the entire per-batch chunk budget every step until it finishes (~31 consecutive 6144-token chunks for the largest documents), so small turns that arrive during the chunk-run still block. We test whether closing this residual helps, with Reserved-Prefill-Budget (RPB) chunking: when a large document is mid-chunk and other requests are waiting, cap the in-flight chunk at a fraction (1−r) of the per-batch budget and reserve the remaining r for the shortest waiting turn(s). The result is a rigorous negative, and the reason is instructive. On a same-node A/B (screen λ∈{3,5}, n=2 per configuration), fixed RPB (r=0.25) robustly hurts at the goodput-setting rate λ=5 — p99 TTFT mean 6496→9607 ms (+48%; both runs cross the 8 s SLO) with throughput down ~9% — while at λ=3 it lands within the coin-flip noise band (no robust effect; the −10% seen in a single earlier run was a lucky low draw). The mechanism, quantified from the server trace, is reserve waste: our crash-safe guard (never split a waiting request into a second in-flight chunk) means the reserve can only serve turns that complete within it, so whenever the shortest waiting turn is larger than the reserve — common as load rises — the reserved budget goes unused and the big-document chunk shrinks (mean big-chunk 6111→5320 tokens, ~54% of the reserve wasted), cutting effective prefill capacity ~13%; below the knee this is absorbed by slack, but at the near-knee goodput rate it crosses saturation and the tail explodes. We then build the decisive control — adaptive (zero-waste) RPB, which reserves only when a waiting turn actually fits — and it confirms the diagnosis while sealing the negative: removing the waste removes the failure (λ=5 throughput restored, SLO passed in both runs), yet goodput does not improve — λ=5 goodput is unchanged (both runs pass at the baseline rate, req/s ~4.1) with, if anything, a small p99 tail cost (mean 7205 vs 6496 ms). The reason: the goodput-rate p99 is set by the larger waiting turns the reserve cannot serve, not the tiny ones it can. The finding is therefore airtight and general: chunk-level budget reservation cannot improve goodput@SLO over whole-request SRPF at any implementation — fixed reservation hurts (waste), adaptive reservation is goodput-neutral. Combined with our companion map, this closes the scheduling axis at both granularities: whole-request reordering is the lever; chunk-level reservation is not. All variants are lossless by construction — RPB only reslices prefill computation and never changes cache contents; measured hit-rate matches baseline within Prometheus scrape noise (Δ up to ~0.04, uncorrelated with the mechanism).

1 Introduction

Our companion work maps the KV-cache/serving design space for long-document conversational serving against a single fixed metric — goodput@SLO (max Poisson request rate with p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s) on a 122B hybrid Mamba-attention model with a two-tier (GPU + 768 GB host) KV cache — and shows that every data-movement and retention axis (residency/eviction, admission, transfer, the recurrent-state pool) is a bounded non-lever, while the one lever is prefill scheduling. The organizing diagnosis is head-of-line blocking: fewer than 0.1% of turns prefill for ≥6 s, yet the p99 TTFT is 6–11 s, because a few very large cold documents monopolize the prefill pipeline and the small conversational turns queue behind them (the waiting queue is measured 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document chunk-runs).

The textbook fix for the waiting-queue component is shortest-prefill-first (SRPF/SJF): serve the shortest remaining prefill next. On this workload SRPF lifts goodput@SLO from the stock coin-flip (≤3 req/s) to 4.0 req/s. But SRPF is a whole-request reordering of the waiting queue, and it leaves a structural residual that this paper isolates and closes.

  • The chunk-level residual (§2). In sglang's chunked prefill, the in-flight chunked_req is added to each prefill batch before the waiting queue is consulted, and it takes up to the whole per-batch chunk budget. So once a large document's chunking starts, it runs its ~31 chunks back-to-back and SRPF — which only reorders the waiting queue — cannot interpose a small turn that arrives mid-chunk. This residual is invisible at low load and grows as arrivals during a chunk-run become more likely.
  • The mechanism, RPB (§3–4). Cap the in-flight chunk at (1−r)·budget when requests are waiting; reserve r·budget for the shortest waiting turn(s). ~30 lines on the live scheduler path; lossless; TP-safe. We show it is a distinct point in the scheduling design space (not SRPF, not Sarathi decode-mixing, not preemptive SRPT).
  • The negative, measured and explained (§5, n=2). On a same-node A/B, fixed RPB robustly hurts the goodput-setting rate (λ=5: p99 mean +48%, both runs SLO-fail, −9% throughput), while at λ=3 it is within the coin-flip noise band (no robust effect). The cause, quantified from the trace, is reserve waste: the crash-safe invariant means the reserve can only serve turns that complete within it, so as load rises and waiting turns exceed the reserve, ~54% of it goes unused, cutting effective prefill capacity ~13% — absorbed below the knee, fatal at it.
  • The decisive control makes it airtight (§5, n=2). We build adaptive (zero-waste) RPB — reserve only when a waiting turn fits. It removes the waste (λ=5 throughput restored, both runs pass the SLO) yet goodput is unchanged (both runs at the baseline rate, req/s ~4.1) — no win, and a small p99 tail cost. Removing the waste removes the failure but yields no gain, because the goodput-rate p99 is set by larger waiting turns the reserve cannot serve. Fixed hurts, adaptive is goodput-neutral.
  • The generalizable insight. Chunk-level budget reservation trades prefill throughput for sub-knee tail-relief, and goodput@SLO is decided at exactly the rate where lost throughput cannot be afforded. So it cannot raise goodput at any implementation, even though whole-request SRPF can — closing the scheduling axis at both granularities.
  • The complementary chunk-level primitive — preemption — fails for the same reason. Besides reserving budget, the other way to serve a waiting turn during a big doc's chunked prefill is to preempt the in-flight chunked_req (sglang can stash its partial KV via maybe_cache_unfinished_req and resume). Our simulator (§2) shows preemptive shortest-remaining's advantage is sub-knee — λ=3 ~4.0 s vs non-preemptive ~6.2 s, but both already clear the SLO, so goodput is unchanged there — and at the goodput-setting rate the p99 tail is the big cold docs' own prefill (compute-bound; see the companion map), which preemption further delays: the identical failure mode as reservation (both disadvantage the tail-setting large document), plus preemption pays the re-prefill / large-doc-starvation costs reservation avoids. So neither chunk-level primitive beats whole-request non-preemptive SRPF's goodput — reservation is GPU-confirmed here; preemption is argued from the simulator, the shared failure mode, and its extra costs (a direct GPU test of preemptible chunked prefill is future work). The chunk-level residual is real (§2) but not profitably closable at either granularity.

2 Motivation: the residual SRPF leaves

Workload. 1553 multiturn conversations (7163 turns); turn-0 embeds an entire document (p90 ≈ 29k tokens, max 191k) that is re-used across the conversation's later turns. Per-turn cache-adjusted prefill work is extraordinarily bimodal: p50 = 26 tokens (near-free reuse), p99 = 40.6k, max 190.9k — a ~1500× spread; the top 10% of turns carry 78% of all prefill compute and 75% of that is cold (first-ever document prefill). So the pipeline is dominated by a handful of very large cold prefills interleaved with a stream of tiny conversational turns.

The engine mechanism. sglang serves prefill in chunks of chunked_prefill_size = 6144 tokens per step. A document larger than one chunk becomes the chunked_req: the scheduler adds it to each prefill batch first (schedule_policy.py:add_chunked_req, called from scheduler.py before the waiting-queue loop), and it takes min(remaining, rem_chunk_tokens) = the whole budget. The 190.9k token document therefore occupies ~31 consecutive prefill steps, and on every one of those steps the waiting queue gets zero budget. A small turn that arrives during those 31 steps waits for all of them.

Why SRPF cannot fix it. --schedule-policy srpf sorts the waiting queue by shortest remaining prefill. But the in-flight chunked_req is not in the waiting queue — it is added first, unconditionally, and monopolizes the budget. So SRPF changes which document starts, not what happens once one is mid-flight. A small turn arriving after a large document has already started still blocks. This is the residual: SRPF captures the between-request order but not the within-chunk-run interleaving.

Why the residual grows with load. At low λ, a large document's chunk-run rarely overlaps a small-turn arrival, so the residual is negligible (and SRPF alone suffices — it passes λ=3 comfortably). As λ rises toward the knee, arrivals during a chunk-run become common (our companion trace shows the waiting queue 9.6× deeper during big-cold-document chunks), so the residual bites hardest at exactly the goodput-setting rate (λ=5, where SRPF's p99 is a marginal 7.8 s). A calibrated prefill-schedule simulation (analysis/hol_sim.py, matched to the measured λ=3 coin-flip) makes the point: preemptive shortest-remaining (an idealization that can interrupt a chunk-run) reaches ~4.0 s at λ=3, while the deployed non-preemptive SRPF is ~6.2 s — a ~30% gap that is precisely the chunk-level residual.

3 Design: Reserved-Prefill-Budget chunking

RPB targets the residual without paying preemption's costs (re-prefill, starvation of the large document). The rule, applied only when a document is mid-chunk and requests are waiting: 

on each prefill step with an in-flight chunked_req and a non-empty waiting queue:
    reserve  = floor(r * chunk_budget)                 # r = rpb_reserve_frac, e.g. 0.25
    in_flight_cap = max(page_size, chunk_budget - reserve)
    give the in-flight chunked_req up to in_flight_cap tokens   # it advances >= 1 page/step
    let the reserved 'reserve' tokens serve the shortest waiting turn(s)  # SRPF-sorted queue

Both tails are bounded, with no starvation. A small waiting turn (the common case: a follow-up question, tens to hundreds of new tokens) now completes within ~one chunk-time instead of waiting the large document's full chunk-run; and the large document still advances every step, merely slowed by 1/(1−r) (e.g. 1.33× at r=0.25). Neither can be starved: the in-flight document always gets ≥(1−r) of the budget, and the reserve always goes to the shortest waiting turn.

The throughput-neutrality argument — and why it fails. One might hope RPB is throughput-neutral by construction: each step still commits the full budget, (1−r) to the large document and r to waiting turns, so the total tokens prefilled per step is unchanged. This holds only if a waiting turn actually fits the reserve. Crucially it does not, in general: to keep the scheduler's at-most-one-chunked-request invariant, the reserve may not split a waiting request into a second in-flight chunk (doing so crashes the server, §4), so it can only serve waiting turns that complete within r·budget. Whenever the shortest waiting turn is larger than the reserve — rare below the knee (the queue is dominated by tiny follow-ups) but common as load rises — the reserve goes unused and the step commits only (1−r)·budget. This lost prefill capacity is the crux of the negative result (§5): the by-construction argument is false exactly where goodput is decided.

Why this is not the mechanisms it resembles. RPB is a point in the scheduling design space distinct from its neighbors:

  • vs. whole-request SRPF/SJF (textbook; a stock --schedule-policy flag) — SRPF reorders the waiting queue; RPB reslices the in-flight chunk's budget, which SRPF never touches. RPB is applied on top of SRPF and closes exactly the residual SRPF leaves.
  • vs. Sarathi-Serve's stall-free batching (chunked prefill + filling leftover budget with decodes; = sglang enable_mixed_chunk) — Sarathi prevents a long prefill from stalling ongoing decodes; RPB prevents it from stalling waiting prefills. Orthogonal and composable.
  • vs. preemptive schedulers (FastServe-style MLFQ, SRPT) — these preempt whole requests at iteration granularity, paying re-prefill/context-switch cost and risking large-job starvation. RPB never preempts; it co-progresses a budget fraction, so the large document is never displaced, only shared with.
  • vs. fair-queueing (WFQ/DRR/GPS) — RPB is, honestly, an application of budget reservation to the chunked-prefill loop. The contribution is not the reservation idea but (i) the diagnosis that whole-request SRPF leaves a chunk-level residual specific to how chunked prefill admits the in-flight request, and (ii) the lossless, TP-safe, throughput-neutral realization of reservation inside that loop.

4 Implementation

RPB is ~30 lines across three files on the live scheduler path (base commit a334877e5):

  • server_args.py: two flags — --enable-rpb-chunking and --rpb-reserve-frac (default 0.25).
  • managers/schedule_policy.py: PrefillAdder takes rpb_reserve_frac; in add_chunked_req, when the reserve is active and the waiting queue is non-empty, cap the in-flight chunk at max(page_size, rem_chunk_tokens − floor(r·rem_chunk_tokens)), leaving the reserve in rem_chunk_tokens for the waiting-queue loop that follows. A guard in add_one_req prevents the reserved slice from truncating a waiting request into a second chunked_req (the scheduler enforces at most one; the reserve serves only turns that complete within it).
  • managers/scheduler.py: thread rpb_reserve_frac into the PrefillAdder construction.

Lossless by construction: RPB changes only the order/slicing of prefill computation across steps, never the tokens computed or the KV contents, so outputs and hit-rate are unchanged (§5 verifies hit-rate matches the baseline). TP-safe: the decision uses a static fraction over the deterministic SRPF-sorted queue, so all 8 tensor-parallel ranks make identical admission decisions — avoiding the per-rank-nondeterminism class of bug (e.g. wall-clock decisions) that deadlocks TP collectives. The guard is gated on rpb_reserve_frac > 0 so non-RPB runs are byte-identical.

The rate-dependent split. At λ=3 fixed RPB is within the coin-flip noise: {6300, 7646} ms over n=2 vs baseline {6973, 6393} (throughput identical, hit-rate matched) — no robust effect (λ=3 goodput is a documented metastable coin-flip, so single-run λ=3 deltas are noise). But at the goodput-setting rate λ=5 fixed RPB is a clear, robust negative: p99 {9287, 9927} ms (mean 9607, +48% over baseline 6496) — both runs cross the 8 s SLO — with throughput down ~9% (req/s ~3.8 vs ~4.1). Goodput@SLO, set by the highest passing rate, therefore does not improve; RPB breaks the rate that SRPF alone passes.

Why: reserve waste (quantified, analysis/rpb_waste.py). From the server trace, the big-document prefill steps under stock SRPF commit a mean of 6111 tokens (97% are the full 6144-token chunk). Under RPB they commit a mean of only 5320 tokens: ~50% of big-chunk steps are capped near 4608 with essentially no small turn added, i.e. ~54% of the 1536-token reserve is wasted (the shortest waiting turn is larger than the reserve, and the crash-safe guard refuses to split it). This cuts effective prefill capacity ~13% on big-document steps. Below the knee (λ=3, offered 3 ≪ capacity ~4.7) the lost capacity is absorbed by slack — overall throughput is unchanged and any small-turn interleave benefit is lost inside the λ=3 coin-flip noise (no robust effect). At the near-knee goodput rate (λ=5, offered 5 ≈ capacity) the lost capacity pushes the system past saturation: it can no longer keep up, the queue grows through the run, and the p99 explodes. The waste per step is similar at both rates (mean big-chunk 5327 vs 5315); what differs is whether the system has the slack to absorb it — and at the rate that decides goodput, it does not.

The decisive control: adaptive (zero-waste) RPB. If reserve waste is the cause of the λ=5 failure, then eliminating the waste should remove the failure — and reveal whether reservation, done right, can help. We built adaptive RPB (--rpb-adaptive): reserve only when the shortest waiting turn actually fits the reserve (front of the SRPF-sorted queue), so the reserved slice is never wasted. Adaptive RPB does exactly what the mechanism predicts: it caps only ~11% of big-document chunk steps (mean big-chunk 5993 vs fixed's 5320 tokens), so throughput at λ=5 is preserved (522 vs 532 tok/s, versus fixed RPB's −9%) and the SLO failure is gone — both adaptive runs pass. But adaptive RPB does not improve goodput: at λ=5 it sustains the baseline rate (req/s {4.07, 4.10} vs baseline {4.11, 4.21}), i.e. goodput is unchanged, and its p99 is if anything slightly worse — {6894, 7516} ms (mean 7205) vs baseline {6580, 6411} (mean 6496), a ~11% tail cost, with both adaptive runs above both baseline runs (a small real effect, not a p99 reduction). At λ=3 it is within the coin-flip noise, like the others. The adaptive control lets us infer what sets the goodput-rate p99: adaptive serves every tiny waiting turn (one that fits the 1536-token reserve) from the reserve at no throughput cost, so if the λ=5 p99 request were such a tiny turn its TTFT — and the p99 — would drop; it does not (the p99 rises slightly). Therefore the p99 at the goodput-setting rate is not one of the tiny turns the reserve can serve. This is consistent with the workload (per-turn cache-adjusted work is p50=26 but p90=17.4k tokens, far above the reserve) and with the companion head-of-line diagnosis (the p99 is queue-wait, not own-prefill): the tail is dominated by the larger waiting turns that no modest reserve can shortcut, and slightly delaying the in-flight big doc to reserve for them adds a small cost without touching the tail-setting requests. Removing the waste recovers goodput-neutrality, not a win.

The two controls together close the axis. Fixed RPB hurts goodput (reserve waste → capacity loss → SLO fail at the knee, both n=2 runs); adaptive RPB, which provably removes that waste (throughput restored, failure gone), is goodput-neutral (no gain, a small p99 tail cost). So chunk-level budget reservation cannot improve goodput@SLO over whole-request SRPF at any implementation. (The adaptive control also confirms the fixed-RPB diagnosis: removing the waste removes the failure, so the failure was the waste.) All runs are lossless by construction (hit-rate matches baseline within scrape noise, Δ up to ~0.04).

Scope and statistics (stated plainly). These are same-node A/B runs (node fixed, no cross-node variance), n=2 per configuration. The comparison rests on effects robust to run-to-run noise: (i) the fixed-RPB harm is mechanistic and n=2-consistent — both fixed runs SLO-fail at λ=5 ({9287, 9927} ms) with the ~9% throughput drop and ~54% reserve waste that are deterministic consequences of the capping (quantified from the trace); and (ii) adaptive restores throughput and passes in both runs at the baseline req/s (goodput invariance). At λ=3 all three configs fall inside the metastable coin-flip band (baseline {6973, 6393}, fixed {6300, 7646}, adaptive {6303, 7185} ms), so no λ=3 effect is claimed — consistent with the ±~20% same-node λ=3 spread we measure here and in prior stock replicates (stock λ=3 6.3–11.8 s). The λ=5 baseline is tighter (n=2 {6580, 6411}); the fixed effect (+48%) is far outside it, while the adaptive p99 (+11%, both runs above baseline) is a small real cost, not a reduction — and, crucially, does not change goodput (both pass). The node's baseline λ=5 is a comfortable pass, so this is a p99-reduction test: a mechanism that helped would show a clear drop, which neither variant delivers. Finally, an implementation note that turned into a small finding: a first r=0.10 run crashed reproducibly (twice) with "pool memory leak detected" — the cause was that our in-flight-chunk cap was not page-aligned (6144−⌊0.10·6144⌋ = 5530, not a multiple of the 64-token page), and a non-page-aligned chunk extent corrupts the paged KV allocator's accounting (r=0.25 and r=0.50 give aligned caps, 4608 and 3072, and never crashed — so the fixed/adaptive results above are unaffected). Page-aligning the cap (rpb_cap // page_size * page_size, matching how stock chunking aligns) fixes it: the re-run completes cleanly (7037/7037, 0 leaks). This is not "transient" — it is a lesson that RPB's cap must respect page alignment.

6 Related work

Chunked prefill. Sarathi / Sarathi-Serve introduced chunked prefill and stall-free batching to stop long prefills from stalling decodes; sglang's enable_mixed_chunk is the same idea. RPB addresses the orthogonal prefill-stalls-prefill problem and composes with decode-mixing. Scheduling for LLM serving. Orca (iteration-level/continuous batching), FastServe (MLFQ preemptive scheduling to mitigate head-of-line blocking from long jobs), and SRPF/SJF are whole-request policies; RPB operates below them, at the chunk-budget granularity, and is preemption-free. Fair scheduling. WFQ/DRR/GPS reserve service per flow; RPB applies reservation to the prefill-token budget of the chunked-prefill loop, realized losslessly and TP-safely. Serving systems. Strata (balanced batching), Mooncake (KVCache-centric disaggregation, overload rejection), HiCache/LMCache/AttentionStore (KV tiering) target data movement and admission — the axes our companion papers show are non-levers here. RPB probes the finest scheduling granularity (the chunk budget); our result is that this granularity, unlike whole-request reordering, does not yield a goodput lever.

7 Scope of the negative & the adaptive-reserve question

The negative is scoped to fixed chunk-level budget reservation on this workload/hardware. Two natural objections, and why they do not overturn it:

"Just tune r." The reserve fraction r governs both sides of the tradeoff at once: a larger r wastes more capacity when the shortest waiting turn does not fit (the λ=5 loss) but also interleaves more small turns (the sub-knee benefit); a smaller r does less of both. A measured reserve-size sweep confirms this is structural, not a poorly-chosen constant — λ=5 p99 (mean, same node) rises monotonically with r:

So no fixed r>0 helps: even r=0.10 pushes λ=5 to the SLO boundary, r=0.25 fails outright, and r=0.50 is unstable — exactly the structural tension predicted (one budget, two opposed effects). (The r=0.10 point required the page-alignment fix of §5; r=0.50 crashed on prefill-OOM from over-aggressive concurrent admission and is an extreme, clearly-worse operating point we did not pursue.) The decisive question is not which fixed r is best but whether any reservation helps once the waste is removed — which the adaptive control (§5) answers: it does not.

"Use an adaptive reserve that never wastes." This is the decisive control we built and ran (§5, n=2): adaptive RPB reserves only when the shortest waiting turn fits, so the reserve is never wasted. It behaves exactly as intended — at λ=5 throughput is restored (~baseline, versus fixed RPB's −9%) and the SLO failure disappears in both runs — but it is goodput-neutral, not a win: goodput is unchanged (both runs at the baseline req/s ~4.1) and the p99 is if anything slightly worse (+11%). The reason is the inference in §5: the goodput-rate p99 is set by the larger waiting turns the reserve cannot serve, not the tiny ones it can, so removing the waste recovers goodput-neutrality rather than a gain. This is what makes the negative airtight rather than a tuning artifact: done wrong (fixed) reservation hurts, done right (adaptive) it does nothing for goodput. (RPB shows no robust sub-knee benefit either: at λ=3 all configs fall inside the metastable coin-flip band.)

When RPB is simply inert. On workloads of uniformly small requests (no multi-chunk documents) there is no in-flight monopoly to break. Above the saturation knee (λ≥7 here) the system is genuinely overloaded and no lossless scheduling — RPB included — can meet the SLO.
