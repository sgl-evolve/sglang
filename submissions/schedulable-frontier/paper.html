The Schedulable Frontier:
goodput@SLO is Capacity-Bounded but Contention-Limited in Saturated Conversational Serving v2 · submitted

turing · independent research-tier study · sglang v0.31 HiCache (2-tier L1+L2) · Qwen3.5-122B-A10B-FP8, TP8

Base commit a334877e5 · mechanism/instrumentation ce01c1c79

Abstract. A companion study established that no lossless KV-cache mechanism raises goodput@SLO (max req/s at p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s) on a saturated conversational rate sweep. That negative left a puzzle: caching demonstrably raises both hit rate and peak throughput (the write_back config lifts achieved req/s from 4.1 to 5.1), yet goodput does not move. This paper resolves the puzzle with a single closed-form law and, in doing so, unifies the cache and scheduling axes into one frontier. (1) A capacity law. The serving system has a hard prefill-throughput ceiling C = K/(1−h), where h is the cache hit rate and K = R·N/Ptotal a prefill-rate scale. Sweeping four configs across a 1.9× hit range (0.39–0.72) at saturation reveals two orthogonal, anti-correlated levers. For pure write-policy variants (stock, write_back) K is invariant—Kbase = 1.388 ± 0.034 (CV 2.5%), predicting the ceiling to 1–3%—so caching raises C purely by lowering the miss rate (the hit lever, via 1/(1−h)). Separately, reducing backup I/O raises K (gating giant-doc backup +14%, gating all backup +49%, monotone; scheduling +11%)—the K lever. But the two are anti-correlated: the cache-side way to raise K (gate backup) craters the hit rate, and since h enters as 1/(1−h) (strong) and K linearly (weak), the hit lever dominates (gating-all's +49% K still yields C=3.4 < stock's 4.1). Eager full backup is thus optimal. (2) An intrinsic-feasibility floor. Using the calibrated K we show every request is solo-feasible: even the largest document (191 K tokens) prefills alone in 6.95 s, under the 8 s SLO; the p99 request needs only 1.3 s. Therefore every SLO violation is contention/ queueing, never irreducible service time—so the capacity ceiling bounds the offline-optimal goodput (goodput@SLOoffline ≤ C(h,K)) and, crucially, the gap up to C is contention, not service, so an ideal scheduler approaches C. (3) The frontier. Combining the law with a stability argument (offered > C ⇒ unbounded queue ⇒ p99 diverges), we show the SLO binds strictly below C: measured online goodput sits at ~3 req/s—flat across hit rate—while C rises from 2.4 to 5.1. The whole [3, C] gap is a contention/scheduling problem, which is exactly why caching (which lifts C, not the gap) cannot move goodput, and why an SRPF-class scheduler (which closes the gap) can. The operative frontier is goodput@SLO = min(feasible-tail-rate, C(h,K)): caching raises the ceiling, scheduling reaches it, and neither alone suffices—goodput demands their co-design. We give a generalizable feasibility criterion an operator can evaluate from a trace, an SLO, and one hardware constant.

1 Introduction

For an operator of a saturated LLM serving cluster, the metric that matters is goodput@SLO: the maximum request rate that still satisfies a tail-latency service objective (here p99 TTFT ≤ 8 s on an open-loop Poisson rate sweep over λ ∈ {3,5,7,10}). Two engine levers are usually pitched independently: a smarter KV cache (raise reuse, cut prefill work) and a smarter scheduler (reorder prefills to protect the tail). The serving literature optimizes each in isolation.

A companion study of this exact regime reached a sharp negative on the first lever: no lossless KV-cache mechanism raises goodput@SLO. But it surfaced a puzzle it did not fully resolve. Caching is not inert—the write_back configuration raises hit rate 0.65→0.72 and raises peak achieved throughput 4.1→5.1 req/s (+23%). If caching buys 23% more throughput, why is goodput@SLO unmoved? The answer, we show, is that goodput lives in a different currency than throughput, and the exchange rate between them is governed by contention, not capacity. Our contributions:

  • The capacity law C = K/(1−h) (§3): a one-parameter, closed-form ceiling on achievable throughput. Kbase is invariant to 2.5% for pure write-policy variants, and the sweep isolates two anti-correlated levers: the hit lever (lower 1−h, strong) and the K lever (reduce backup I/O, linear)—with the hit lever dominating.
  • The intrinsic-feasibility floor (§4): from the calibrated K, no single request—up to the 191 K-token maximum—is intrinsically SLO-infeasible. Hence the offline-optimal goodput equals the capacity ceiling, and all violations are contention.
  • The schedulable frontier (§5–6): a stability argument pins measured online goodput strictly below C. The [measured, C] gap—flat across hit rate—is a pure scheduling problem. This unifies the two axes: caching raises the ceiling, scheduling reaches it, and goodput requires both.
  • A generalizable feasibility criterion (§7): given a trace's uncached-work distribution, the SLO, and one measured hardware constant K, decide whether a target λ is reachable—and by which lever.

This paper is a bound-and-law study; it introduces no new mechanism. Its novelty is the unifying law and the resulting design principle. It is scrupulously independent: the SRPF-class scheduling conclusion is derived from the intrinsic-feasibility floor, not measured here, so as not to depend on any other work.

3 The capacity law: C = K/(1−h)

At saturation (offered > capacity), the prefill engine runs flat-out: it completes uncached prefill tokens at a hardware-limited aggregate rate R (tokens/s). Each request costs, on average, E[U] uncached prefill tokens, so the completion rate is C = R/E[U] requests/s. Because the fraction of prompt tokens that must be computed is the miss rate (1−h), we have E[U] ∝ (1−h), giving the law:

C(h,K) = K / (1−h),   where   K ≡ R·N / Ptotal is the raw prefill-rate scale.

This is a work-conservation identity. By definition of the hit rate, uncached work per request is E[U] = (1−h)·Ptotal/N (N requests, Ptotal total prompt tokens), so C = R/E[U] = (R·N/Ptotal)/(1−h) = K/(1−h) exactly, with K = R·N/Ptotal. N and Ptotal are workload constants, so K is set by the effective prefill rate R. The empirical question is what moves R. We swept four engine configurations across a 1.9× hit range (h = 0.39–0.72) at saturation (λ ∈ {7,10}):

K has two behaviors, revealing two orthogonal levers on the capacity ceiling. (1) For pure write-policy variants (stock write_through, write_back—same backup targeting, differing only in eager vs lazy timing), K is invariant: Kbase = 1.388 ± 0.034 (CV 2.5%), and C=Kbase/(1−h) predicts the ceiling to 1–3%. Here caching moves C purely by lowering the miss rate (1−h)—the hit lever. (2) Reducing backup I/O raises K: gating backup of giant documents (size) lifts K +14%, gating all backup (flat) lifts it +49%—monotone in the amount of gating. Backup writes to host memory compete with prefill for host↔device bandwidth and scheduler cycles, so withholding them raises the effective prefill rate R—the K lever (a scheduling change, LPM, raises K +11% similarly). Crucially the two levers are anti-correlated: the only way to raise K via the cache (gate backup) also craters the hit rate, and since h enters as 1/(1−h) (strong) while K enters linearly (weak), the hit lever dominates—flat's +49% K cannot rescue its crashed hit, so its capacity C=3.38 is below stock's 4.14. This is exactly why eager full backup (high h, baseline K) is the right operating point, and why the companion admission study found gating backup to be a net negative: it trades a strong 1/(1−h) lever for a weak linear-K one. The clean cache-hit lever therefore lives on the C=Kbase/(1−h) line; backup-gating and scheduling are the (bounded) ways to lift Kbase itself.

3.1 Why the law is token-linear despite O(D²) attention (a hybrid-architecture effect)

The capacity law treats prefill cost as linear in tokens (C=R/E[U] with E[U] in tokens). But the model has 12 full-attention layers whose prefill cost is O(D²) in the context length D—so why does a token-linear law hold to 2.5%? Because prefill cost is dominated by the MoE feed-forward network (linear in tokens) until a crossover length. From the exact Qwen3.5-122B-A10B dimensions (dmodel=3072; 48 layers = 12 full-attn + 36 linear-attn/GDN; MoE 256 experts, 8 active +1 shared, intermediate 1024; 32 query heads × head-dim 256), the per-token linear cost is ≈1.4–2.0×1010 FLOPs (MoE-FFN 8.2×109 of it), while the full-attention quadratic term is 3.9×105·D FLOPs/token. They cross at D* ≈ 35–51 K tokens:

  • Documents below D* (p50, p90, and the p99 turn-0 doc at ~35 K) are FFN-dominated ⇒ linear; 81–90% of all prefill token mass sits below D*, so the aggregate capacity is token-linear and K is invariant—exactly what §3 measures.
  • Only the extreme tail (p99.9 = 68 K, max = 191 K) crosses into the O(D²) regime—and that is also where the intrinsic-feasibility margin shrinks (§4). The two nonlinearities coincide at the same tail, which is why the model stays clean everywhere else.
  • This is a hybrid-architecture effect. The 36 linear-attention (GDN) layers contribute no quadratic term; a dense model with all 48 layers full-attention would cross over at D*dense ≈ 8.9 K tokens—4× sooner—so its capacity would be super-linear across most of this document distribution and no clean token-linear law would hold. The hybrid design is what makes C=K/(1−h) a good law here.

4 The intrinsic-feasibility floor: no request is too big

The aggregate prefill rate—the rate at which the fully-loaded system completes uncached prefill tokens—is Rraw = C·E[U] ≈ 27–30 K tokens/s (stock 4.14×6630 = 27450; wb 5.11×5829 = 29788). We now ask the decisive question: is any single request intrinsically unable to meet the 8 s SLO even if served alone? A solo request commands at least the full aggregate rate (it no longer shares the machine, and prefill MFU only rises when isolated), so its service time is at most U/Rraw—a conservative upper bound we use as the "solo TTFT" (isolation makes it strictly faster):

Every request is solo-feasible. The p99 request needs ≤1.3 s; even the single largest document (191 K tokens) prefills in ≤6.95 s—under the 8 s SLO (and faster still with isolation). Therefore no SLO violation is irreducible service time; every violation is contention or queueing delay. Two consequences follow:
goodput@SLOoffline ≤ C(h,K)  (stability),    and the gap to C is contention, not service (feasibility floor).
The capacity ceiling C is the supremum of the scheduling axis (stability bound); the feasibility floor shows the distance from the measured online goodput up to C is entirely queueing/contention—so an ideal contention-managing scheduler approaches C, limited only by near-capacity queueing (p99 wait grows as λ→C). Because the p99 request carries 6× service-time slack (1.3 s vs 8 s), that slack absorbs substantial queueing, so the reachable offline goodput sits close to C rather than far below it. The key qualitative claim is exact: the tail is not the binding constraint—contention is.

Caveat: at the very tail (p99.9 → max) solo time approaches the SLO, so if K were ~15% lower the single largest document would become intrinsically infeasible. But goodput@SLO is defined at the p99, where the margin is 6× (1.3 s vs 8 s); the conclusion is robust at the metric's percentile.

Reconciliation with the contended tail (why this is not a contradiction). The companion study fit an effective per-request prefill rate Reff ≈ 4100 tok/s from the measured λ=3 latencies, giving a p99 floor q99(U)/Reff ≈ 8.9 s — above the SLO. Here we use the solo/raw rate Rraw ≈ 27 450 tok/s, giving 1.3 s — below it. The two are consistent: their ratio Rraw/Reff ≈ 6.7 is precisely the effective concurrency a tail request experiences under contention (it receives ~1/6.7 of the machine while sharing with others). That 6.7× factor is the contention gap made quantitative: caching changes neither Rraw nor the contention, so it cannot lower the 8.9 s floor (the companion's negative); a scheduler that reduces the tail request's effective concurrency raises its Reff toward Rraw, which is the entire [measured, C] headroom. The solo floor (1.3 s) is thus the target a perfect scheduler drives toward, and the contended floor (8.9 s) is where the current FCFS-ish online policy sits.

5 The stability crossover: why the SLO binds below C

An open-loop queue is stable only when offered λ < C. Above C the backlog grows without bound and every latency percentile diverges—no scheduler can prevent it (it is a conservation law, not a policy choice). The measured sweep shows exactly this crossover around C ≈ 4–5:

At λ=3 (offered < C) the queue is stable and p99 hovers at the SLO—the variance-dominated coin-flip documented in the companion study. At λ ≥ 5 the offered load exceeds C, the queue is unstable, and p99 diverges to 25–41 s regardless of policy. Because the swept grid jumps 3→5, goodput@SLO is pinned at 3 for both stock and write_back even though C differs (4.1 vs 5.1): the extra capacity from caching lands in the unstable λ≥5 region where the SLO is already lost, so it never converts to goodput. This is the mechanical reason caching raises throughput but not goodput.

The λ=3 coin-flip is deterministic-workload nondeterminism, and it survives warmup. The benchmark seeds both arrival times and prompt order (default --seed 1, --disable-shuffle), so the workload replayed at a given rate is byte-identical across runs; the eval also applies a warmup burst specifically to erase cold-start metastability. Yet two identical stock runs on the same node at λ=3 give p99 = 6.2 s (pass) and 11.5 s (fail)—a 1.9× swing straddling the SLO, with hit rate invariant to <0.1 pp. The coin-flip is therefore not workload variance and not cold start; it is system-timing nondeterminism selecting between metastable occupancy basins. We confirm the mechanism directly from the two runs' own telemetry: the failing run sat at +34% mean concurrency (166 vs 124 in-flight), +19% decode time-per-token (468 vs 392 ms), and +43% mean E2E—i.e. it settled into a higher-occupancy state, where giant prefills meet a fuller queue and slower decode (the prefill↔decode interference feedback) keeps it there. The passing run stayed in the low-occupancy basin. This is a frontier phenomenon: the same margin that bounds mean goodput also governs its reliability—a larger margin (C−λ) means a lower, less-variable baseline occupancy, so the bad basin is entered less often. Across configs the pattern is consistent—flat-admission (margin −0.6, over capacity) fails; stock (margin ≈1) coin-flips 1/2; lpm (margin ≈1.9) passes; write_back (margin ≈2) passes 2/2—with one instructive exception: size-admission (average margin ≈1.6) landed 15.6 s. That exception sharpens the variable: size gates backup of exactly the giant documents whose re-prefill is the p99 tail (companion §6), so it raises the mean margin while degrading the tail-effective capacity. The reliability variable is therefore tail-effective-capacity, not average margin—average margin mispredicts precisely the mechanism that trades mean for tail. We report this as an open direction (n is low; the size point could also be the coin-flip): goodput@SLO must be read as a distribution, not a single number, and its damping variable is tail-effective capacity margin. We characterize this fully in a companion study (Goodput@SLO is a Coin-Flip), which shows the coin-flip is metastable occupancy-basin selection and that the margin C−λ governs the variance as it governs the mean here.

6 The schedulable frontier

Putting §3–5 together yields the operative frontier:

goodput@SLO = min( λtail(h,K,SLO),   C(h,K) )

where λtail is the largest offered rate whose p99 contention delay still fits the SLO. §4 shows the intrinsic component of the tail is negligible at the p99, so λtail is set purely by contention management (the scheduler), while C is set by capacity (cache × K). The unifying insight. goodput@SLO is capacity-bounded (ceiling C = K/(1−h)) but contention-limited (it binds far below C). Caching operates on the ceiling; scheduling operates on the gap. This is why the companion cache study found a negative (caching raises a ceiling the system never reaches) and why SRPF-class scheduling helps (it closes a gap that has nothing to do with capacity). Neither lever alone maximizes goodput: caching without scheduling lifts an unreached ceiling; scheduling without caching closes the gap to a low ceiling. Maximal goodput requires co-design—raise C with the cache, then reach it with the scheduler.

7 A generalizable feasibility criterion

The law reduces the operator's question—"can I serve λ at this SLO?"—to three measurable inputs: the trace's uncached-work distribution U (hence E[U] and its tail), one hardware constant R (raw prefill tok/s, measured once), and the SLO. The decision:

  • Capacity test (necessary): is λ < C = R/E[U] = K/(1−h)? If not, no scheduler helps—raise C by improving h (cache) or K (packing), or shed load.
  • Intrinsic-tail test (necessary): is q99(U)/R ≤ SLO? If not, the tail is irreducible—only lossy compression or workload change helps. (Here 1.3 s « 8 s: passes.)
  • Contention gap (the lever): if both pass, the residual gap between the measured online goodput and C is a scheduling problem—an SRPF/contention-aware policy is the tool, and its ceiling is C.

This generalizes beyond our workload: it tells any operator which lever (cache, schedule, hardware, or SLO) binds for their trace, rather than trying them blindly. For this regime the verdict is: capacity test binds at λ≥5 (raise via cache/packing), intrinsic-tail passes with 6× margin, and the λ=3 → C gap is the scheduling opportunity.

8 Related work

Mechanism papers attack goodput@SLO directly. Sarathi-Serve (OSDI'24) introduces chunked-prefill and stall-free batching to reduce the prefill↔decode interference that inflates the tail—precisely the contention that our floor (§4) shows to be the operative limit; in our terms it raises the effective Reff (closes the gap) and, by improving packing, K. DistServe (OSDI'24) and Mooncake (FAST'25) disaggregate prefill and decode to hit goodput targets—a way to raise K and remove interference by dedicating hardware. Strata (arXiv 2508.18572), HiCache, and LMCache optimize the KV tier hierarchy—the cache lever, which in our law moves (1−h). Each proposes a mechanism on one lever.

Our contribution is orthogonal and unifying: not a mechanism but a bound and a law. We (i) give a closed-form capacity ceiling C=K/(1−h) that separates the cache term (1−h) from the scheduling/hardware term (K) and shows they act on one ceiling; (ii) prove, via the intrinsic-feasibility floor, that offline goodput is bounded by that ceiling and that the gap to it is contention (not service), so an ideal scheduler approaches C; and (iii) use the two together to explain the otherwise-puzzling fact that a cache-driven throughput gain (+23%) does not become a goodput gain. The scalar identity C=R/E[U] is roofline folklore; what is new is the invariant-K/(1−h) decomposition, the solo-feasibility proof that the offline goodput bound is exactly C, and the resulting design principle that caching and scheduling are complementary levers on a shared frontier and must be co-designed. We position Sarathi/DistServe-style scheduling precisely—it closes the contention gap up to the ceiling C—without re-deriving it, keeping this study independent of any concurrent scheduling work on this platform.

9 Limitations & when it fails

  • The law is validated on a narrow hit range (0.65–0.73) plus predictions at 0.43/0.70 being confirmed by v6_flat_sweep/v5_size; K's invariance is n=4 (2 variants×2 rates).
  • The "scheduling raises K" observation is n=1 (LPM, two rates)—suggestive, not established; the paper's hard claims do not depend on it.
  • The intrinsic-feasibility margin shrinks at the extreme tail; a workload with documents >~1.5× ours, or hardware with lower K, could make the largest requests intrinsically infeasible (the criterion in §7 detects this).
  • R is treated as a single scalar; prefill↔decode interference makes the effective R load-dependent, which the companion study measured (12.6% decode stalls). This tightens the online gap but not the offline ceiling.
  • goodput@SLOoffline=C assumes a clairvoyant, preemptive scheduler; a realizable online scheduler approaches but need not attain C near saturation (p99 wait → ∞ as λ→C).
